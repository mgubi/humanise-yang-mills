3The Wilson lattice and the quadratic transformation

3.1Finite lattice gauge theory

The starting point is a probability measure on finitely many group-valued links. Compactness makes this measure elementary to define; the difficulty of the continuum problem is to control its observables uniformly as the number of links grows. We first fix the action and its normalization.

3.1.1The periodic lattice and its probability law

Fix a closed subgroup \(G \subset U (r_G)\) in a specified faithful unitary representation, with nonabelian simple real Lie algebra as in Section 1. The group is compact. For a positive integer \(\ell\), let

\(\displaystyle \Lambda_{\ell} = (\mathbb{Z}/ \ell \mathbb{Z})^4, \qquad E_{\ell} = \Lambda_{\ell} \times \{0, 1, 2, 3\} .\)

A link configuration is a function \(U : E_{\ell} \to G\). The four coordinates are Euclidean; this finite model does not distinguish a time direction in its action. An oriented link in the negative direction is represented by the inverse of the corresponding positively oriented link.

For \(x \in \Lambda_{\ell}\) and distinct axes \(i, j\), the positively oriented plaquette holonomy based at \(x\) is

\(\displaystyle U_{ij} (x) = U_i (x) U_j (x + e_i) U_i (x + e_j)^{- 1} U_j (x)^{- 1} .\)

Count each pair \(i < j\) once. The action is

\(\displaystyle S_{\ell} (U) = \sum_{x \in \Lambda_{\ell}} \sum_{0 \leqslant i < j < 4} \left( 1 - \frac{\text{Retr} \hspace{0.17em} U_{ij} (x)}{r_G} \right) . \)

Each summand is real and nonnegative: a unitary matrix has each diagonal entry of modulus at most one, so \(\text{Retr} \hspace{0.17em} V \leqslant r_G\). The convention has no extra factor of \(1 / 2\) over faces. This action is dimensionless. The bare coefficient \(\beta\) multiplies it in the Boltzmann weight; it is not the blocking-kernel penalty used later in the RG construction.

Let \(H_G\) be probability Haar measure on \(G\), and let

\(\displaystyle \rho_{\ell} = \bigotimes_{(x, i) \in E_{\ell}} H_G, \quad Z_{\ell} (\beta) = \int e^{- \beta S_{\ell} (U)} \hspace{0.17em} d \rho_{\ell} (U), \quad d \mu_{\ell, \beta} (U) = Z_{\ell} (\beta)^{- 1} e^{- \beta S_{\ell} (U)} d \rho_{\ell} (U) .\)

The partition function is finite and strictly positive. Indeed the link space is a finite product of compact groups, hence compact, and \(S_{\ell}\) is continuous; therefore \(e^{- \beta S_{\ell}}\) is bounded and integrable. It is strictly positive at every configuration, so its integral against the probability measure \(\rho_{\ell}\) is positive. Integrating the displayed density gives \(Z_{\ell} (\beta) / Z_{\ell} (\beta) = 1\), so \(\mu_{\ell, \beta}\) is a probability measure. This argument works for every real \(\beta\) for which the displayed definition is used; the construction theorem later selects its own coefficient sequence.

Proposition 3.1. (Gauge invariance of the finite action and law) For any map \(g : \Lambda_{\ell} \to G\), transform links by

\(\displaystyle (g \cdot U)_i (x) = g (x) U_i (x) g (x + e_i)^{- 1} .\)

Then \(S_{\ell} (g \cdot U) = S_{\ell} (U)\) and \(\mu_{\ell, \beta}\) is invariant under \(U \mapsto g \cdot U\).

Proof. Substitution into the four factors of \(U_{ij} (x)\) cancels the group elements at the three intermediate vertices. The remaining factors give

\(\displaystyle (g \cdot U)_{ij} (x) = g (x) U_{ij} (x) g (x)^{- 1} .\)

Trace is invariant under conjugation, since for matrices \(A, B\) of matching finite size \(\text{tr} (AB) = \text{tr} (BA)\); hence every plaquette summand and their finite sum are unchanged.

For each link \((x, i)\), the transformed value is obtained from \(U_i (x)\) by left and right multiplication by fixed group elements. Left and right translations preserve Haar measure. The full transformation acts separately on the coordinates of the finite product \(E_{\ell}\), so the product Haar measure \(\rho_{\ell}\) is preserved. Since the density \(Z_{\ell} (\beta)^{- 1} e^{- \beta S_{\ell} (U)}\) is invariant, the weighted measure is preserved as well. This proves invariance of the law, not gauge invariance of every observable. An insertion must separately satisfy the relevant invariance condition.\(\Box\)

3.1.2Reflection positivity on an even torus

Osterwalder and Seiler established physical positivity for Euclidean lattice gauge theories [OS78]. We give the finite-torus argument for both reflection cuts and for the specified unitary representation of the compact gauge group.

We next prove the finite-volume positivity needed for the reflection construction. Fix an axis as the time coordinate, let \(\ell = 2 h\) with \(h \geqslant 1\), and take \(\beta \geqslant 0\). There are two cuts: the site reflection \(t \mapsto - t\), whose strict positive sector is \(1 \leqslant t < h\), and the link reflection \(t \mapsto - 1 - t\), whose positive sector is \(0 \leqslant t < h\); both coordinates are taken modulo \(2 h\). Reflections based at other time coordinates are obtained by translation. The reflected configuration pulls back links along the reflected oriented edges; a reflected negative edge is read as the inverse of its positive representative. Write \(\Theta U\) for this configuration.

These reflections preserve the law. Fix an axis \(a\), an integer \(c\) and \(\kappa \in \{0, 1\}\), and let \(\textsf{r}\) be the involution of \(\Lambda_{\ell}\) given by \((\textsf{r} x)_a = 2 c - \kappa - x_a\) and \((\textsf{r} x)_i = x_i\) for \(i \ne a\), coordinates modulo \(\ell\). Thus \(\kappa = 0\) is a site reflection and \(\kappa = 1\) a link reflection; with \(\ell = 2 h\) and \(c = 0\) they are the two cuts above, and other \(c\) give their translates. The edge from \(x\) to \(x + e_i\) is carried to the edge from \(\textsf{r} x\) to \(\textsf{r} x + e_i\) if \(i \ne a\), and to the negatively oriented edge from \(\textsf{r} x\) to \(\textsf{r} x - e_a\) if \(i = a\). The pulled-back configuration is therefore

\(\displaystyle (\Theta U)_i (x) = U_i (\textsf{r} x) \quad (i \ne a), \qquad (\Theta U)_a (x) = U_a (\textsf{r} x - e_a)^{- 1} .\) (3.1)

Proposition 3.2. (Reflection invariance of the finite law) For every positive integer \(\ell\), axis \(a\), integer \(c\) and \(\kappa \in \{0, 1\}\), the map \(\Theta\) of (3.1) is a measurable involution of \(G^{E_{\ell}}\). It satisfies \(S_{\ell} (\Theta U) = S_{\ell} (U)\), and both the product Haar measure \(\rho_{\ell}\) and the law \(\mu_{\ell, \beta}\), \(\beta \geqslant 0\), are invariant under \(\Theta\).

Proof. Since \((\textsf{r} (\textsf{r} x - e_a))_a = x_a + 1\), applying (3.1) twice gives \((\Theta^2 U)_a (x) = U_a (x)\), and clearly \((\Theta^2 U)_i = U_i\) for \(i \ne a\). Hence \(\Theta\) is an involution. It acts on the coordinates of the product \(G^{E_{\ell}}\) by the bijections \(x \mapsto \textsf{r} x\) (for \(i \ne a\)) and \(x \mapsto \textsf{r} x - e_a\) (for \(i = a\)) of \(\Lambda_{\ell}\), composed with inversion in the \(a\)-coordinates. Haar measure on the compact group \(G\) is invariant under inversion, and a product of identical probability measures is invariant under a permutation of its factors. Therefore \(\rho_{\ell}\) is \(\Theta\)-invariant.

Now compare plaquettes. If \(a \not\in \{i, j\}\), then substitution gives \((\Theta U)_{ij} (x) = U_{ij} (\textsf{r} x)\). Let \(i < j\) with \(i = a\), and put \(z = \textsf{r} x - e_a\). Since \(\textsf{r} (x + e_a) = z\) and \(\textsf{r} (x + e_j) = \textsf{r} x + e_j\),

\(\displaystyle (\Theta U)_{aj} (x) = U_a (z)^{- 1} U_j (z) U_a (z + e_j) U_j (z + e_a)^{- 1} = U_a (z)^{- 1} \hspace{0.17em} U_{aj} (z)^{- 1} \hspace{0.17em} U_a (z) .\)

Similarly, if \(j = a\) and \(z = \textsf{r} x - e_a\), then

\(\displaystyle (\Theta U)_{ia} (x) = U_i (z + e_a) U_a (z + e_i)^{- 1} U_i (z)^{- 1} U_a (z) = U_a (z)^{- 1} \hspace{0.17em} U_{ia} (z)^{- 1} \hspace{0.17em} U_a (z) .\)

Trace is invariant under conjugation. For \(V \in U (r_G)\), \(\text{tr} \hspace{0.17em} V^{- 1} = \text{tr} \hspace{0.17em} V^{\ast} = \overline{\text{tr} \hspace{0.17em} V}\), so \(\text{Retr} \hspace{0.17em} V^{- 1} = \text{Retr} \hspace{0.17em} V\). Hence the summand of \(S_{\ell} (\Theta U)\) indexed by \((x, i, j)\) equals the summand of \(S_{\ell} (U)\) indexed by \((\textsf{r} x, i, j)\) or by \((\textsf{r} x - e_a, i, j)\). For each fixed pair \(i < j\) these are bijections of \(\Lambda_{\ell}\), so \(S_{\ell} (\Theta U) = S_{\ell} (U)\).

Finally, for every bounded Borel \(F\), invariance of \(\rho_{\ell}\) and of the density gives

\(\displaystyle \int F \circ \Theta \hspace{0.17em} d \mu_{\ell, \beta} = \frac{1}{Z_{\ell} (\beta)} \int F (\Theta U) e^{- \beta S_{\ell} (\Theta U)} \hspace{0.17em} d \rho_{\ell} (U) = \int F \hspace{0.17em} d \mu_{\ell, \beta} .\)

\(\Box\)

This supplies the reflection-invariance hypothesis of Proposition 4.2 for the Wilson law. Its seam reflection \(R_{p, a}\) is the case \(c = b_a + \ell p_a\) and \(\kappa = \delta\), on the torus of side \(2 h \ell\).

Proposition 3.3. (Finite-volume reflection positivity) For either cut, let \(F\) be a bounded Borel, gauge-invariant function of the link configuration which depends only on links with both endpoints in the strict positive sector. Then

\(\displaystyle \int F (U) \hspace{0.17em} \overline{F (\Theta U)} \hspace{0.17em} d \mu_{2 h, \beta} (U) \geqslant 0.\)

The inequality is real and the same statement holds at every translated cut and for every choice of time axis. It applies at each finite volume; no continuum limit is asserted.

Proof. We first fix the transfer-kernel normalization. Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be the spatial link configurations on two adjacent time slices, let \(u_v\) be the intervening time link at spatial vertex \(v\), and let \(e = (v, v + e_j)\) run over the positive spatial links in one slice. The temporal plaquette has holonomy \(X_e u_{v + e_j} Y_e^{- 1} u_v^{- 1}\). Put

\(\displaystyle H (X, Y) = \int \exp \hspace{-0.17em} \left[ - \beta \sum_e \left( 1 - \frac{\text{Retr} (X_e u_{v + e_j} Y_e^{- 1} u_v^{- 1})}{r_G} \right) \right] \prod_v dH_G (u_v) .\)

This is the single common integration over the family of time links, not an independent gauge average for each spatial edge. We claim that \(H\) is a positive-semidefinite kernel. Define the spatial gauge action \((u \cdot Y)_e = u_v Y_e u_{v + e_j}^{- 1}\) and the real inner product \(\langle X, Y \rangle = \sum_e \text{Retr} (X_e Y_e^{\ast})\). For \(c = \beta / r_G \geqslant 0\), set

\(\displaystyle H_0 (X, Y) = e^{- \beta |E_{\text{sp}} |} \exp (c \langle X, Y \rangle) .\)

For any finitely many \(X^a\) and complex coefficients \(z_a\), \(\sum_{a, b} \overline{z_a} z_b \langle X^a, X^b \rangle^m \geqslant 0\): view the matrix entries as real coordinates and take the squared norm of \(\sum_a z_a (X^a)^{\otimes m}\) in the complexified real tensor space. The exponential series, with nonnegative coefficients, therefore makes \(H_0\) positive semidefinite. It is invariant under a simultaneous spatial gauge transformation of both arguments. Product Haar invariance gives

\(\displaystyle H (X, Y) = \int H_0 (X, u \cdot Y) \hspace{0.17em} du = \int \hspace{-0.17em} \hspace{-0.17em} \int H_0 (v \cdot X, u \cdot Y) \hspace{0.17em} du \hspace{0.17em} dv.\)

More explicitly, if \(M_m (X) = \int (u \cdot X)^{\otimes m} \hspace{0.17em} du\), then

\(\displaystyle H (X, Y) = e^{- \beta |E_{\text{sp}} |} \sum_{m \geqslant 0} \frac{c^m}{m!} \langle M_m (X), M_m (Y) \rangle .\)

Thus \(H\) is real, symmetric, and positive semidefinite. The series converges uniformly because the spatial-link space is compact. Products of such kernels are positive semidefinite by taking tensor products of their feature spaces; the same remains true when the two boundary slice variables coincide.

For a link cut, partition the temporal links into those wholly in the positive half, those in its reflected half, and the two sets of crossing links. The spatial links are partitioned between the two halves. Every spatial plaquette belongs to one half, every non-crossing temporal plaquette belongs to one half, and the remaining temporal plaquettes occur at the two cut boundaries. Reflection identifies the two half actions. After integrating the crossing links, the unnormalized reflected expectation is a double integral over the two open half-configurations, with integrand

\(\displaystyle \Phi (x) \hspace{0.17em} \overline{\Phi (y)} \hspace{0.17em} H (X_1 (x), X_1 (y)) \hspace{0.17em} H (X_2 (x), X_2 (y)) .\)

Here \(\Phi\) is \(F\) times the positive-half Boltzmann factor, and \(X_1, X_2\) are its two boundary spatial slices. Insert the two uniformly convergent feature expansions. Fubini turns each term into the squared norm of the integral of \(\Phi\) against the corresponding tensor feature, with a nonnegative coefficient. Boundedness of \(F\) and compactness justify the interchange of integrals and sums. The integral is therefore nonnegative. This also covers \(h = 1\), when the two boundary slice variables within one half are the same.

For a site cut, condition instead on the spatial links in the two fixed reflection planes. The remaining links occur in reflected pairs, and the plaquette action splits into the action in one half, its reflection, and the spatial plaquettes contained in the fixed planes. Since \(F\) reads only links with both endpoints in the strict positive sector, the two half integrations are conjugates. Their product is an absolute square, multiplied by the positive Boltzmann weight of the fixed-plane plaquettes. The same factorization works when the strict sector is empty. Product Haar invariance supplies the reflected change of variables, including inversion of reversed links. All integrands are bounded and measurable on a compact finite product, so Fubini's theorem applies. Finally \(Z_{2 h} (\beta) > 0\); division by it preserves nonnegativity. Translation of the cut and permutation of axes preserve the product prior and plaquette action, proving the remaining cases.\(\Box\)

3.1.3Scales, observables, and the ordered limit

For mesh index \(N\) and physical-period index \(p\), the family uses strictly increasing positive-integer sequences \(D_N\) and \(P_p\). Set

\(\displaystyle a_N = D_N^{- 1}, \qquad \ell_{N, p} = P_p D_N = P_p / a_N, \qquad \Lambda_{N, p} = (\mathbb{Z}/ \ell_{N, p} \mathbb{Z})^4 .\)

Thus \(\ell_{N, p}\) is a site count per axis, while \(P_p\) is the physical linear period. In the selected construction, every \(P_p\) is divisible by four and every \(\beta_{N, p}\) is positive. Consequently Proposition 3.3 applies on every one of its finite \((N, p)\) grids. These extra properties are of the constructed schedule and coupling; strict increase alone in the abstract family definition does not impose either one. A site is embedded in Euclidean spacetime by multiplying the symmetric least-absolute residue in each coordinate by \(a_N\). A token \(v\) specifies an insertion \(O_{N, p} (v, z ; U)\). We require the insertion to be continuous and gauge invariant, with the fixed-stencil locality specified in Section 1.

The finite \(m\)-point moment is the integral of the product of its \(m\) insertions under \(\mu_{\ell_{N, p}, \beta_{N, p}}\). Smearing uses a lattice sum weighted by \(a_N^{4 m}\). Continuity and compactness of the finite configuration space give integrability of each finite product. The public convergence interface first demands, at each fixed suitable physical volume, a full \(N \to \infty\) mesh limit of these smeared moments, and only then a volume limit along a specified strictly increasing subsequence. It is not an arbitrary joint or diagonal limit. The support of a test function must eventually fit inside the period box and its arguments must remain separated as specified in Section 1.

The next task is to control these moments as the number of links tends to infinity. The required estimates must concern the same bare coefficients and local insertions at all cutoffs and periods. The extraction argument in the next section isolates a local modulus of continuity and a local size bound; the renormalization construction must supply those estimates with the indicated order of quantifiers.

Corollary 3.4. (Finite smeared reflection forms on the selected grids) Fix finitely many positive-time words, of lengths \(n_{\nu} \geqslant 0\), with continuous gauge-invariant insertions and compactly supported test functions \(f_{\nu}\). Suppose every time coordinate in their supports lies in \([\varepsilon, R]\), where \(\varepsilon > 0\), and every spatial coordinate lies in \([- R, R]\). Suppose the insertions in these words read only links whose endpoints are within \(b\) lattice steps of their insertion site, for one fixed \(b < \infty\). For a fixed selected physical period \(P_p > 2 R\), define

\(\displaystyle A_{\nu, N, p} (U) = a_N^{4 n_{\nu}} \sum_{z_1, \ldots, z_{n_{\nu}} \in \Lambda_{N, p}} f_{\nu} (a_N \ell (z_1), \ldots, a_N \ell (z_{n_{\nu}})) \prod_{j = 1}^{n_{\nu}} O_{N, p} (v_{\nu, j}, z_j ; U),\)

where \(\ell\) is the centered lift on this torus; a word of length zero is its constant coefficient. For all sufficiently large \(N\), the matrix

\(\displaystyle \left( \int A_{\nu, N, p} (U) \overline{A_{\mu, N, p} (\Theta U)} \hspace{0.17em} d \mu_{\ell_{N, p}, \beta_{N, p}} (U) \right)_{\nu, \mu}\)

is positive semidefinite. The same mesh threshold works for every selected period \(P_p \geqslant P_{\ast} > 2 R\).

Proof. Choose \(N_0\) so that for \(N \geqslant N_0\),

\(\displaystyle ba_N < \tfrac{1}{2} \min \{\varepsilon, P_{\ast} / 2 - R\} .\)

Such a threshold exists because the strictly increasing integer denominators \(D_N\) tend to infinity. A nonzero summand has every insertion time between \(\varepsilon\) and \(R\). Every endpoint read by its insertions therefore has physical time strictly between \(0\) and \(P_p / 2\). In lattice coordinates this is exactly the strict positive sector of the site reflection on the even torus of side \(P_p D_N\). The same margin keeps all stencil endpoints away from the boundary of the centered coordinate box. Thus each \(A_{\nu, N, p}\) depends only on links with both endpoints in that sector. It is bounded and Borel: it is a finite sum of products of continuous functions on the compact configuration space. It is gauge invariant for the same reason, using the gauge invariance of each insertion. These properties hold also for any finite linear combination \(F = \sum_{\nu} c_{\nu} A_{\nu, N, p}\). Apply Proposition 3.3 to \(F\) and expand the finite sum to obtain the stated positive form. The bounds used only \(P_p \geqslant P_{\ast}\), which proves the uniformity assertion.

Expanding an entry of the matrix gives the product of the two smearing weights, namely \(a_N^{4 (n_{\nu} + n_{\mu})}\), and the corresponding finite Wilson moment under its normalized law. If the reflected insertion is written using its reflected label and reflected site, its exact covariance identity identifies this with the usual reflected smeared moment; there is no extra volume or partition-function factor. The corollary provides its finite positivity. Passing that identity through an ordered mesh and volume limit uses the convergence and reflected-label matching of Proposition 4.20; those are separate from the support-margin argument proved here.\(\Box\)

3.2From strip estimates to an order-two atlas

The local flat-sector estimate requires an order-two atlas for the two response kernels. This section proves the quantitative extraction of that atlas from the exponentially localized bed kernels and the supplied tower cross-kernel. It isolates what the construction has to provide: the strip receipt for the tower kernel and its first differences. The receipt-to-atlas argument itself is proved here.

3.2.1Cover and installed kernels

Write \(\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{Z}^4\) with \(|x|_1 = \sum_{a = 1}^4 |x_a |\). For an odd positive integer \(L\), let \(\Lambda_L = (\mathbb{Z}/ L\mathbb{Z})^4\), let \(\pi_L\) be reduction modulo \(L\), and let \(\ell_L\) denote the centered representative. There is a unique \(\rho_L (x) \in \mathcal{C}\) such that

\(\displaystyle x = \ell_L (\pi_L x) + L \rho_L (x) .\)

For \(x, y \in \mathcal{C}\), put

\(\displaystyle d_L (x, y) = L^{- 1} |x - Ly|_1 .\)

The centered representative satisfies \(| \ell_L (r) |_1 \leqslant 2 L\), hence, for every \(x, y \in \mathcal{C}\),

\(\displaystyle d_L (x, y) \leqslant |y - \rho_L (x) |_1 + 2 \leqslant |y - \rho_L (x) |_1 + 4. \) (3.2)

We retain the harmless margin \(4\) used in the finite-scale estimates.

For finite type sets \(F, G\), a cross-stencil is a family \(T (r, i, j, z) \in \mathbb{R}\) indexed by \(r \in \Lambda_L\), \(i \in F\), \(j \in G\), and \(z \in \mathcal{C}\). Its width-\(\alpha\) strip cost is the per-label bound

\(\displaystyle \sum_{z \in \mathcal{C}} e^{\alpha |z|_1} |T (r, i, j, z) | \leqslant C.\)

For each coordinate direction \(a\), define its residue difference by

\(\displaystyle (\nabla_a^L T) (r, i, j, z) = T (r + \pi_L (e_a), i, j, z - \epsilon_a (r)) - T (r, i, j, z), \qquad \epsilon_a (r) = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} e_a, & 2 \ell_L (r)_a = L - 1,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{array} \right.\)

Here \(L\) is odd, so the boundary condition in the displayed case is equivalently \(\ell_L (r)_a = \lfloor L / 2 \rfloor\); this is the centered-lift carry convention. The installed cover kernel is

\(\displaystyle \hat{T} ((x, i), (y, j)) = T (\pi_L x, i, j, y - \rho_L x) . \) (3.3)

Its forward difference is

\(\displaystyle (\partial_a \hat{T}) ((x, i), (y, j)) = \hat{T} ((x + e_a, i), (y, j)) - \hat{T} ((x, i), (y, j)) .\)

The centered-lift identity gives the exact relation

\(\displaystyle \partial_a \hat{T} = \widehat{\nabla_a^L T} . \) (3.4)

Indeed, if \(x = \ell_L (r) + Lv\), then \(x + e_a = \ell_L (r + \pi_L (e_a)) + L (v + \epsilon_a (r))\); substituting this identity in (3.3) produces the displayed difference formula.

Lemma 3.5. (Installation of strip estimates) Suppose \(0 \leqslant \sigma \leqslant \alpha\) and \(C \geqslant 0\). If \(T\) has strip cost \(C / L^2\) at width \(\alpha\) and every \(\nabla_a^L T\) has strip cost \(C / L^3\) at that width, then for every fixed pair of types \(i, j\),

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \sum_y e^{\sigma d_L (x, y)} | \hat{T} ((x, i), (y, j)) | & \leqslant e^{4 \sigma} CL^{- 2},\\ \sum_x e^{\sigma d_L (x, y)} | \hat{T} ((x, i), (y, j)) | & \leqslant e^{4 \sigma} CL^2,\\ \sum_y e^{\sigma d_L (x, y)} | \partial_a \hat{T} ((x, i), (y, j)) | & \leqslant e^{4 \sigma} CL^{- 3} . \end{array}\)

Thus \(\hat{T}\) has the order-two atlas bounds with cost \(e^{4 \sigma} C\): row \(L^{- 2}\), column \(L^2\), and first-difference row \(L^{- 3}\). These are per-label bounds; summing over a finite type set adds its cardinality explicitly.

Proof. Fix \(x\) and put \(r = \pi_L x\), \(v = \rho_L x\). In the row sum, the substitution \(z = y - v\) is a bijection of \(\mathcal{C}\). By (3.2),

\(\displaystyle \sum_y e^{\sigma d_L (x, y)} | \hat{T} ((x, i), (y, j)) | \leqslant e^{2 \sigma} \sum_z e^{\sigma |z|_1} |T (r, i, j, z) | \leqslant e^{2 \sigma} CL^{- 2} \leqslant e^{4 \sigma} CL^{- 2} .\)

The last inequality uses \(\sigma \geqslant 0\).

For the column, decompose every \(x\) uniquely as \(x = \ell_L (r) + Lv\), where \((r, v) \in \Lambda_L \times \mathcal{C}\). At fixed \(r\), the installed coefficient is \(T (r, i, j, y - v)\) and

\(\displaystyle d_L (\ell_L (r) + Lv, y) \leqslant |v - y|_1 + 2,\)

again because \(| \ell_L (r) |_1 / L \leqslant 2\). Thus the sum over \(v\) is at most \(e^{2 \sigma} CL^{- 2}\) for each \(r\). There are \(| \Lambda_L | = L^4\) residues, so the column sum is at most \(e^{2 \sigma} CL^2 \leqslant e^{4 \sigma} CL^2\). Finally apply the row argument to each \(\nabla_a^L T\), use (3.4), and replace the cost \(C / L^2\) by \(C / L^3\).\(\Box\)

3.2.2Composition with translation-invariant bed kernels

A translation-invariant row stencil \(I\) on a finite type set \(F\) has coefficients \(I (i, b, u)\) for \(u \in \mathcal{C}\). Its strip cost \(C_I\) at width \(\alpha\) means

\(\displaystyle \sum_{u \in \mathcal{C}} e^{\alpha |u|_1} |I (i, b, u) | \leqslant C_I \quad \text{for every } i, b \in F.\)

It acts on the cover by convolution. Let \(J\) be a second such stencil, on a finite type set \(G\), with strip cost \(C_J\). Given a cross kernel \(K\) from \(\mathcal{C} \times F\) to \(\mathcal{C} \times G\), form its sandwich

\(\displaystyle S ((x, i), (y, j)) = \sum_{u, v \in \mathcal{C}} \sum_{b \in F, \hspace{0.17em} c \in G} I (i, b, u) K ((x + u, b), (y + v, c)) J (c, j, - v) .\)

Assume the sums below are absolutely convergent; this follows, in each application here, from the weighted bounds just stated and Tonelli's theorem.

Lemma 3.6. (Sandwich atlas estimate) Suppose \(I, J\) have nonnegative strip costs \(C_I, C_J\) at width \(\alpha\), and \(K\) has per-label atlas bounds of order two with nonnegative cost \(Q\) at width \(\sigma\). If \(0 \leqslant \sigma \leqslant \alpha\) and \(L \geqslant 1\), then \(S\) has per-label order-two atlas bounds at width \(\sigma\) with cost

\(\displaystyle |F| \hspace{0.17em} |G| \hspace{0.17em} C_I C_J Q.\)

The first input difference of \(S\) is obtained by replacing \(K\) with its first input difference in the same formula; in particular its row cost is at most \(|F| \hspace{0.17em} |G| C_I C_J Q / L^3\).

Proof. For \(x, y, u, v \in \mathcal{C}\), the triangle inequality gives

\(\displaystyle d_L (x, y) \leqslant d_L (x + u, y + v) + |u|_1 / L + |v|_1 .\)

Consequently the exponential weight at \((x, y)\) is bounded by the central kernel weight multiplied by \(e^{\alpha |u|_1} e^{\alpha |v|_1}\), since \(0 \leqslant \sigma / L \leqslant \alpha\) and \(0 \leqslant \sigma \leqslant \alpha\). Sum absolute values first in \(y, v, c\). For each fixed \(u, b\), the kernel row bound, summed over the \(|G|\) possible intermediate output types \(c\), is \(|G| QL^{- 2}\). For each resulting intermediate output, the stencil \(J\) contributes at most \(C_J\). Summing in \(u, b\) against \(I\) costs at most \(|F| C_I\). The row bound is therefore

\(\displaystyle |F| C_I \cdot |G| QL^{- 2} \cdot C_J .\)

For the column, fix the external input type \(i\) and sum first in \(x, u, b\). The stencil \(I\) contributes \(C_I\); the kernel column bound, summed over the \(|F|\) possible input types, is \(|F| QL^2\); and summing the remaining displacement and type of \(J\) contributes \(|G| C_J\). This gives \(|F| |G| C_I C_J QL^2\).

Subtract the formulas for \(S ((x + e_a, i), (y, j))\) and \(S ((x, i), (y, j))\) term by term with the same summation variables. Since \(I, J\) do not depend on their torus positions, this replaces \(K ((x + u, b), (y + v, c))\) by

\(\displaystyle K ((x + u + e_a, b), (y + v, c)) - K ((x + u, b), (y + v, c)) .\)

Repeating the row estimate with the difference-row cost \(Q / L^3\) proves the last assertion. Absolute convergence follows from the same calculation with all exponential weights replaced by \(1\); this licenses the subtraction.\(\Box\)

Theorem 3.7. (Response atlas from primitive strip estimates) Let \(F\) be the common finite type set of the fine inverse stencil and the coarse metric stencil, and let \(T\) be the tower cross-kernel. Suppose the two bed stencils have nonnegative strip costs \(C_f, C_c\) at width \(\alpha\), and \(C_T \geqslant 0\), while \(T\) has base strip cost \(C_T / L^2\) and each first residue-difference strip cost \(C_T / L^3\) at that width. For \(0 \leqslant \sigma \leqslant \alpha\), let \(\hat{T}\) be the installed cross-kernel and let

\(\displaystyle K = I_f \hspace{0.17em} \hat{T} \hspace{0.17em} I_c\)

denote its convolution sandwich with the fine inverse and coarse metric stencils. Then \(K\) has the order-two atlas bounds at width \(\sigma\) with cost

\(\displaystyle Q = |F|^2 e^{4 \sigma} C_f C_c C_T . \) (3.5)

In particular its per-label row, column and first-difference row bounds are \(Q / L^2\), \(QL^2\) and \(Q / L^3\), respectively.

Proof. Lemma 3.5 gives the order-two atlas for \(\hat{T}\) with cost \(e^{4 \sigma} C_T\). Apply Lemma 3.6 with \(I_f\) and \(I_c\); the two intermediate type sums each contribute one factor \(|F|\). This yields exactly (3.5) and all three asserted estimates.\(\Box\)

Proposition 3.8. (Constant-column test for the constructed response) In Theorem 3.7, suppose \(T\) is the two-form wire tower of a flight with \(4 \leqslant t \leqslant j \leqslant t + 1\), odd block side \(L \geqslant 3\) and kernel parameter \(k > 0\). Then the sum of each response row over its output cover coordinate is independent of the input cover coordinate. More precisely, for fixed face labels \(i, j\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{y \in \mathcal{C}} K ((x, i), (y, j)) = L^{- 2} \sum_{b \in F} \left( \sum_{u \in \mathcal{C}} I_f (i, b, u) \right) \left( \sum_{v \in \mathcal{C}} I_c (b, j, v) \right) .\)

For every positive coarse side \(n\), periodizing \(K\) over the output fibre gives the finite carrier formed from these same fine and coarse bed rows and the tower installed between sides \(Ln\) and \(n\).

Proof. The wire calculation at zero momentum in Subsection 3.7 gives the identity matrix at the principal alias and zero at all other aliases. In the residue-symbol sum of Subsection 3.5, the tower scaling therefore gives \(L^{- 2} I\) at zero momentum for every residue. The strip receipt makes the coefficient rows absolutely summable, so evaluating their Fourier series at zero yields the same value after taking real parts, since this zero-momentum matrix is real:

\(\displaystyle \sum_z T (r, i, j, z) = L^{- 2} \delta_{ij}, \qquad r \in \Lambda_L .\)

The installation formula reindexes \(z\) by \(y - \rho_L (x)\), and the sum of each installed row is therefore the same \(L^{- 2} \delta_{ij}\), independently of \(x\).

Insert this identity in the sandwich formula. The two bed rows are translation invariant and absolutely summable; the atlas row bound makes the full triple sum absolutely convergent. Summing first in \(y\) eliminates the tower's intermediate output label and gives the displayed product of the two bed-row sums. In particular the result does not depend on \(x\). To identify the finite carrier, periodize the final output \(y\) modulo \(n\). Group the fine intermediate coordinate modulo \(Ln\) and the coarse one modulo \(n\). The identity \(\rho_L (x + Lnw) = \rho_L (x) + nw\) transfers the fine period shift to the coarse argument of the tower. Absolute convergence permits the exchange of the fibre sums. Each translation-invariant bed row then gives its finite convolution matrix, and the centered-residue decomposition gives the installed tower matrix. The resulting finite sum is exactly the matrix product of these three factors. This is the quotient-fibre calculation of Subsection 3.3, now applied to the two successive products. It works for every \(n \geqslant 1\) without a new period-dependent constant.\(\Box\)

Remark 3.9. (What this estimate does not supply) The theorem starts with the weighted cross-kernel receipts for \(T\) and its first differences, and with the two bed-stencil receipts. The wire calculation in Sections 3.5 and 3.6 supplies a uniform tower receipt for the flights and fixed parameters stated there. Applying it here also requires identifying those flights and the installed tower with the chosen RG history and providing both bed bounds for the same data, uniformly in the period. The atlas calculation alone does not establish those matches.

3.3One-step propagation of a neutral curvature representative

This section gives the local mathematical content of the one-step flat-sector update. It concerns an order-zero quadratic precision difference; it does not assert convergence of the full Wilson theory. The inputs are exact one-step Schur data and weighted kernel estimates. All cost sums below are absolute weighted sums in \([0, \infty]\); signed kernel sums are used only after their absolute convergence has been established.

3.3.1Spaces and estimates

Let \(\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{Z}^4\) be the cover with Laurent distance \(\text{sep} (x, y) = \sum_{a = 1}^4 |x_a - y_a |\). For a positive integer scale \(L\), set

\(\displaystyle \delta_L (x, y) = L^{- 1} \text{sep} (x, Ly), \qquad \mathcal{W} (a, t) = |a| e^t .\)

For finite label sets \(I, J\), a kernel \(K\) is a real function on \((\mathcal{C} \times I) \times (\mathcal{C} \times J)\). All three atlas bounds below are per fixed finite label: for every cover point \(x\), input label \(i\), and output label \(j\),

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} & \sum_{y \in \mathcal{C}} \mathcal{W} (K ((x, i), (y, j)), \omega \delta_L (x, y)) \leqslant bL^{- 2}, \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.6)} \\ & \sum_{x \in \mathcal{C}} \mathcal{W} (K ((x, i), (y, j)), \omega \delta_L (x, y)) \leqslant bL^2, \quad (y, j) \text{ fixed}, \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.7)} \\ & \sum_{y \in \mathcal{C}} \mathcal{W} (K ((x + e_a, i), (y, j)) - K ((x, i), (y, j)), \omega \delta_L (x, y)) \leqslant bL^{- 3} . \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.8)} \end{array}\)

In the column line, \(i\) and \((y, j)\) are fixed and the sum is over the cover coordinate of the input. These are the order-two atlas costs. Summing a row bound over all \(|J|\) output labels or a column bound over all \(|I|\) input labels contributes the corresponding explicit cardinality factor; neither line above already includes that factor. Here and below an estimate stated at width \(\omega\) also holds at every smaller nonnegative width.

A rank-one packet \(P\) has coefficients \(P ((x, i), (v, j))\), with both positions in \(\mathcal{C}\) and both types in \(I\). Define its folded cost by

\(\displaystyle \|P\|_{\tau, \text{fold}} := \sup_{(x, i)} \sum_{(v, j) \in \mathcal{C} \times I} |P ((x, i), (v, j)) | e^{\tau \text{sep} (x, v)} .\)

The supremum is taken in \([0, \infty]\); in particular it is zero if the input label set is empty. The packet is neutral when \(\sum_{v \in \mathcal{C}} P ((x, i), (v, j)) = 0\) for every \(x, i, j\). It is diagonally stationary when translating both cover positions by the same unit vector leaves each coefficient unchanged.

Let \(T, U\) map fine cover-label spaces to coarse cover-label spaces, using the same finite type set \(I\), \(|I| = m\). The push is

\(\displaystyle (P \star (T, U)) ((y, k), (z, l)) = \sum_{(x, i) \in \mathcal{C} \times I} T ((x, i), (y, k)) \sum_{(v, j) \in \mathcal{C} \times I} P ((x, i), (v, j)) U ((v, j), (z, l)) . \) (3.9)

Its folded cost is defined by the same formula as for \(P\), with fine labels replaced by coarse labels and \(\tau\) replaced by \(\sigma\).

3.3.2Centering and the kernel row bound

Lemma 3.10. (Centering bound from an order-two atlas) Suppose \(U\) has the per-label difference bound (3.8) with cost \(p\) and width \(\omega\). For \(x, v \in \mathcal{C}\) and types \(j, l \in I\), set

\(\displaystyle U_x (v, j ; z, l) = U ((v, j), (z, l)) - U ((x, j), (z, l)) .\)

If \(\tau > 0\), \(0 \leqslant \sigma \leqslant \omega\), and \(2 \sigma \leqslant L \tau\), then for every fixed output type \(l\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{z \in \mathcal{C}} |U_x (v, j ; z, l) | e^{\sigma \delta_L (x, z)} \leqslant \frac{2 p}{\tau L^3} e^{\tau \text{sep} (x, v)} . \) (3.10)

Proof. Let \(a = \text{sep} (x, v)\). Choose a coordinate path \(x = v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_a = v\) with each increment a signed unit vector. The cover distance triangle inequality gives

\(\displaystyle \delta_L (x, z) \leqslant \text{sep} (x, v_k) / L + \delta_L (v_k, z) \leqslant a / L + \delta_L (v_k, z) .\)

For each path edge, the per-label difference row bound (3.8) applies at the initial endpoint for a positive edge and at the other endpoint for a negative edge, after reversing its sign. Both endpoints are at distance at most \(a\) from \(x\). Thus its weighted row at center \(x\) is bounded by \(pL^{- 3} e^{\sigma a / L}\). Telescoping the coefficient difference and summing the \(a\) edge bounds yields

\(\displaystyle \sum_z |U ((v, j), (z, l)) - U ((x, j), (z, l)) | e^{\sigma \delta_L (x, z)} \leqslant pL^{- 3} ae^{\sigma a / L} .\)

Since \(0 \leqslant \sigma / L \leqslant \tau / 2\),

\(\displaystyle ae^{\sigma a / L} \leqslant ae^{\tau a / 2} \leqslant (2 / \tau) e^{\tau a},\)

where the last inequality is \(u \leqslant e^u\) for \(u = \tau a / 2 \geqslant 0\). This proves the claim, including \(a = 0\).\(\Box\)

Remark 3.11. The path bound is for one fixed output label. Summing it over all \(m\) output labels gives the aggregate outgoing-row bound used below. No cancellation has yet been used. Neutrality is used next to replace \(U\) by this centered kernel inside the convolution.

3.3.3Weighted push estimate

Lemma 3.12. (Weighted convolution) Let \(P\) be a neutral rank-one packet with \(\|P\|_{\tau, \text{fold}} \leqslant r\), where \(r \geqslant 0\). Suppose \(T\) has the order-two atlas row and column bounds with cost \(q \geqslant 0\), and \(U\) has the order-two atlas bounds with cost \(p \geqslant 0\), both at width \(\omega\). Assume explicitly \(\tau > 0\), \(0 \leqslant \sigma \leqslant \omega\), and \(2 \sigma \leqslant L \tau\). Then

\(\displaystyle \|P \star (T, U)\|_{\sigma, \text{fold}} \leqslant \frac{m^2 q (2 / \tau) p}{L} \hspace{0.17em} r. \)

Proof. Before using neutrality, the uncentered absolute sum is already finite. For a fixed output \((y, k)\), first sum over the outgoing cover coordinate and type: its per-label row budget is \(pL^{- 2}\), so summing the \(m\) output types costs \(mpL^{- 2}\). The packet factor then sums to at most \(r\), since its nonnegative exponential weight is at least one. Finally the root column has per-input- type budget \(qL^2\) and summing the \(m\) input types costs \(mqL^2\). Thus

\(\displaystyle \sum_{x, i, v, j, z, l} |T ((x, i), (y, k)) P ((x, i), (v, j)) U ((v, j), (z, l)) | \leqslant (mqL^2) r (mpL^{- 2}) = m^2 qpr.\)

This finite majorant justifies Fubini and all subsequent changes of order, including the neutrality replacement. For fixed \((x, i)\) and output type \(l\), neutrality gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_{v, j} P ((x, i), (v, j)) U ((v, j), (z, l)) = \sum_{v, j} P ((x, i), (v, j)) [U ((v, j), (z, l)) - U ((x, j), (z, l))] .\)

Indeed, for each \(j\), the subtracted term is independent of \(v\) and its coefficient sum is zero. The folded bound makes these coefficient sums absolutely convergent; the Atlas pointwise bounds and the centered-row bound make the products summable. Thus this replacement and the following uses of Tonelli are legitimate.

Fix an output market \((y, k)\). For output \((z, l)\), the cover geometry gives

\(\displaystyle \text{sep} (y, z) \leqslant \delta_L (x, y) + \delta_L (x, z) .\)

After taking absolute values in the push and summing in \((z, l)\), the root factor is weighted by \(\sigma \delta_L (x, y)\) and the outgoing factor by \(\sigma \delta_L (x, z)\). For every fixed pair of input and output types of \(U\), Lemma 3.10 bounds the outgoing cover sum by \(2 p (\tau L^3)^{- 1} e^{\tau \text{sep} (x, v)}\). Summing over its \(m\) output types gives the factor \(m\). The exponential is exactly the packet weight on the leg \(v\). Summing the packet over \((v, j)\) therefore costs at most \(r\) for each fixed \((x, i)\).

The remaining root sum is over \((x, i)\). For the fixed output \((y, k)\), the column bound in (3.7) is per fixed input type \(i\); summing over the \(m\) possible \(i\) costs \(mqL^2\). Consequently the whole nonnegative sum is bounded by

\(\displaystyle (mqL^2) \left( m \frac{2 p}{\tau L^3} \right) r = \frac{m^2 q (2 / \tau) p}{L} r.\)

This bound is finite and proves both absolute convergence of the defining push and the asserted folded estimate. More formally, the argument applies first to finite subsets of each countable cover sum; the uniform right-hand side bounds their increasing limit, so monotone convergence gives the full sums and justifies every rearrangement.\(\Box\)

Remark 3.13. The estimate is a neutral-packet estimate: the extra factor \(L^{- 1}\) comes from centering \(U\) and using its first-difference row cost \(L^{- 3}\), while the coarse-root column cost contributes \(L^2\). Without neutrality, the centering identity is unavailable and the stated contraction does not follow.

3.3.4Periodization and matching of the pushed representative

For each positive integer \(n\), let \(\Lambda_n = (\mathbb{Z}/ n\mathbb{Z})^4\), let \(\pi_n : \mathcal{C} \to \Lambda_n\) be coordinatewise reduction, and let \(\ell_n : \Lambda_n \to \mathcal{C}\) choose the centered representative with \(\pi_n \ell_n\) the identity. The fibre of \(\pi_n\) over \(y\) is exactly \(\{\ell_n (y) + nr : r \in \mathcal{C}\}\): if \(\pi_n z = y\), then each coordinate of \(z - \ell_n (y)\) is divisible by \(n\), with unique quotient. Fix positive periods \(n_f, n_c, n\) when using a composition. For a kernel \(K\) and output side \(n\), define its displayed finite matrix by

\(\displaystyle (D_n K) ((x, i), (y, j)) := \sum_{r \in \mathcal{C}} K ((x, i), (\ell_n (y) + nr, j)) .\)

This display is used only when its fibre sum converges absolutely. When the input side is \(n_f\) and output side is \(n_c\), \(K\) matches a matrix \(A\) if these fibre sums converge absolutely for every input and output index and

\(\displaystyle (D_{n_c} K) ((x, i), (y, j)) = A ((\pi_{n_f} (x), i), (y, j)) \quad \text{for every } (x, i) \in \mathcal{C} \times I, (y, j) \in \Lambda_{n_c} \times J. \) (3.11)

Thus matching requires the same value for every cover representative of the input torus point, not just its chosen lift. A kernel is city-covariant at ratio \(L\) if

\(\displaystyle K ((x + Lu, i), (y + u, j)) = K ((x, i), (y, j)) \quad (u, x, y \in \mathcal{C}) . \) (3.12)

For kernels \(K\) and \(U\) with an intermediate finite type set \(J\), define their cover composition by

\(\displaystyle (K \diamond U) ((x, i), (z, k)) := \sum_{v \in \mathcal{C}} \sum_{j \in J} K ((x, i), (v, j)) U ((v, j), (z, k)) .\)

Suppose \(K\) matches \(A\) from side \(n_f\) to side \(n_c\), and \(U\) matches \(B\) from side \(n_c\) to side \(n\). Assume for each fixed external input and output that the total double absolute sum over the intermediate and final cover coordinates and intermediate labels is finite. Then

\(\displaystyle (D_n (K \diamond U)) ((x, i), (y, k)) = \sum_{w \in \Lambda_{n_c}} \sum_{j \in J} A ((\pi_{n_f} (x), i), (w, j)) B ((w, j), (y, k)) . \) (3.13)

Indeed, replace the output fibre in \(D_n\) by its explicit coset, interchange absolutely convergent sums, and first sum in the final cover lift:

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} (D_n (K \diamond U)) ((x, i), (y, k)) & = \sum_{v, j} K ((x, i), (v, j)) \sum_{r \in \mathcal{C}} U ((v, j), (\ell_n (y) + nr, k))\\ & = \sum_{v, j} K ((x, i), (v, j)) B ((\pi_{n_c} (v), j), (y, k))\\ & = \sum_{w, j} A ((\pi_{n_f} (x), i), (w, j)) B ((w, j), (y, k)) . \end{aligned}\)

The second equality is the matching identity for \(U\). For the third, partition \(\mathcal{C}\) into the disjoint fibres \(\pi_{n_c}^{- 1} (w)\) and use the matching identity for \(K\) on each fibre. This proves the exact finite-matrix product, including the sum over intermediate types.

The transpose also has an exact matching rule. Suppose \(n_f = Ln_c\), \(T\) is city-covariant at ratio \(L\) and matches \(M\) from side \(n_f\) to side \(n_c\), and its rows and columns are absolutely summable for each fixed pair of types. The atlas bounds above supply this summability. Then \(\text{swap} \hspace{0.17em} T\), defined by \((\text{swap} \hspace{0.17em} T) ((x, j), (y, i)) = T ((y, i), (x, j))\), matches \(M^{\textsf{T}}\) from side \(n_c\) to side \(n_f\). For fixed \(x \in \mathcal{C}\) and \(y \in \Lambda_{n_f}\), its display is

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} (D_{n_f} \text{swap} \hspace{0.17em} T) ((x, j), (y, i)) & = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{C}} T ((\ell_{n_f} (y) + n_f v, i), (x, j))\\ & = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{C}} T ((\ell_{n_f} (y), i), (x - n_c v, j))\\ & = \sum_{r \in \mathcal{C}} T ((\ell_{n_f} (y), i), (\ell_{n_c} (\pi_{n_c} x) + n_c r, j))\\ & = (D_{n_c} T) ((\ell_{n_f} (y), i), (\pi_{n_c} x, j)) = M ((y, i), (\pi_{n_c} x, j)) . \end{aligned}\)

The second line uses city covariance with shift \(u = n_c v\). In the third line use \(x = \ell_{n_c} (\pi_{n_c} x) + n_c \hspace{0.17em} \text{rent}_{n_c} (x)\) and the bijective reindexing \(r = \text{rent}_{n_c} (x) - v\) of \(\mathcal{C}\). The last line is the matching identity and equals the \(((\pi_{n_c} x, j), (y, i))\) entry of \(M^{\textsf{T}}\). Thus no unproved periodization rule is needed.

For a rank-one packet, define its two-point kernel by \(P^{\sharp} ((x, i), (v, j)) = P ((x, i), (v, j))\). Direct substitution in the push definition gives the cover-kernel identity

\(\displaystyle (P \star (T, U))^{\sharp} = (\text{swap} \hspace{0.17em} T) \diamond (P^{\sharp} \diamond U) . \) (3.14)

For completeness, the absolute sum in the two compositions is finite. The row atlas estimate implies \(|U ((v, j), (z, l)) | \leqslant pL^{- 2}\), and the folded bound implies \(\sum_{v, j} |P ((x, i), (v, j)) | \leqslant r\). Hence \(\sum_{v, j} |P ((x, i), (v, j)) U ((v, j), (z, l)) | \leqslant pL^{- 2} r\). The outer sum against \(\text{swap} \hspace{0.17em} T\) is finite by the column atlas bound, summed over its \(m\) input types. For the output-fibre rearrangement, fix \((y, k)\) and the final type \(l\) and sum over all \(z \in \mathcal{C}\) instead:

\(\displaystyle \sum_{x, i, v, j, z} |T ((x, i), (y, k)) P ((x, i), (v, j)) U ((v, j), (z, l)) | \leqslant (mqL^2) \hspace{0.17em} r \hspace{0.17em} (pL^{- 2}) = mqpr.\)

Here the row bound of \(U\) is uniform in \((v, j)\), the folded packet bound is uniform in \((x, i)\), and the column bound of \(T\) is summed over its \(m\) input types. Restricting \(z\) to any periodization fibre only decreases the sum. This bound justifies Fubini for both applications below, including the sum over every representative of the final torus point. If \(P^{\sharp}\) matches \(A\), and \(T, U\) match \(M, N\), respectively, apply (3.13) first to \(P^{\sharp}\) and \(U\), then to \(\text{swap} \hspace{0.17em} T\) and that composition. The transpose calculation above supplies the match \(M^{\textsf{T}}\) for \(\text{swap} \hspace{0.17em} T\). The resulting displayed coarse matrix is exactly \(M^{\textsf{T}} AN\).

3.3.5Exact propagation of the quadratic representative

Let \(E_n^1 =\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda_n \times I_1}\) and \(E_n^2 =\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda_n \times I_2}\) be the one-form and curvature coordinate spaces; \(d_n : E_n^1 \to E_n^2\). The precision matrices \(Q_H, Q_Y\) act on \(E_{n_f}^1\), and \(Q_{H'}, Q_{Y'}\) act on \(E_{n_c}^1\). The curvature representative matrix \(A\) acts on \(E_{n_f}^2\), whose type set is the same \(I\) used for the packet legs. The mean matrices \(M_A, N_A : E_{n_c}^1 \to E_{n_f}^1\), and the matched carrier matrices \(C_Y, C_H : E_{n_c}^2 \to E_{n_f}^2\).

Here are the exact identities used below and their derivations. The two one-step precision inputs share a blocking (coarse-averaging) map \(B : E_{n_f}^1 \to E_{n_c}^1\) and a positive blocking penalty \(\lambda\). Write \(\kappa = \lambda^{- 1}\) for its reciprocal, which is the coefficient in the dual identities. For the two histories they say

\(\displaystyle Q_H M_A = B^{\textsf{T}} Q_{H'}, \quad BM_A = I - \kappa Q_{H'}, \qquad Q_Y N_A = B^{\textsf{T}} Q_{Y'}, \quad BN_A = I - \kappa Q_{Y'} .\)

The floor data make all four precision matrices symmetric. Transposing the first identity for \(Y\) gives \(N_A^{\textsf{T}} Q_Y = Q_{Y'} B\), while transposing \(BN_A = I - \kappa Q_{Y'}\) gives \(N_A^{\textsf{T}} B^{\textsf{T}} = I - \kappa Q_{Y'}\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} N_A^{\textsf{T}} (Q_Y - Q_H) M_A & = Q_{Y'} BM_A - N_A^{\textsf{T}} B^{\textsf{T}} Q_{H'}\\ & = Q_{Y'} (I - \kappa Q_{H'}) - (I - \kappa Q_{Y'}) Q_{H'}\\ & = Q_{Y'} - Q_{H'} . \end{aligned}\)

This proves the mixed Schur identity

\(\displaystyle Q_{Y'} - Q_{H'} = N_A^{\textsf{T}} (Q_Y - Q_H) M_A . \) (3.15)

For either history, write \(G, G'\) for fine/coarse metric matrices, \(D, D'\) for the diagonal matrices, \(R\) for the installed transfer on curvature coordinates, and \(Z\) for its mean on one-form coordinates. Its carrier is \(C = G^{- 1} RG'\). The floor equalities on exact forms and the diagonal tower identity are respectively \(Gd_f = Dd_f\), \(G' d_c = D' d_c\), and \(RD' d_c = Dd_f Z\). The floor inverse identity is \(G^{- 1} G = I\). Thus, with all domains as above,

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} Cd_c & = G^{- 1} RG' d_c = G^{- 1} RD' d_c = G^{- 1} Dd_f Z = G^{- 1} Gd_f Z = d_f Z. \end{aligned}\)

Applying this calculation to the \(Y\) history with \(Z = N_A\) and the \(H\) history with \(Z = M_A\) proves the inherited identities

\(\displaystyle C_Y d_c = d_f N_A, \qquad C_H d_c = d_f M_A . \) (3.16)

Proposition 3.14. (One-step flat-sector propagation) Let \(P\) be a rank-one packet satisfying neutrality, diagonal stationarity, and \(\|P\|_{\tau, \text{fold}} \leqslant r\). Suppose the fine two-point kernel associated with \(P\) matches a matrix \(A\), which represents the curvature difference, and

\(\displaystyle d_f^{\textsf{T}} Ad_f = Q_Y - Q_H .\)

Suppose \(T, U\) are matched, on the coarse torus, by their carrier matrices \(M = C_Y\) and \(N = C_H\), respectively. They satisfy the order-two atlas bounds with nonnegative costs \(q, p\) and width \(\omega\) used in Lemma 3.12, and are city-covariant at ratio \(L\). The fine and coarse torus periods satisfy \(n_f = Ln_c\). Assume \(\tau > 0\), \(r \geqslant 0\), \(0 \leqslant \sigma \leqslant \omega\), and \(2 \sigma \leqslant L \tau\). Also assume the exact Schur and inherited identities (3.15) and(3.16). Finally suppose \(U\) has the travel property that each fixed pair of finite labels has a row sum independent of the cover point. Then the pushed packet \(P' = P \star (T, U)\) has the following properties:

  1. \(P'\) is neutral;

  2. \(P'\) is diagonally stationary;

  3. its folded cost satisfies

    \(\displaystyle \|P' \|_{\sigma, \text{fold}} \leqslant \frac{m^2 q (2 / \tau) p}{L} \hspace{0.17em} r ;\)

  4. \(P'\) is matched by \(M^{\textsf{T}} AN\), and

    \(\displaystyle d_c^{\textsf{T}} (M^{\textsf{T}} AN) d_c = Q_{Y'} - Q_{H'} .\)

Proof. The folded estimate is Lemma 3.12. For neutrality, expand the pushed constant-column sum, interchange the sums using the same absolute weighted bounds, and first sum in the output cover coordinate of \(U\). Its value depends only on the finite labels and is independent of the intermediate cover point by the travel assumption. The remaining cover sum is then the neutral column sum of \(P\), hence is zero. For diagonal stationarity, translate the outer summation variable and then the inner one. The two covariance identities for \(T\) and \(U\), together with the simultaneous stationarity of \(P\), leave each summand unchanged. Absolute convergence just established permits these translations in the sums.

By the exact reindexing argument in (3.13) and the transpose calculation following it, the pushed packet is matched by \(M^{\textsf{T}} AN\); the two applications have absolutely convergent sums by the weighted estimates. Finally, using (3.16),

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} d_c^{\textsf{T}} M^{\textsf{T}} ANd_c & = (Md_c)^{\textsf{T}} A (Nd_c)\\ & = (d_f N_A)^{\textsf{T}} A (d_f M_A)\\ & = N_A^{\textsf{T}} (d_f^{\textsf{T}} Ad_f) M_A\\ & = N_A^{\textsf{T}} (Q_Y - Q_H) M_A\\ & = Q_{Y'} - Q_{H'}, \end{aligned}\)

where the final equality is (3.15). This proves the propagated curvature identity.\(\Box\)

At a fixed block size \(L\), if the displayed hypotheses hold for every coarse period \(n_c\) with the same \(p, q, r, \tau, \sigma, \omega\) and type set, then the folded-cost bound holds uniformly in \(n_c\). The proof made no additional choice or estimate depending on that period. The retained-horizon construction in Subsection 3.9 supplies the two response atlases on its fixed adjacent stages; Proposition 3.8 supplies the matching and travel assertions for the retained wire response. The remaining packet, floor, width and funding hypotheses must still be matched to the selected history.

Lemma 3.15. (The quadratic output is independent of the completion) Fix a level schedule, a cutoff \(N\), and two sequences of quadratic precisions on the scheduled tori. At each step assume a common blocking map \(B\) and penalty \(\lambda > 0\), and transports \(M_A, N_A\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle Q_H M_A = B^{\textsf{T}} Q_{H'}, \quad BM_A = I - \lambda^{- 1} Q_{H'}, \qquad Q_Y N_A = B^{\textsf{T}} Q_{Y'}, \quad BN_A = I - \lambda^{- 1} Q_{Y'} .\)

Assume \(Q_Y\) and \(Q_{Y'}\) are symmetric and that the two terminal one-form precisions at level \(N\) agree on every scheduled torus. Then their one-form precisions agree at every level \(i \leqslant N\) on every scheduled torus. If, at that level, their corresponding cover kernels have absolutely summable rows and both periodize to those finite precisions, and if the scheduled torus sides tend to infinity along the period index, the two cover kernels agree entry by entry.

Proof. At level \(N\) equality is the hypothesis. If the two fine precisions agree at level \(i + 1\), the mixed Schur identity (3.15) writes the difference of their coarse precisions as a left and right matrix multiple of that fine difference. It is therefore zero, even if the two transports are different. Backward induction proves the finite-torus assertion, separately for each period.

For the cover assertion, fix an input and an output cover coordinate and their labels. In the periodization sum the input is wrapped to the finite torus, and the output is summed over the congruence class of its wrapped coordinate. As the side tends to infinity, each fixed cover point eventually belongs to that class exactly when it is the chosen output point. The absolute row sum is a summable bound independent of the period, so dominated convergence recovers the single cover coefficient from the finite matrix entries. The same argument applies to the second kernel. Those finite entries are equal by the first part, hence their limits are equal. No equality of the two curvature metrics, their square roots, or their transports is implied.\(\Box\)

3.4A scalar decay estimate for cutoff errors

The following elementary lemma converts a one-step contraction with a polynomially controlled source into a power of the ratio of scales. In an RG application, the difficult step is to prove that the difference of two quadratic precisions has a positive coefficient majorant satisfying the hypotheses below. The lemma here does not prove that RG estimate.

Proposition 3.17. (Contraction with a polynomially controlled source) Let \(M \in \mathbb{N}\), and let

\(\displaystyle t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_M\)

be real numbers whose successive gaps satisfy

\(\displaystyle \Delta_j := t_{j + 1} - t_j \geqslant \delta > 0 \qquad (0 \leqslant j < M) .\)

Fix \(s \in \mathbb{R}\), \(Z \geqslant 0\), \(d \in \mathbb{N}\), \(c_0, \theta > 0\), and \(A \geqslant 0\). Put \(T = t_M\), choose

\(\displaystyle \mu = \min \{c_0, \theta / 2\}, \qquad \lambda = \theta - \mu, \qquad R = \max \left\{ 2, \frac{2 (d + 1)}{\lambda} \right\}, \qquad y_j = R + Z + |t_j - s|,\)

and set \(q = \exp (- \lambda \delta / 2)\) and \(C = A / (1 - q)\). Suppose \(f_0, \ldots, f_M\) and \(g_0, \ldots, g_{M - 1}\) are nonnegative real numbers such that, for \(0 \leqslant j < M\),

\(\displaystyle f_j \leqslant e^{- \theta \Delta_j} f_{j + 1} + g_j, \qquad e^{c_0 (T - t_j)} g_j \leqslant Ay_j^d, \) (3.17)

and at the terminal index

\(\displaystyle f_M \leqslant Ay_M^d . \) (3.18)

Then, for every \(j \in \{0, \ldots, M\}\),

\(\displaystyle f_j \leqslant Cy_j^d e^{- \mu (T - t_j)} . \) (3.19)

If positive scales are defined by \(a_j = e^{- t_j}\), the exponential factor in (3.19) equals \((a_M / a_j)^{\mu}\).

Proof. The constants in the conclusion depend on \(A, d, c_0, \theta, \delta\) but not on \(s\) or \(Z\). The choices give \(0 < \mu \leqslant c_0\), \(\mu \leqslant \theta / 2\), and hence \(\lambda = \theta - \mu \geqslant \theta / 2 > 0\). Thus \(0 < q < 1\) and \(C \geqslant A\). Introduce the tilted errors

\(\displaystyle F_j = e^{\mu (T - t_j)} f_j, \qquad G_j = e^{\mu (T - t_j)} g_j .\)

Since \(t_j \leqslant T\) and \(\mu \leqslant c_0\), the source bound in (3.17) implies

\(\displaystyle 0 \leqslant G_j \leqslant Ay_j^d . \) (3.20)

Multiplying the recurrence by \(e^{\mu (T - t_j)}\) gives

\(\displaystyle F_j \leqslant e^{- \lambda \Delta_j} F_{j + 1} + G_j . \) (3.21)

We first control the change in the polynomial weight. The triangle inequality gives

\(\displaystyle y_{j + 1} = R + Z + |t_{j + 1} - s| \leqslant y_j + \Delta_j .\)

Because \(y_j \geqslant R \geqslant 2 (d + 1) / \lambda\), we have \(d / y_j \leqslant \lambda / 2\). Using \(1 + x \leqslant e^x\) for \(x \geqslant 0\), we obtain

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} e^{- \lambda \Delta_j} y_{j + 1}^d & \leqslant y_j^d e^{- \lambda \Delta_j} \left( 1 + \frac{\Delta_j}{y_j} \right)^d\\ & \leqslant y_j^d e^{- (\lambda - d / y_j) \Delta_j}\\ & \leqslant y_j^d e^{- \lambda \Delta_j / 2} \leqslant qy_j^d . \end{aligned} \ \) (3.22)

For \(d = 0\), the same inequalities hold with the polynomial factor equal to one.

We now argue backward from \(M\). At the endpoint, \(F_M = f_M \leqslant Ay_M^d \leqslant Cy_M^d\). Suppose \(F_{j + 1} \leqslant Cy_{j + 1}^d\). Equations (3.21), (3.20), and (3.22) imply

\(\displaystyle F_j \leqslant Cqy_j^d + Ay_j^d = (Cq + A) y_j^d = Cy_j^d,\)

where the last equality follows from \(C = A / (1 - q)\). Induction proves this bound for every \(j\). Undoing the tilt gives

\(\displaystyle f_j = e^{- \mu (T - t_j)} F_j \leqslant Cy_j^d e^{- \mu (T - t_j)},\)

as required. Finally, \(e^{- (T - t_j)} = e^{- t_M + t_j} = a_M / a_j\), so the exponential factor is the stated scale ratio to the power \(\mu\).\(\Box\)

Remark 3.18. (Scope of the recurrence input) The proposition is a complete scalar argument once the nonnegative recurrence, its polynomially bounded tilted source, and its endpoint estimate are known. In the Yang–Mills construction, those data must be obtained from the scale-by-scale propagation of a neutral curvature representative for the difference of two quadratic RG precisions. Establishing that propagation requires the exact Schur-complement, locality, and weighted-kernel hypotheses of the RG step. This proposition supplies none of them. It therefore proves a power-rate consequence of that RG input, not the RG input itself or convergence of the public Wilson moments.

3.5Weighted strip bounds for the tower kernel

The atlas estimate in Subsection 3.2 needs a Laurent strip bound for the tower cross-kernel and for its first residue differences. The construction supplies these bounds from a stronger momentum-space estimate: each entry of the alias-dependent wire symbol is holomorphic and bounded by a summable polynomial weight in the alias. 3.5.1From an analytic cube to a weighted Fourier strip

For \(p = (p_1, \ldots, p_4) \in \mathbb{C}^4\), let

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{Q}_{\eta} = \{p : | \text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} p_a | < \eta, \quad | \text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} p_a | < \pi + \eta \text{ for all } a\} .\)

Suppose \(f\) is holomorphic on \(\mathcal{Q}_{\eta}\), \(|f (p) | \leqslant A\) there, and for every real \(p\) with \(|p_a | < \pi + \eta\) and integer vector \(n\) for which \(|p_a + 2 \pi n_a | < \pi + \eta\) in each coordinate, \(f (p + 2 \pi n) = f (p)\). Define \(u\) on the horizontal strip \(| \text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} p_a | < \eta\) by translating each real coordinate into \([- \pi, \pi]\) before evaluating \(f\). The overlap condition makes this definition independent of the representative on the cell boundaries. Moreover \(u\) is holomorphic: on each open cell it is a translate of \(f\); on a shared boundary the two holomorphic expressions agree on a real open box, hence agree on the complex overlap by applying the one-variable identity theorem successively in the four coordinates. The same argument at a meeting of several cells gives a single analytic continuation. The bound \(|u| \leqslant A\) follows from the bound on \(f\).

Let

\(\displaystyle \hat{u} (n) = (2 \pi)^{- 4} \int_{[- \pi, \pi]^4} u (x) e^{- in \cdot x} \hspace{0.17em} dx, \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}^4 .\)

For \(h, b > 0\) with \(h + b < \eta\), shifting the \(a\)th contour by \(- i (h + b) \text{sgn} (n_a)\) gives

\(\displaystyle | \hat{u} (n) | \leqslant Ae^{- (h + b) |n|_1} . \) (3.23)

To justify the shift, apply Cauchy's theorem on the rectangle with horizontal sides at imaginary parts \(0\) and \(- (h + b) \text{sgn} (n_a)\). The two vertical integrals cancel because both \(u\) and \(e^{- in_a p_a}\) are \(2 \pi\)-periodic in the real direction. Repeat in each coordinate; the integrand remains holomorphic and bounded throughout the product of the four rectangles. On the shifted contour the exponential factor has modulus \(e^{- (h + b) |n|_1}\), proving the estimate.

The geometric series is bounded explicitly. Since \(e^{- b} \leqslant (1 + b)^{- 1}\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} e^{- b |m|} = \frac{1 + e^{- b}}{1 - e^{- b}} \leqslant \frac{2 (1 + b)}{b} .\)

Taking products over the four coordinates and using (3.23) yields

\(\displaystyle \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}^4} e^{h |n|_1} | \hat{u} (n) | \leqslant A \left( \frac{2 (1 + b)}{b} \right)^4 . \) (3.24)

If real coefficients are needed, replace \(\hat{u} (n)\) by its real part; the estimate is unchanged. If in addition \(u (- x) = \overline{u (x)}\) for real \(x\), the Fourier coefficients are real, since conjugating the integral and changing \(x\) to \(- x\) gives \(\overline{\hat{u} (n)} = \hat{u} (n)\).

Lemma 3.19. (Uniform strip extraction) Let \(\mathcal{I}\) be any index set, \(w_i \geqslant 0\), and let \(f_i\) be a family of symbols satisfying the preceding hypotheses with common \(\eta > 0\) and \(|f_i | \leqslant Aw_i\). If \(0 < h, b\) and \(h + b < \eta\), then their real Fourier coefficient kernels \(K_i (n) = \text{Re} \hat{u}_i (n)\) satisfy

\(\displaystyle \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}^4} e^{h |n|_1} |K_i (n) | \leqslant A \left( \frac{2 (1 + b)}{b} \right)^4 w_i \quad (i \in \mathcal{I}) .\)

The width and the prefactor are independent of the index.

Proof. Apply (3.24) to \(f_i / w_i\) when \(w_i > 0\). When \(w_i = 0\), the bound forces \(f_i = 0\), so the result is immediate. Taking real parts can only decrease absolute values.\(\Box\)

3.5.2The finite-residue average and its scale powers

For a real cross-kernel \(K (r, i, j, z)\), the strip bound of width \(\sigma\) and cost \(A\) means

\(\displaystyle \sum_{z \in \mathbb{Z}^4} e^{\sigma |z|_1} |K (r, i, j, z) | \leqslant A \quad \text{for every fixed } r, i, j.\)

The estimate is per fixed pair of finite labels; summing over labels later contributes their cardinalities.

For an odd side \(L\), let \(\Lambda_L = (\mathbb{Z}/ L\mathbb{Z})^4\) and choose the centered lift \(\ell_L (r)\). Write the alias momentum as

3.6.1The one-dimensional alias estimate

Lemma 3.23. (Block average and box decay) There are constants \(B\) and \(\eta_0 > 0\) such that, for every odd \(L \geqslant 1\), every \(0 < \eta \leqslant \eta_0\), every \(p \in \mathcal{Q}_{\eta}\), and every centered residue \(l\),

\(\displaystyle | \text{avg}_L (q_l^{(L)} (p)_a) |, | \text{cavg}_L (q_l^{(L)} (p)_a) | \leqslant B (1 + | \ell_L (l)_a |)^{- 1} .\)

At the zero residue the reciprocal block average is also uniformly bounded on a common smaller complex cube.

Proof. If \(\ell_L (l)_a = 0\), the imaginary part of \(Lq_l^{(L)} (p)_a = p_a\) is bounded by \(\eta\), and the normalized finite geometric sum defining the block average has modulus at most \(\exp (\eta)\); multiplication by the centered phase defining the centered average costs at most another fixed exponential factor. If \(\ell_L (l)_a \ne 0\), the centered-lift estimate for aliases gives

\(\displaystyle (\pi / 4) (1 + | \ell_L (l)_a |) \leqslant L |q_l^{(L)} (p)_a | \leqslant C (1 + | \ell_L (l)_a |) .\)

The lower bound uses that \(| \text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} p_a | < \pi + \eta\) and the lift is a nonzero integer; the upper bound is the triangle inequality. On the fixed disk containing these momenta, the function \((e^{iz} - 1) / z\) is bounded above and bounded away from zero, with its value at zero defined by continuity. The identity

\(\displaystyle L \hspace{0.17em} \text{avg}_L (z) (e^{iz} - 1) = e^{iLz} - 1\)

then bounds the block average by a constant divided by \(L |e^{iz} - 1|\), hence by the stated inverse lift weight. The centered average differs from the block average by a centered phase whose modulus is bounded on the same complex cube. At the zero alias, for real \(p_a\) with \(|p_a | \leqslant R_0\), the same identity and the two-sided Fourier difference bound give \(| \text{avg}_L (p_a / L) | \geqslant \ell / h\): apply the lower bound to \(p_a\) and the upper bound to \(p_a / L\), with the value at zero obtained by continuity. The following quantitative continuation promotes this lower bound to a common complex cube, uniformly in the side. Suppose \(C, c, w, \eta, \theta > 0\) and a holomorphic function has modulus at most \(Cw\) on a cube of width \(\eta\) and modulus at least \(cw\) on its real slice of width \(\theta\). At a complex point \(p\), let \(y = \text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} p\). The one-variable Cauchy estimate along the complex line through \(y\) and \(p\) gives

\(\displaystyle |f (p) - f (y) | \leqslant \frac{4 Cw}{\eta} \hspace{0.17em} \|p - y\|_{\infty}\)

provided \(\|p - y\|_{\infty} \leqslant \eta / 4\) and the ball lies in the original cube. Indeed, for \(d = \|p - y\|_{\infty} > 0\) apply Cauchy's derivative bound to \(g (z) = f (y + z (p - y) / d)\) on the disk \(|z| < \eta / 2\), where \(|g| \leqslant Cw\). For \(0 \leqslant z \leqslant d\) the disk of radius \(\eta / 4\) around \(z\) remains inside that domain, so \(|g' (z) | \leqslant 4 Cw / \eta\); integrate from \(0\) to \(d\). The case \(d = 0\) is immediate. On the smaller cube of width \(r = \min (\eta / 4, \theta, c \eta / (8 C))\), the radius-\(\eta / 2\) ball about \(y\) lies in the original cube, and \(y\) lies in the stated real slice. Consequently one has \(|f (p) | \geqslant (c / 2) w\). In particular the reciprocal is holomorphic there and bounded by \(2 / (cw)\). Applying this with \(w = 1\) to the zero-alias block average proves the asserted uniform reciprocal bound.\(\Box\)

Lemma 3.24. (Principal centered average estimate with unit coefficient) If \(M\) and \(L\) are positive integers with \(M \leqslant L\), \(z\) is real, and \(L |z| \leqslant R_0\), then

\(\displaystyle | \text{cavg}_M (z) | \geqslant \exp (- c_0 M / L) .\)

Proof. The centered-sum formula is

\(\displaystyle \text{cavg}_M (z) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{v = 0}^{M - 1} e^{i (v - \lfloor M / 2 \rfloor) z} .\)

Each centered integer has modulus at most \(M\). The Fourier difference bound therefore gives \(| \text{cavg}_M (z) - 1| \leqslant hM |z| \leqslant hR_0 x\), where \(x = M / L\). The quotient formula for the block average and the two-sided Fourier difference bound also give \(| \text{cavg}_M (z) | \geqslant p_0\) because \(M |z| \leqslant R_0\) and \(|z| \leqslant R_0\). Consequently \(1 \leqslant | \text{cavg}_M (z) | + hR_0 x\) by the triangle inequality. Since \(e^{c_0 x} \geqslant 1 + c_0 x\) and \(| \text{cavg}_M (z) | / p_0 \geqslant 1\), it follows that \(e^{c_0 x} | \text{cavg}_M (z) | \geqslant | \text{cavg}_M (z) | + hR_0 x \geqslant 1\). Rearranging proves the claimed estimate. For the lower bound, the principal one-dimensional estimate with coefficient one states that if \(M \leqslant L_0\), \(z\) is real, and \(L_0 |z| \leqslant R_0\), then \(| \text{cavg}_M (z) | \geqslant e^{- c_0 M / L_0}\). Four coordinates give the same estimate with exponent multiplied by four. The same induction proves \(|v_{\text{extra}} (p / L_0) | \geqslant e^{- 8 c_0 M / L_0}\). For \(J = j\), the extra is unchanged and the mass increases. For \(J = j + 1\), the new box costs exponent \(4 c_0 LM / L_0\), and the old extra costs at most \((8 / 3) c_0 LM / L_0\); their sum is bounded by \(8 c_0 LM / L_0\). This is the geometric mass-ratio estimate that keeps the bound independent of the history length.

For \(M = 1\) the only alias is zero and the top kernel history has \(S_v = D_{v, a} = 1\). For \(M > 1\), the alias estimate for each centered average gives \(| \text{area}_M (q_l^{(M)} (p)) | \leqslant B^4 \Gamma_M (l)^{- 1}\), while the upper extra estimate at \(L_0 = M\) costs at most \(e^{8 \text{zone}}\). Consequently, for the fixed output stage \(j\), \(|S_v (q_l^{(M)} (p)) | \leqslant (B^4)^j e^{8 \text{zone}} \Gamma_M (l)^{- j}\). At zero alias the lower estimate with \(L_0 = M\) gives \(|S_v (p / M) | \geqslant \exp (- 4 j \hspace{0.17em} c_0 - 8 \hspace{0.17em} c_0)\). The zero-root block average is at least \(p_0\) and at most a uniform constant. Thus every directional filter and its two-sided directional filter product have uniform upper and positive lower bounds there. At any alias, the kernel history smooth upper bound is \(C \Gamma_M (l)^{- j}\) and the block average is bounded, so the filter has that same weight. The same bound holds at \(- q_l^{(M)} (p)\): for odd \(M\), \(- q_l^{(M)} (p) = q_{- l}^{(M)} (- p)\), the complex cube is invariant under \(p \mapsto - p\), and \(\Gamma_M (- l) = \Gamma_M (l)\). Thus a two-sided filter product has weight at most \(C^2 \Gamma_M (l)^{- 2 j}\).

The divisor \(d_M (p)\) satisfies, for real \(p\) in the fixed disk,

\(\displaystyle \text{low}^2 |p|^2 \leqslant d_M (p) \leqslant \text{high}^2 |p|^2 .\)

Indeed each seed magnitude lies between \(\text{low} |p_a |\) and \(\text{high} |p_a |\), and the divisor is the sum of their squares. The single-feed noise term uses the constant function one and equals

\(\displaystyle M^{- 2} \sum_{l \in \Lambda_M} D_{v, a} (q_l^{(M)} (p)) D_{v, a} (- q_l^{(M)} (p)) .\)

The alias filter bound gives a summand at most \(C \Gamma_M (l)^{- 2 j} \leqslant C \Gamma_M (l)^{- 2}\), whose sum is at most \(81 C\); the retained factor \(M^{- 2}\) therefore bounds the feed by \(81 CM^{- 2}\). By definition, the noise list is empty for a top kernel history and, for a hop of dilation \(L\), consists of one top kernel history followed by the hop applied to every entry of the previous list. Its total bound \(T\) obeys \(T_{\text{top}} = 0\) and \(T_{\text{hop}} = 1 + L^{- 2} T_{\text{old}}\). As \(L \geqslant 3\), induction gives \(T \leqslant 1 + 2 / 9 < 2\). Summing the feed estimates at these actual inverse-square side bounds controls the full accumulated noise uniformly.

The high-alias term is a finite sum of two-sided filter products times the reciprocal blow-up \([M^2 d_1 (q_l^{(M)} (p))]^{- 1}\). For nonzero \(l\), choose a coordinate attaining \(\max_a | \ell_M (l)_a |\). The centered-alias lower bound in that coordinate and the seed lower bound give \(M^2 d_1 (q_l^{(M)} (p)) \geqslant c \hspace{0.17em} g_M (l)^2\) on the real cube. Its complex upper bound has the same \(g_M (l)^2\) weight. The quantitative continuation argument above therefore bounds its reciprocal by \(Cg_M (l)^{- 2}\) on a common smaller cube. At \(M = 1\) the high-alias sum is empty. For \(M > 1\), the remaining filter product has weight \(\Gamma_M (l)^{- 2 j}\), so this sum is bounded by the exponent-two residue sum. Thus the full corrected alias sum is uniformly bounded. It is nonnegative on the real slice.

The regularized factor is the directional filter product plus \(d_M (p)\) times the corrected alias sum. The real lower bounds for the directional filter product and nonnegativity of both latter factors give a positive lower bound for the regularized factor; the complex upper bound follows from the already established bounds. The halo is \(S_v (p / M) S_v (- p / M)\) and has a positive real lower bound by the same cumulative-filter estimate. The exact scalar correction identity follows as follows: the block average identity gives \(J_a (p) h_v (p) = s_M (p)_a s_M (- p)_a \ell_{v, a} (p)\), where \(h_v = S_v (p / M) S_v (- p / M)\) and \(\ell_{v, a}\) is the directional filter product. Therefore \(d_M (p) / h_v (p) = \sum_a J_a (p) / \ell_{v, a} (p)\). Combining this with \(b_{v, a} = \ell_{v, a} + d_M G_{v, a}\) yields

\(\displaystyle d_M (p) \hspace{0.17em} \beta_v (p) = \sum_{a = 1}^4 \frac{J_a (p)}{b_v (p)_a}, \qquad J_a (p) = (e^{ip_a} - 1) (e^{- ip_a} - 1) .\)

Indeed, each summand on the right is \(J_a / \ell_{v, a} - d_M J_a G_{v, a} / (\ell_{v, a} b_{v, a})\), which is exactly the corresponding term in \(d_M \beta_v\). On the real cube, \(J_a \geqslant 0\), \(\sum_a J_a \geqslant \text{low}^2 |p|^2\), and \(d_M (p) \leqslant \text{high}^2 |p|^2\). If \(|b_v (p)_a | \leqslant C_b\), the identity gives \(\beta_v (p) \geqslant \text{low}^2 / (C_b \text{high}^2)\) for \(p \ne 0\); at zero \(\beta_v (0) = 1\). The scalar correction's complex-cube upper bound follows from its defining formula: first shrink the cube using the positive real lower bounds for the halo and directional filter product, so their reciprocals are bounded. Next the regularized factor has a positive real lower bound and a complex upper bound, so shrink again to bound its reciprocal. With these factors, the displayed scalar correction formula gives a complex upper bound. The real identity then gives a positive lower bound for the scalar correction; shrink once more to bound its reciprocal for the pulse and vault. This proves all assertions uniformly over kernel histories.\(\Box\)

Lemma 3.28. (Port denominator and levy weights) For every nonzero alias, the port vector has entrywise weight \(C_D g_L (l)^{- 2}\), the reciprocal port normalizer has weight \(C_R g_L (l)^{- 4}\), and the levy scalar is uniformly bounded when \(j \geqslant 4\).

Proof. The regularized factor and reciprocal scalar correction bounds are those of Lemma 3.27. The rescaled kernel is \(L^2 d_M (q_l^{(L)} (p))\), with side \(M\). Choose a coordinate \(a_{\ast}\) attaining \(\max_a | \ell_L (l)_a |\); since \(l \ne 0\), its lift is nonzero. The centered alias lower bound gives \(L |q_l^{(L)} (p)_{a_{\ast}} | \geqslant c (1 + | \ell_L (l)_{a_{\ast}} |) = cg_L (l)\) on the real cube. The seed lower bound \(|M \hspace{0.17em} \delta_{\text{F}} (q_l^{(L)} (p)_{a_{\ast}} / M) | \geqslant \text{low} |q_l^{(L)} (p)_{a_{\ast}} |\) then implies \(L^2 d_M (q_l^{(L)} (p)) \geqslant c' g_L (l)^2\). The analytic-cube upper bound for the blow-up has the same weight \(g_L (l)^2\), so the quantitative continuation argument gives its reciprocal at cost \(Cg_L (l)^{- 2}\) on a common smaller cube. The port vector is the bounded regularized factor times this reciprocal. The reciprocal port normalizer is the reciprocal of blow-up squared times the scalar correction; it therefore costs \(Cg_L (l)^{- 4}\). Finally, the levy contains two patch mass factors, of total weight \(\Gamma_L (l)^{- 2 j}\), times the bounded land scalar correction and this reciprocal port normalizer. Since both weights are at most one, the levy is uniformly bounded.\(\Box\)

3.6.3Uniform tails and regular zero-alias factors

Lemma 3.29. (Uniform tail and regular factors) The tail vector, the correction matrix, the notch matrix, and every zero-alias dual multiplier in the dual-stencil formula are bounded on a common complex cube, uniformly in \(L\) and the kernel history. The same is true of the regularized factor and scalar correction factors and their reciprocals wherever they occur in the wire formula.

Proof. Use the component and reciprocal bounds of Lemma 3.27; the nonzero port bounds are Lemma 3.28. For each nonzero alias, a tail summand is the product of two patch masses, two bounded block average factors, and a port vector. It is bounded by \(C \Gamma_L (l)^{- 2 j} g_L (l)^{- 2} \leqslant C \Gamma_L (l)^{- 2}\). Thus the sum is uniformly bounded, since

\(\displaystyle \sum_{l \in \Lambda_L} \Gamma_L (l)^{- 2} \leqslant \left( \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} (1 + |n|)^{- 2} \right)^4 \leqslant 81.\)

The extra constant \(k^{- 1}\) is finite. The divisor, scaled signals, and all zero-alias ratios in the correction and notch are bounded by the preceding kernel history lemma. Their formulas contain only finitely many sums and products of these quantities, so the triangle inequality and the finite-dimensional matrix product bound complete the uniform estimate.\(\Box\)

Lemma 3.30. (Uniform one-form transport bound) Fix \(k > 0\) and \(4 \leqslant t \leqslant j \leqslant t + 1\). Over the blocking steps of odd side \(L \geqslant 3\), the one-form column \(\mathcal{A}_l\) of Subsection 3.7 is holomorphic on one common complex cube, with each entry bounded by \(C \Gamma_L (l)^{- j}\). The constant and cube may depend on the fixed orders and penalty.

Proof. Use the notation \(s, b_a, b_{l, a}, m_l, a, h, B_l, g_{l, a}, x_l, \sigma\) of Subsection 3.7. In particular \(h\) is the sum over \(l \ne 0\) of \(m_l (p) m_{- l} (- p) \text{tr} \hspace{0.17em} D_l (p)\). In analytic expressions an adjoint on real momenta continues by reflection and transposition. Lemmas 3.28 and 3.29 bound each summand of \(h\) by \(C \Gamma_L (l)^{- 2 j} g_L (l)^{- 2}\). Hence \(h\) is uniformly bounded by the exponent-two residue sum. The regular denominator

\(\displaystyle sa = m_0 (p) m_0 (- p) \sum_b b_{0, b} + sh\)

is uniformly bounded on the common complex cube. On its real slice \(s \geqslant 0\) and \(h \geqslant 0\). The zero-alias mass and the fine regularized factors are uniformly bounded away from zero there by the component bounds, so the first term gives \(sa \geqslant c > 0\), also at \(p = 0\). The uniform continuation argument of the kernel history component bounds therefore bounds \((sa)^{- 1}\) on a smaller common cube.

For \(l \ne 0\), the leading matrix \(B_l = sD_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} \text{diag} (b^{- 1})\) has weight \(\Gamma_L (l)^{- j} g_L (l)^{- 2}\) by the port, flux and reciprocal regularized factor bounds. The two summands of \(g_{l, a}\) have this same weight: each contains a reflected patch mass and a port, and the remaining denominators and block average factors are uniformly bounded after inversion where needed. The vector \(x_l\) has weight \(g_L (l)\) and the row \(\sigma\) has weight one by the signal, scalar correction and regularized factor bounds. Thus the correction \(\text{diag} (g_{l, a}) x_l \sigma\) has weight at most \(\Gamma_L (l)^{- j}\), since \(g_L (l) \geqslant 1\). For \(l = 0\) the quotient \(sD_0\) is the diagonal of fine regularized factors, so \(B_0\) is bounded. The principal gain formula uses only the bounded tail and \(h\), and the reciprocals of \(m_0\), \(b_a\) and \(sa\) on the smaller cube. The principal signal and \(\sigma\) are bounded as well. These estimates prove the bound on both branches.\(\Box\)

3.6.4Closing the two wire branches

Proposition 3.31. (Entrywise weighted wire bound) For fixed stages \(j, t \geqslant 4\) and \(k > 0\), each scalar entry of the dual stencil is holomorphic on one common complex cube and satisfies

\(\displaystyle |W_{P, 0} (p) | \leqslant C_0, \qquad |W_{P, l} (p) | \leqslant C_1 \Gamma_L (l)^{- j} \quad (l \ne 0),\)

uniformly over all wing kernel histories and odd \(L \geqslant 3\). Hence the unified bound is \(C \Gamma_L (l)^{- j}\).

Proof. Use the zero and nonzero branch formulas in Subsection 3.5. In the zero branch, Lemma 3.25 and Lemma 3.29 show that every factor has weight one. In the first nonzero branch product, the scaled signal, port vector, and flux contribute respectively \(g_L (l)\), \(g_L (l)^{- 2}\), and \(\Gamma_L (l)^{- j}\). Their product is at most \(\Gamma_L (l)^{- j}\). The second nonzero product has levy weight one and flux weight \(\Gamma_L (l)^{- j}\). Finite intermediate matrix sums multiply the constant by only the fixed dimension of the face spaces. The branch constants and widths can be replaced by their maximum and minimum, respectively. This proves the assertion.\(\Box\)

3.7The exact identities of the original transport kernels

We now identify the kernels estimated in the preceding sections with the transports used in the quadratic construction. This also accounts for the harmonic mode. Throughout this section \(k > 0\), the block side \(L\) is odd and at least three, and the adjacent kernel history orders \(j, t\) satisfy \(4 \leqslant t \leqslant j \leqslant t + 1\).

3.7.1Covariance recursion and exterior algebra

Fix a nonzero real coarse momentum \(p\) in the common real cube containing the Brillouin cube, and write \(q_l = (p + 2 \pi \ell_L (l)) / L\) for its centered aliases. In the following formulas the fine exterior symbol is rescaled: \(\delta_r = Ld_r (q_l)\), whereas \(d_r = d_r (p)\) is the coarse symbol. Stars denote Hermitian adjoints at these real momenta. Let \(H_l, H\) be the fine and coarse two-form pulse symbols. Let \(D_l\) be the diagonal matrix with entries \(L^{- 2} \mathscr{D}_{f, a} (q_l)\), and \(D\) the diagonal matrix with entries \(\mathscr{D}_{c, a} (p)\), where \(\mathscr{D}_{v, a}\) is the propagated one-form covariance from Subsection 3.6. Set

\(\displaystyle x = d_0, \quad x_l = \delta_0, \quad R = d_1^{\ast} Hd_1, \quad R_l = \delta_1^{\ast} H_l \delta_1 .\)

All diagonal entries of \(D, D_l\) are positive. Put

\(\displaystyle \rho = x^{\ast} D^{- 1} x, \quad \pi = \rho^{- 1} x^{\ast} D^{- 1}, \quad c = \rho^{- 1} D^{- 1} x.\)

The corresponding fine objects have subscript \(l\). The pulse formula is \(H = \rho^{- 1} \bigwedge^2 D^{- 1}\); with the stated scaling, \(H_l = \rho_l^{- 1} \bigwedge^2 D_l^{- 1}\) as well. Consequently

\(\displaystyle R = D^{- 1} - \rho^{- 1} D^{- 1} xx^{\ast} D^{- 1}, \qquad DR = I - x \pi, \qquad RD = I - cx^{\ast} .\) (3.31)

For completeness, if \(D^{- 1} = \text{diag} (f_a)\), the diagonal entry of \(d_1^{\ast} (\bigwedge^2 D^{- 1}) d_1\) at \(a\) is \(f_a \sum_{b \ne a} f_b |x_b |^2\), and its off-diagonal entry at \((a, b)\) is \(- f_a f_b x_a \overline{x_b}\). Division by \(\rho = \sum_b f_b |x_b |^2\) proves the first equality; multiplication by \(D\) proves the other two. In particular \(Rx = 0\) and \(x^{\ast} R = 0\).

Let \(m_l\) be the patch mass filter at \(q_l\) and define

\(\displaystyle F_{r, l} = m_l \bigwedge^r \text{diag} (\text{avg}_L (q_{l, a})) .\)

The geometric-series identity \(L \text{avg}_L (q_{l, a}) (e^{iq_{l, a}} - 1) = e^{ip_a} - 1\) gives

\(\displaystyle F_{r + 1, l} \delta_r = d_r F_{r, l} .\) (3.32)

The covariance recursion is

\(\displaystyle \sum_l F_{1, l} D_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} = D - k^{- 1} I.\) (3.33)

Here the sum is a sum over all \(L^4\) aliases, with no averaging factor. To verify the normalization and the recursion, the feed of a kernel history of total side \(M\) is

\(\displaystyle M^{- 2} \sum_{u \in \Lambda_M} | \text{filter}_{v, a} (q_u) |^2 f (q_u) .\)

On appending a block, its filter is the old filter times the new patch filter evaluated at the old rescaled momentum. Decomposing a residue modulo \(LM\) into its residues modulo \(L\) and \(M\) therefore turns this feed into the successive two feeds: the factors are \((LM)^{- 2} = L^{- 2} M^{- 2}\), and the summands agree by periodicity. The noise-prefix list of the new kernel history consists of its new unit prefix and the old prefixes with the block appended. Thus its noise feed is one plus the patch feed of the old noise. Applying these two facts to the free covariance and to each earlier independent noise gives \(\mathscr{D}_{c, a} = k^{- 1} + \text{slide}_a \mathscr{D}_{f, a}\). The diagonal entries of (3.33) are precisely this equality. All off-diagonal entries vanish.

3.7.2The two punctured transport formulas

Define scalars

\(\displaystyle a = \sum_l |m_l |^2 \text{tr} \hspace{0.17em} D_l, \qquad e_l = \overline{m_l} \text{tr} \hspace{0.17em} D_l / a, \qquad y_l = |m_l |^2 \rho / \rho_l .\)

The principal mass filter is nonzero on the real cube. Since each \(D_l\) is positive, its contribution shows \(a > 0\). In particular

\(\displaystyle \sum_l e_l m_l = 1.\) (3.34)

Define the rescaled columns

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} \mathcal{A}_l & = D_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} R + e_l x_l \pi, & & \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.35)} \\ \mathcal{T}_l & = H_l \delta_1 D_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} d_1^{\ast} + y_l F_{2, l}^{\ast} (I - C), & C & = Hd_1 Dd_1^{\ast} . \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.36)} \end{array}\)

The complement \(I - C\) is part of the two-form transport; it cannot be discarded merely because it vanishes on weighted exact forms. Indeed (3.31) and \(d_1 x = 0\) imply

\(\displaystyle RDd_1^{\ast} = d_1^{\ast}, \qquad d_1 DR = d_1, \qquad d_1^{\ast} (I - C) = 0, \qquad (I - C) Hd_1 = 0.\)

Also, the adjoint of (3.32) gives \(x_l^{\ast} F_{1, l}^{\ast} d_1^{\ast} = 0\). These identities prove

\(\displaystyle R_l \mathcal{A}_l = F_{1, l}^{\ast} R, \qquad \delta_1^{\ast} \mathcal{T}_l = F_{1, l}^{\ast} d_1^{\ast}, \qquad \mathcal{T}_l Hd_1 = H_l \delta_1 \mathcal{A}_l .\) (3.37)

Here are the cancellations explicitly. The second term in \(R_l \mathcal{A}_l\) vanishes because \(R_l x_l = 0\); in the first, \(R_l D_l = I - c_l x_l^{\ast}\) and \(x_l^{\ast} F_{1, l}^{\ast} R = 0\). For the second identity the first term is \(R_l D_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} d_1^{\ast} = F_{1, l}^{\ast} d_1^{\ast}\), while the second is \(y_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} d_1^{\ast} (I - C) = 0\). For the third identity the complement term vanishes by \((I - C) Hd_1 = 0\), and the remaining term is \(H_l \delta_1 D_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} R\). The other side equals this because \(\delta_1 x_l = 0\).

Finally (3.32) at degree zero, (3.34), and (3.33) give

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \sum_l F_{1, l} \mathcal{A}_l & = (D - k^{- 1} I) R + x \pi\\ & = I - k^{- 1} R. \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.38)} \end{array}\)

This is the identity that incorporates the new Gaussian noise.

3.7.3Removing the puncture without changing the kernels

We identify the analytic continuations to which the component bounds apply. In this continuation every real-momentum adjoint is written as transposition after reflection \(p \mapsto - p\); for example, \(|m (p) |^2\) continues as \(m (p) m (- p)\). For an alias-indexed factor the reflection is \(q_l \mapsto - q_l\), equivalently \((p, l) \mapsto (- p, - l)\). Thus none of the complex-momentum formulas below uses literal complex conjugation of its variable. Let \(s\) be the coarse divisor, \(b_a\) the coarse regularized factor and \(b_{l, a}\) the fine regularized factor. Write \(q_0 = p / L\) and \(u_a = \text{tail}_a\), including the current noise. Splitting the covariance recursion into its principal alias and the other aliases gives

\(\displaystyle b_a = |m_0 \text{avg}_L (q_{0, a}) |^2 b_{0, a} + su_a .\) (3.39)

For \(p \ne 0\) this follows by multiplying \(D_a = |m_0 \text{avg}_L (q_{0, a}) |^2 b_{0, a} / s + u_a\) by \(s\). Both sides of (3.39) are continuous, so it holds at zero as well.

For the one-form column put \(B_l = D_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} D^{- 1}\) and define \(r_{l, a}\) by \(B_l x = \text{diag} (r_{l, a}) x_l\). Entrywise,

\(\displaystyle r_{l, a} = \overline{m_l} (D_l)_{aa} | \text{avg}_L (q_{l, a}) |^2 / D_{aa} .\)

Using (3.31) in (3.35) gives

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{A}_l = B_l + \text{diag} (e_l - r_{l, a}) x_l \pi .\)

Now \(D^{- 1} = s \text{diag} (b_a^{- 1})\) and \(\pi = s^{- 1} \sigma\), where \(\sigma = (\text{scalar correction}_c)^{- 1} x^{\ast} \text{diag} (b_a^{- 1})\) is analytic. For a nonprincipal alias, \(D_l\) has the regular port formula; thus \(e_l - r_{l, a} = sg_{l, a}\) with

\(\displaystyle g_{l, a} = \overline{m_l} \left( \frac{\text{tr} \hspace{0.17em} D_l}{sa} - \frac{(D_l)_{aa} | \text{avg}_L (q_{l, a}) |^2}{b_a} \right) .\)

For the principal alias let \(h\) be the sum of the nonprincipal terms in \(a\). Then \(sa = |m_0 |^2 \sum_a b_{0, a} + sh\), and (3.39) instead gives the regular formula

\(\displaystyle g_{0, a} = m_0^{- 1} \left( \frac{u_a}{b_a} - \frac{h}{sa} \right) .\)

These are exactly the principal and nonprincipal gain formulas. The reciprocals of \(b_a\), \(sa\), and \(m_0\) are uniformly bounded on the common smaller cube of Lemma 3.30. Consequently

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{A}_l = B_l + \text{diag} (g_{l, a}) x_l \sigma\)

has the weighted bound of that lemma, including at the origin.

For the two-form column, at a nonprincipal alias write \(\lambda_l = |m_l |^2 \text{scalar correction}_c / \text{portR}_l\). Then \(y_l = s^2 \lambda_l\) and

\(\displaystyle s^2 (I - C) = s^2 I - sHd_1 \text{diag} (b_a) d_1^{\ast} .\)

Substitution in (3.36) is exactly the regular nonprincipal wire formula. At the principal alias set \(J = F_{2, 0}^{- 1}\), \(U = \text{diag} (u_a)\), \(M = \text{diag} (b_a)\), and \(z_a = u_a / b_a\). The inverse exists on the common cube. The exterior chain rule and the principal covariance split give

\(\displaystyle \delta_1 = Jd_1 F_{1, 0}, \qquad F_{1, 0} D_0 F_{1, 0}^{\ast} = D - U.\)

The cancellation needed for the apparent remaining pole is

\(\displaystyle s\mathcal{B}= H_0 - y_0 F_{2, 0}^{\ast} HF_{2, 0}, \qquad \mathcal{B}= H_0 \left( \text{diag}_{\{a, b\}} (z_a + z_b) - s \bigwedge^2 \text{diag} (z_a) \right) .\) (3.40)

To verify it, put \(Z_a = |m_0 \text{avg}_L (q_{0, a}) |^2 b_{0, a} / b_a = 1 - sz_a\) by (3.39). The pulse formula shows \(y_0 F_{2, 0}^{\ast} HF_{2, 0} = H_0 \bigwedge^2 \text{diag} (Z_a)\): on a face \(\{a, b\}\) both sides have the same product of its two diagonal entries and the same scalar correction ratio. Finally \(1 - (1 - sz_a) (1 - sz_b) = s (z_a + z_b - sz_a z_b)\) proves (3.40) entrywise.

Using \(M = sD\), the principal expression therefore becomes

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{T}_0 = y_0 F_{2, 0}^{\ast} + (\mathcal{B}Jd_1 M - H_0 Jd_1 U) d_1^{\ast} .\) (3.41)

Indeed its second term expands to \(H_0 Jd_1 (D - U) d_1^{\ast} - y_0 F_{2, 0}^{\ast} Hd_1 Dd_1^{\ast}\), which is (3.36) after the two chain identities. All factors in (3.41) have the analytic bounds already proved. This verifies agreement with the principal wire as well as the nonprincipal one.

At \(p = 0\) the principal analytic columns have values \(\mathcal{A}_0 = I\) and \(\mathcal{T}_0 = I\): the regularized factors, scalar corrections, rails, and principal filter have value one and the exterior symbols vanish. For \(l \ne 0\) the patch mass filter vanishes at \(q_l (0)\). Indeed some nonzero alias coordinate has a nontrivial \(L\)th root of unity, and the geometric-series rail vanishes there. The corresponding plank is a nonzero phase times that rail, and the patch mass is a positive power of the product of the four planks, so it vanishes. The nonprincipal one-form column is zero because both its leading term and gain contain that filter. The two-form column is zero because its first term contains \(d_1 (0)^{\ast} = 0\) and its other term contains the mass filter. Thus (3.37) and (3.38) also hold at \(p = 0\).

3.7.4Returning to physical counting measure

The installed one-form and two-form columns at alias \(l\) are \(L^{- 1} \mathcal{A}_l\) and \(L^{- 2} \mathcal{T}_l\). The block mean \(Q\) is the alias row with columns \(LF_{1, l}\). In physical counting measure the adjoint alias column of \(Q\) is \(L^{- 3} F_{1, l}^{\ast}\): the factor \(L\) is multiplied by the coarse-to-fine volume ratio \(L^{- 4}\). Since \(\delta_r = Ld_r (q_l)\), the second and third identities in (3.37) become respectively

\(\displaystyle d_{1, f}^{\ast} T = Q^{\ast} d_{1, c}^{\ast}, \qquad TH_c d_{1, c} = H_f d_{1, f} A_0 .\)

The alias sum becomes \(QA_0 = I - k^{- 1} d_{1, c}^{\ast} H_c d_{1, c}\). The first identity in (3.37) gives the primal identity as well: the fine rotor is \(L^{- 2} R_l\) in physical units, so its product with \(L^{- 1} \mathcal{A}_l\) is \(L^{- 3} F_{1, l}^{\ast} R\). On a torus of side \(n\) we use \(\hat{u} (p) = n^{- 4} \sum_x u (x) e^{- ip \cdot x}\), with the analogous normalization on the fine torus of side \(Ln\). In the periodization of a cover row the sum over each period fibre can be interchanged with the finite Fourier sum by absolute summability. At a grid momentum the exponential is constant on each fibre; the resulting cover Fourier series is therefore evaluated at that momentum. For a cross row the centered-residue decomposition groups the fine frequencies into the aliases \(q_l\) of each coarse \(p\). The reality calculation in Subsection 3.5 ensures that the real coefficient rows recover these symbols. Fourier inversion then gives the real matrix identities

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} d_{1, f}^{\textsf{T}} H_f d_{1, f} A_0 & = Q^{\textsf{T}} d_{1, c}^{\textsf{T}} H_c d_{1, c}, & & \\ T^{\textsf{T}} d_{1, f} & = d_{1, c} Q, & TH_c d_{1, c} & = H_f d_{1, f} A_0,\\ QA_0 & = I - k^{- 1} d_{1, c}^{\textsf{T}} H_c d_{1, c} . & & \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.42)} \end{array}\)

This argument includes every finite period, including the one-site period, and uses the Fourier coefficients of the original analytic columns. No new choice of transport has been made.

3.8Positive Laurent rows and the actual quadratic beds

The inverse-closed matrix algebras of Gröchenig and Leinert [GL06, Theorem 6 and Remark 1 following Corollary 7] offer a functional-calculus approach to localization for weights satisfying the Gelfand–Raikov–Shilov condition. A fixed exponential weight violates that condition. Here the positivity on the real torus permits us to pass to a narrower complex strip and estimate its width before taking Laurent coefficients.

The quadratic construction needs positive factors with exponentially decaying coefficients, uniformly in the finite period. Taking a separate matrix square root on each torus would not supply such coefficients. We construct one analytic symbol and then recover its Laurent row. This is also how the construction preserves its zero-frequency conventions.

3.8.1A uniform analytic inverse and square root

Let \(I\) be a fixed finite index set of cardinality \(r\). A real Laurent row is a collection \(A_{ij} (z)\), \(i, j \in I\), \(z \in \mathbb{Z}^4\). Suppose, for some \(\eta, C > 0\),

\(\displaystyle \sup_{i, j} \sum_{z \in \mathbb{Z}^4} e^{\eta |z|_1} |A_{ij} (z) | \leqslant C.\) (3.43)

Its symbol

\(\displaystyle \hat{A} (p) = \sum_z A (z) e^{ip \cdot z}\)

is periodic in each real coordinate and holomorphic whenever \(\max_a | \text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} p_a | < \eta\). If the index set is empty all the statements below are vacuous, so assume \(r \geqslant 1\). On every smaller strip of width \(\sigma\), termwise differentiation is justified by

\(\displaystyle |z|_1 e^{\sigma |z|_1} \leqslant (\eta - \sigma)^{- 1} e^{\eta |z|_1} .\)

Thus each entry has derivative norm at most \(C / (\eta - \sigma)\), for the maximum norm on momenta. This also proves local uniform convergence of the differentiated series. In operator norm the corresponding Lipschitz bound is at most \(rC / (\eta - \sigma)\), since a matrix with entries bounded by \(a\) has operator norm at most \(ra\).

Assume \(A_{ij} (- z) = A_{ji} (z)\), so that \(\hat{A} (p)\) is Hermitian at real momenta. Suppose in addition that for every real momentum \(p\),

\(\displaystyle vI \leqslant \hat{A} (p) \leqslant MI, \qquad 0 < v \leqslant M,\) (3.44)

as Hermitian forms. These bounds are uniform over the whole real torus. Let \(\sigma = \eta / 2\), \(D = rC / (\eta - \sigma)\), and choose

\(\displaystyle 0 < \tau \leqslant \min \{\sigma, v / (2 D)\} .\)

For a complex momentum in this strip, put \(q = \text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} p\). The segment joining \(q\) to \(p\) lies in the strip of width \(\sigma\), and therefore

\(\displaystyle \| \hat{A} (p) - \hat{A} (q)\| \leqslant v / 2.\)

Diagonalizing the Hermitian matrix at \(q\) gives

\(\displaystyle \|M^{- 1} \hat{A} (q) - I\| \leqslant 1 - v / M.\)

Hence throughout the smaller strip

\(\displaystyle \|M^{- 1} \hat{A} (p) - I\| \leqslant 1 - v / (2 M) = : \rho < 1.\) (3.45)

All constants depend only on the original uniform bounds and the fixed index set.

Put \(Z (p) = M^{- 1} \hat{A} (p) - I\). The normally convergent series

\(\displaystyle \hat{F} (p) = \sqrt{M} \sum_{n \geqslant 0} \binom{1 / 2}{n} Z (p)^n, \qquad \hat{B} (p) = M^{- 1} \sum_{n \geqslant 0} (- Z (p))^n\)

define holomorphic periodic symbols on this strip. Normal convergence follows from \(\rho < 1\) and absolute convergence of the scalar power series on every smaller closed disk. Cauchy multiplication gives \(\hat{B} \hat{A} = I\) and \(\hat{F}^2 = \hat{A}\). On the real torus diagonalization gives eigenvalues \(\lambda^{- 1}\) and \(\sqrt{\lambda}\), respectively, where \(v \leqslant \lambda \leqslant M\). Consequently

\(\displaystyle M^{- 1} I \leqslant \hat{B} \leqslant v^{- 1} I, \qquad \sqrt{v} I \leqslant \hat{F} \leqslant \sqrt{M} I.\) (3.46)

In particular these are the positive inverse and positive square root, with no choice of eigenvectors as the momentum varies.

We verify that they are symbols of real exponentially summable rows. Either symbol is bounded on a still smaller strip, say of width \(\tau_0 > 0\), by a constant \(K\). Its Fourier coefficient is the integral over \([- \pi, \pi]^4\) against \(e^{- ip \cdot z}\), divided by \((2 \pi)^4\). Shift the \(a\)th contour to imaginary part \(- \tau_1 \text{sign} (z_a)\), where \(0 < \tau_1 < \tau_0\). Cauchy's theorem applies one coordinate at a time; the vertical sides cancel by periodicity. The coefficient is therefore bounded by \(Ke^{- \tau_1 |z|_1}\). For \(0 < e < \tau_1\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_z e^{e |z|_1} |F_{ij} (z) | \leqslant K \left( \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} e^{- (\tau_1 - e) |n|} \right)^4 < \infty,\)

and likewise for \(B\). The original real row satisfies \(\hat{A} (- p) = \overline{\hat{A} (p)}\) on real momenta. Both power series have real coefficients, so their symbols have the same symmetry. Changing \(p\) to \(- p\) in the Fourier integral shows that every recovered coefficient is real. Absolute Fourier convergence identifies the recovered symbols with the displayed analytic functions on the real torus.

On a finite torus, periodize these same rows. The discrete Fourier transform diagonalizes their convolution matrices, with symbols evaluated at the finite Fourier frequencies. Thus on every period \(n\), simultaneously,

\(\displaystyle B_n A_n = I, \qquad F_n^{\textsf{T}} = F_n, \qquad F_n^{\textsf{T}} F_n = A_n, \qquad \sqrt{v} I \leqslant F_n \leqslant \sqrt{M} I.\) (3.47)

The same transform transfers the inverse bounds in (3.46). This includes the zero frequency and the one-site torus. The construction has used a fixed number of form labels; no bound involving the growing number of lattice sites has entered the analytic inverse or root estimate.

3.8.2The amendment of the two-form precision

Fix a smoothing stage \(j \geqslant 4\) and the positive kernel parameter \(k\). For every finite kernel history at this stage, let \(H\) and \(B\) be the pulse and dual-pulse rows constructed in Sections 3.5 and 3.6. Their real symbols are diagonal, positive and reciprocal. Their uniform strip bounds therefore also give uniform positive lower bounds: if a diagonal entry of each row is at most \(C\) and their product is one, each is at least \(C^{- 1}\). The preceding Fourier argument yields \(HB = BH = I\) on every period, with uniform spectral bounds.

Let \(d_1, d_2\) be the exterior differences from one-forms to two-forms and from two-forms to three-forms; thus \(d_2 d_1 = 0\). For a fixed amendment coefficient \(l \geqslant 0\), define

\(\displaystyle X = d_2 B, \qquad A = B + lX^{\textsf{T}} X, \qquad G = A^{- 1}, \qquad R = I + ld_2 Bd_2^{\textsf{T}}, \qquad D = R^{- 1} .\) (3.48)

These formulas denote compositions of Laurent rows and hence also their matrices on every finite period. Products and sums preserve exponential summability, after taking a common strip width. On real frequencies \(A \geqslant B > 0\) and \(R \geqslant I\), because \(B > 0\). The analytic inverse construction supplies \(G, D\) with uniform strip and spectral bounds. The constants may depend on \(j, k, l\), but are independent of the kernel history and finite period.

Two identities explain the choice of this amendment. First, \(XHd_1 = d_2 d_1 = 0\), so

\(\displaystyle AHd_1 = d_1, \qquad Gd_1 = Hd_1 .\)

Thus the potential precision is unchanged. Second, expansion of (3.48) gives \(RX = d_2 A\), since \(B\) is symmetric. Multiplication by \(D\) on the left and \(G\) on the right therefore gives

\(\displaystyle XG = Dd_2 .\) (3.49)

Both \(G\) and \(D\) are symmetric. At zero momentum \(d_2 = 0\) and \(B = I\), so \(G (0) = I\); the harmonic convention is retained.

Choose a uniform lower spectral bound \(b > 0\) for \(G\), and allow any direct precision \(0 < p \leqslant b / 2\). Then \(G - pI \geqslant (b / 2) I\). It has a uniform strip bound, including the subtracted constant row, so the analytic square-root construction gives a row \(F\) with

\(\displaystyle F_n \geqslant 0, \qquad F_n^{\textsf{T}} F_n = G_n - pI\)

for every finite period, with uniform strip bounds. The full root matrix is

\(\displaystyle J_p = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \sqrt{p} I\\ F_n \end{array} \right], \qquad J_p^{\textsf{T}} J_p = G_n .\)

Together with \(H, B, G, A\) this is the actual quadratic bed. Its inverse, symmetry, positivity and exterior-intertwining properties have all been verified above. In the top kernel history the pulse \(H\) is the identity, so the bed's unamended diagonal row is the identity as required. This does not assert \(G = I\): the amended metric is \((I + ld_2^{\textsf{T}} d_2)^{- 1}\) in that kernel history, and only its action on exact curvatures and its zero-frequency value agree with the identity. The admissible upper bound for \(p\) and all row bounds are selected before any lattice volume or kernel history is chosen.

3.9Construction of the finite precision tower

This section explains the finite-dimensional algebra that turns the selected quadratic beds and their curvature factor into an exact Wilson tower. It also records how the beds and towers are produced on the actual schedule. All matrices act on finite real torus spaces; there is no limiting or spectral argument in the tower identities.

3.9.1The exact finite-torus block identity

Let \(\mathcal{E}_f^r\) and \(\mathcal{E}_c^r\) be the real coordinate spaces of r-form labels on the fine and coarse tori. The first coboundaries are \(D_f : \mathcal{E}_f^1 \to \mathcal{E}_f^2\) and \(D_c : \mathcal{E}_c^1 \to \mathcal{E}_c^2\); the next coarse coboundary is \(d_2 : \mathcal{E}_c^2 \to \mathcal{E}_c^3\), with \(d_2 D_c = 0\). Let \(J : \mathcal{E}_f^2 \to \mathcal{Y}_f\) and \(J' : \mathcal{E}_c^2 \to \mathcal{Y}_c\) be the full precision-factor maps for the fine and coarse beds, respectively, and write

\(\displaystyle W = J^{\textsf{T}} J, \qquad W' {= J'}^{\textsf{T}} J', \qquad R = W^{- 1}, \qquad R' = (W')^{- 1} .\)

Assume \(W, W'\) are symmetric and the displayed inverses exist. Let \(T\) be the installed two-form transport \(T : \mathcal{E}_c^2 \to \mathcal{E}_f^2\), and let \(A_0 : \mathcal{E}_c^1 \to \mathcal{E}_f^1\) be the selected one-form transport. The conditional mean \(\bar{T} : \mathcal{E}_f^1 \to \mathcal{E}_c^1\) and these transports satisfy

\(\displaystyle T^{\textsf{T}} D_f = D_c \bar{T}, \qquad TW' D_c = WD_f A_0, \) (3.50)

where \(W\) and \(R\) act on \(\mathcal{E}_f^2\), while \(W'\) and \(R'\) act on \(\mathcal{E}_c^2\). Put \(s = \sqrt{k}\), with \(k > 0\).

Define the curvature remainder by

\(\displaystyle Z = R' - T^{\textsf{T}} RT - k^{- 1} D_c D_c^{\textsf{T}} . \) (3.51)

Suppose this remainder has the curvature factorization

\(\displaystyle W' ZW' = d_2^{\textsf{T}} P^{\textsf{T}} Pd_2 . \) (3.52)

Let \(P : \mathcal{E}_c^3 \to \mathcal{E}_c^3\) be a real matrix with the curvature factorization above. Define the coarse projection and its curvature image by

\(\displaystyle \Pi = J' R' {J'}^{\textsf{T}}, \qquad X = Pd_2 R' {J'}^{\textsf{T}} .\)

The three blocks in the tower are

\(\displaystyle M_F {= JRTJ'}^{\textsf{T}}, \qquad M_N = s^{- 1} D_c^{\textsf{T}} {J'}^{\textsf{T}}, \qquad M_O = \left[ \begin{array}{c} I - \Pi\\ X \end{array} \right] . \) (3.53)

Proposition 3.32. (Exact block completion) Assume the inverses and factorization above exist and the two transport identities (3.50) hold. Then the blocks in (3.53) satisfy

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} M_F^{\textsf{T}} M_F + M_N^{\textsf{T}} M_N + M_O^{\textsf{T}} M_O & = I, \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.54)} \\ sM_N^{\textsf{T}} & = J' D_c, \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.55)} \\ M_F J' D_c & = JD_f A_0, \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.56)} \\ M_N J' D_c & = s^{- 1} D_c^{\textsf{T}} W' D_c, \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.57)} \\ M_O J' D_c & = 0, \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.58)} \\ M_F^{\textsf{T}} JD_f + M_N^{\textsf{T}} (- s \hspace{0.17em} \bar{T}) & = 0. \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.59)} \end{array}\)

The three blocks have common domain \(\mathcal{Y}_c\) and respective ranges \(\mathcal{Y}_f\), \(\mathcal{E}_c^1\), and \(\mathcal{Y}_c \oplus \mathcal{E}_c^3\).

Proof. The inverse of a symmetric invertible real matrix is symmetric. Indeed, from \(WR = I\) and \(W^{\textsf{T}} = W\) we obtain \(R^{\textsf{T}} W = I\), hence \(R^{\textsf{T}} = R^{\textsf{T}} (WR) = (R^{\textsf{T}} W) R = R\); the same argument applies to \(R'\).

The matrix \(\Pi\) is the orthogonal projection onto the range of \(J'\). First,

\(\displaystyle \Pi^{\textsf{T}} = J' R' {J'}^{\textsf{T}} = \Pi .\)

Second,

\(\displaystyle \Pi^2 = J' R' {(J'}^{\textsf{T}} J') R' {J'}^{\textsf{T}} = J' R' W' R' {J'}^{\textsf{T}} = \Pi, \qquad \Pi J' = J' R' W' = J' .\)

For the curvature block, substitute (3.52):

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} X^{\textsf{T}} X & = J' R' d_2^{\textsf{T}} P^{\textsf{T}} Pd_2 R' {J'}^{\textsf{T}}\\ & = J' R' (W' ZW') R' {J'}^{\textsf{T}} = J' {ZJ'}^{\textsf{T}} . \end{array}\)

The two middle factors cancel because \(R' W' = W' R' = I\). The other two Gram terms are

\(\displaystyle M_F^{\textsf{T}} M_F = J' T^{\textsf{T}} {RTJ'}^{\textsf{T}}, \qquad M_N^{\textsf{T}} M_N = J' (k^{- 1} D_c D_c^{\textsf{T}}) {J'}^{\textsf{T}} .\)

By (3.51), \(T^{\textsf{T}} RT + k^{- 1} D_c D_c^{\textsf{T}} + Z = R'\). Consequently the sum of the three block Gram matrices is

\(\displaystyle J' R' {J'}^{\textsf{T}} + (I - \Pi)^{\textsf{T}} (I - \Pi) = \Pi + I - \Pi = I,\)

using \(\Pi^{\textsf{T}} = \Pi\) and \(\Pi^2 = \Pi\). This proves (3.54) as an identity on the full coarse augmented row space \(\mathcal{Y}_c\); no restriction to the range of \(J'\) is made.

The remaining formulas are direct consequences of the block definitions and (3.50). Since \(s^2 = k\), \(sM_N^{\textsf{T}} = J' D_c\). For the fine transport,

\(\displaystyle M_F J' D_c = JRT {(J'}^{\textsf{T}} J') D_c = JR (TW' D_c) = JR (WD_f A_0) = JD_f A_0 .\)

For the normal block,

\(\displaystyle M_N J' D_c = s^{- 1} D_c^{\textsf{T}} W' D_c .\)

For the image block, its first component is \((I - \Pi) J' D_c = 0\), since \(\Pi J' = J'\). Its second component is

\(\displaystyle XJ' D_c = Pd_2 R' {J'}^{\textsf{T}} J' D_c = Pd_2 R' W' D_c = Pd_2 D_c = 0,\)

using \(R' W' = I\) and \(d_2 D_c = 0\). This proves (3.58).

Finally, the transpose of \(M_F\) gives

\(\displaystyle M_F^{\textsf{T}} JD_f = J' T^{\textsf{T}} R (J^{\textsf{T}} J) D_f = J' T^{\textsf{T}} D_f .\)

By \(T^{\textsf{T}} D_f = D_c \bar{T}\), this is \(J' D_c \bar{T}\). Meanwhile \(M_N^{\textsf{T}} (s \bar{T}) = J' D_c \bar{T}\). The two terms therefore cancel with the sign in (3.59). This proves the orthogonality identity.\(\Box\)

3.9.2The selected schedule, its beds, and the tower estimates

The schedule input is a family of priced bed and blocking step receipts. At each integer stage \(j \geqslant 4\), for every allowed direct precision \(p_{\text{direct}} \leqslant \gamma_j\) and every kernel history of order \(j\), the bottom receipt supplies a bed whose four precision rows are fixed as above, whose five rows have the stated weighted strip costs, whose floors hold on every torus side, and whose real precision is sworn positive. It also makes the top bed's diagonal the identity. For every admissible adjacent pair of orders \(j, t\) (with \(t = j\) or \(t + 1 = j\)), the blocking step receipt supplies donated unmodified primal/two-form transports with the order-\(j - 2\) difference bounds, together with a curvature factor of the stated strip cost and positive lower quadratic bound. The block-length credit requires the separately selected lower bound on \(L\) before its quadratic floor is used. These receipts are obtained by the one-step kernel estimate and the uniform bed pricing; they are construction inputs here, not conclusions of the block algebra.

Fix the actual level schedule \(A\) and a terminal cutoff \(N\). Its kernel history at level \(N\) is the constant top kernel history. Descending one level, use the blocking step whose input is the kernel history already constructed at level \(i + 1\) and whose output is the kernel history at level \(i\). Thus, for every \(i < N\), the level-\(i\) kernel history is the output kernel history of the blocking step whose input is the already defined kernel history at level \(i + 1\). This uses the actual adjacent block at level \(i\); no second schedule is selected.

The bed at each \((N, i)\) is first constructed for the corresponding kernel history. Its four precision rows are the kernel history's pulse, dual pulse, amended precision and amended inverse, and its remaining root row is a square factor of the positive remainder (Subsection 3.8). The finite-volume root matrix is the vertical stack

\(\displaystyle J_p = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \sqrt{p_{\text{direct}}} I\\ F \end{array} \right], \qquad J_p^{\textsf{T}} J_p = p_{\text{direct}} I + F^{\textsf{T}} F.\)

This identity follows by splitting the row index of the product into its two disjoint blocks. The lower root factor is obtained from the finite matrix square-factor receipt, so the equality holds at every torus side and includes the zero modes. The floor receipt then gives symmetry, inverse identities and the uniform quadratic form bounds required of a bed.

For a step \(i < N\), the blocking step's donated primal and two-form transports are the transports \(A_0\) and \(T\) of its tower. The tangent identities are first obtained for the unamended pulses. On both lattices the amended metric agrees with its pulse on exact curvatures, \(Gd_1 = Hd_1\) (Subsection 3.8). Thus the tangent identities give

\(\displaystyle T^{\textsf{T}} D_f = D_c \bar{T}, \qquad TW' D_c = WD_f A_0,\)

where \(W, W'\) are the actual adjacent bed metrics and the overline is the blocking step's prescribed conditional mean. The blocking step's rebase receipt, followed by the positive square root of Subsection 3.10, supplies the curvature factorization of the remainder \(Z\) in (3.51). Therefore the preceding block proposition applies to these actual adjacent beds, not just to an abstract pair of positive matrices.

The completed tower also has explicit strip costs, uniform in \(L\). We verify them by finite matrix bookkeeping. Let \(\alpha\) be the minimum of the two bed widths (the fine bed width is also the donated width) and the curvature-factor width; write \(\Omega, \Omega'\) for the two bed costs, \(V\) for the curvature factor cost, \(L\) for the odd block side, \(X\) for the maximum difference order, \(k > 0\) for the penalty, and \(n_r = | \text{TypeFace} (r) |\) for the number of \(r\)-form labels. Put

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlllllll} A & = \max \{\| \sqrt{p_{\text{direct}}} \|, \Omega\}, & B & = \max \{\| \sqrt{q_{\text{direct}}} \|, \Omega' \}, & & & & \\ C_A & = n_2 A \Omega, & C_B & = n_2 B \Omega', & C_L & = n_2 C_A \Omega, & C_F & = n_2 C_L B,\\ C_N & = k^{- 1 / 2} n_2 D_1 B, & C_{\text{proj}} & = n_2 C_B B, & C_{\text{up}} & = n_3 VD_2, & C_X & = n_2 C_{\text{up}} \Omega',\\ C_O & = \max \{1 + C_{\text{proj}}, n_2 C_X B\}, & M_0 & = \max \{\Omega, C_F, C_N, C_O \}, & & & & \end{array}\)

where

\(\displaystyle D_r = \sum_{a \in \text{TypeFace} (r + 1)} \sum_{b \in \text{TypeFace} (r)} \sum_{z \in \text{supp} (\delta_r)} e^{\alpha |z|_1} \|(\delta_r)_{ab} (z)\|, \qquad r = 1, 2,\)

is the finite stencil cost of the \(r\)th coboundary, where for a cover displacement \(z\) we set \(|z|_1 = \sum_{j = 1}^4 |z_j |\). For ordinary cover convolution, the basic weighted row bound can be checked entrywise. If \(C_{ij} = \sum_b A_{ib} \ast B_{bj}\) is the routed cover convolution and the weighted absolute sums of each entry of \(A\) and \(B\) are at most \(a_{ib}\) and \(b_{bj}\), then

\(\displaystyle \sum_z e^{\alpha |z|_1} |C_{ij} (z) | \leqslant \sum_b a_{ib} b_{bj} .\)

Indeed, write each convolution coefficient as a sum over \(x + y = z\), apply the triangle inequality, use \(|x + y|_1 \leqslant |x|_1 + |y|_1\), and factor the two nonnegative sums over \(x\) and \(y\). Summing the intermediate label \(b\) then gives the finite cardinality factor in the strip estimate. Each \(n_2\) factor below is this count for a two-form intermediate label; \(n_3\) counts the three-form labels in the curvature factor. A finite coboundary stencil contributes \(D_r\). The fine-to-coarse composition uses a mixed version of this estimate. Fix a fine residue \(r\) and a fine cover displacement \(a\). There is a unique coarse displacement \(\kappa (r, a)\) and a new fine residue \(r' = \text{wrap} (\text{lift} \hspace{0.17em} r + a)\) such that

\(\displaystyle \text{lift} \hspace{0.17em} r + a = \text{lift} \hspace{0.17em} r' + L \kappa (r, a) .\)

Coordinatewise, put \(u = \text{lift} \hspace{0.17em} r\), \(v = \text{lift} \hspace{0.17em} r'\), and \(n = L\). Both representatives lie in the centered interval, so \(|u - v| \leqslant n - 1\). The displayed identity yields \(n | \kappa_i | \leqslant |a_i | + n - 1\). If \(| \kappa_i | > |a_i |\), integrality gives \(| \kappa_i | \geqslant |a_i | + 1\), hence \(n | \kappa_i | \geqslant n|a_i | + n \geqslant |a_i | + n\), a contradiction. Thus \(| \kappa (r, a) |_1 \leqslant |a|_1\). In the mixed composition, each \(a\) selects exactly the single residue \(r'\) above, and its coarse shift is \(\kappa (r, a)\). Apply the routed-convolution estimate at this fixed residue; summing the finite intermediate form label costs \(n_2\), with no sum over all possible residues. This is the \(C_L = n_2 C_A \Omega\) bound, uniform in \(L\).

The fine factor \(JR\) has cost \(C_A\). Its mixed composition with \(T\) has cost \(C_L\), and the final composition with \({J'}^{\textsf{T}}\) has cost \(C_F\); the normal block has cost \(C_N\); the projection and curvature rows have costs \(C_{\text{proj}}, C_X\), respectively. Stacking the projection complement and curvature rows gives \(C_O\). These are precisely the products in the displayed formulas; the explicit factor \(L^{- 2}\) in the donated two-form strip is retained separately. The resulting tower has

\(\displaystyle M_N, M_O \text{ strip-cost at most } M_0, \qquad M_F \text{ strip-cost at most } M_0 L^{- 2},\)

and its \(r\)th iterated primal and two-form differences have costs at most \(M_0 L^{- (1 + r)}\) and \(M_0 L^{- (2 + r)}\) for \(0 \leqslant r \leqslant X\). These latter bounds are inherited unchanged from the donated tower: the block construction retains its \(A_0, T\) rows, whose input receipts already include every iterated difference bound through order \(X\).

For completeness, the construction has two distinct stages. First, the schedule supplies a bed for every \((N, i)\) and the recursively defined blocking step supplies its one-step data. The bed and hop receipts together with the exact block proposition above give a horizon with row, floor, crossing, and roll data. Second, the finite tower-bound construction narrows widths and increases costs, while retaining the original \(A_0, T\) rows; the new rows \(M_F, M_N, M_O\) are the block completion just proved. The construction theorem performs this on the actual level object and retains the same bed family and the actual primal and two-form transports. In particular its retained-horizon equality is equality of the bed families; its tower equality preserves the \(A_0, T\) rows used by the schedule.

The resulting bounds are indexed by the smoothing stage. Write \(F\) for the finite set of two-form labels, \(J_i\) for the stage at level \(i\), and \(\alpha_j > 0\), \(\Omega_j\) for the corresponding retained strip width and cost. For each level \(i\), \(\Omega_{J_i} \geqslant 0\): the covered bed bounds a nonnegative weighted row sum for a fixed face label, and the face-label set is nonempty. At a fixed step \(i < N\), the retained tower and its fine bed have width \(\alpha_{J_{i + 1}}\) and cost \(\Omega_{J_{i + 1}}\), while the coarse bed has width \(\alpha_{J_i}\) and cost \(\Omega_{J_i}\). For any \(0 \leqslant \sigma \leqslant \min (\alpha_{J_{i + 1}}, \alpha_{J_i})\), the response of these very beds and tower therefore has the order-two atlas of Theorem 3.7, with cost

\(\displaystyle |F|^2 e^{4 \sigma} \Omega_{J_{i + 1}}^2 \Omega_{J_i} .\)

The first tower difference exists at the required order because the admissible stages are at least four. For a fixed step these bounds are common to all terminal cutoffs \(N > i\); they need not be bounded by one constant over all stages. Periodization and its uniformity require the separate quotient-fibre argument and the same retained data.

The horizon therefore carries explicit numerical data at all \(N, i\) and on every finite torus side: the stage-indexed bounds the bed strip widths and costs and the floor constants (by the schedule input described at the beginning of this subsection), and the iterated tower costs (by the bookkeeping above); the one-step crossing and roll identities hold by Proposition 3.32. The top bed is the unit diagonal because the top kernel history is constant. These are exactly the data a horizon must carry; the bounds and floors are inputs of the schedule and do not follow from the block identity alone. In the public construction the level schedule and these receipts are attached to the preselected run, as stated next. The public horizon contract alone does not identify its transport rows with the particular recursively produced ones above. For the flat quadratic output, the completion-independence argument of Lemma 3.15 permits the auxiliary priced construction to be compared on the same blocking data; it does not identify the two horizons or their individual tower rows.

3.9.3Actual path data and the public binding

Path data enter through the selected history of that run. Fix a finite torus, a plaquette face \(b\), and a one-form variation \(a\). For a path slot with coefficient \(c_{bi}\), cover offset \(y_{bi}\), and plaquette label \(p_{bi}\), its physical face is the translate \(b + y_{bi}\) modulo the torus. Define the path linear response by the absolutely convergent sum \(\ell_{\text{path}} (b, a) = \sum_i c_{bi} \hspace{0.17em} d_1 a (b + y_{bi}, p_{bi})\). The attachment identity says that, after summing all path coefficients with fixed \((p, y)\), one obtains the corresponding cover entry of the root row. The weighted row bound implies absolute summability of the path coefficients: its exponential weight is at least one. It also implies absolute summability after multiplying by the bounded face curl of \(a\). Therefore we may first group the path sum by physical face, and then sum over the finite set of torus faces. Let \(W\) be the selected bed's metric on this torus, and let \(F_W\) be the actual roots block in its factorization, acting on periodic two-form fields. Periodizing the cover entries by the attachment identity gives, for each color component \(q\),

\(\displaystyle (\ell_{\text{path}} (b, a))_q = [F_W ((d_1 a)_q)] (b),\)

where \(F_W\) is the actual root matrix on the torus and \(d_1 a\) is the finite-torus coboundary. The right side is a finite matrix-vector product. This is the entrywise path-to-root identity used in the physical quadratic-form computation; the absolute bound justifies the only infinite regrouping.

The direct block in the full root is also needed. For two one-form variations \(a, a'\), the identity \(J_p^{\textsf{T}} J_p = W\) and the displayed path-to-root equality give, with the sum over physical plaquettes,

\(\displaystyle \langle d_1 a, Wd_1 a' \rangle = p_{\text{direct}} \langle d_1 a, d_1 a' \rangle + \sum_f \langle \ell_{\text{path}} (f, a), \ell_{\text{path}} (f, a') \rangle .\)

For colour-valued fields the inner products are taken in an orthonormal colour basis. This is a finite counting-measure identity; no lattice-volume normalization is inserted. It uses both the direct block and the attached root block of the same bed.

The public-run adapter carries its original levels and retained horizon into the native component construction; its flat quadratic receipt uses that very horizon. For that same run, the attachment identity holds for every cutoff \(N\), level \(i \leqslant N\), and period label \(p\). Indeed, the successful history retains at \((N, p, i)\) the path fixed by its underlying recipe. That recipe's path is the designated path of the retained bed family, and the bed construction attaches its root row to that designated path. These two path equalities give the attachment for the actual history; no independently selected family is substituted. The same run's planned path data supply weighted row, loop and higher-leg bounds at every such cutoff, level and period, with the width and bound of that level. This separate bound supplies absolute summability; it is not concluded from attachment alone.

The matrix identities prove the block calculation once its displayed inputs hold. The selected tower bounds, path attachment and absolute bounds come from the separate receipts just described; they are not inferred from the block calculation alone.

3.10The curvature factor and the retained horizon

The positive beds of Subsection 3.8 must be joined by one common family of transports. The remaining issue is the positivity of the curvature defect. The construction first writes that defect as a double exterior derivative, then adds a fixed positive amendment before taking its square root.

3.10.1An exponentially summable exterior lift

We give the row-level lifting argument, because a cycle on a single finite torus would not suffice. Write \(q_a (p) = e^{ip_a} - 1\) and \(d_r (p) = q (p) \wedge \cdot\) on \(r\)-forms. Let \(A (p)\) be the symbol of an exponentially summable row from \((r + 1)\)-forms to a fixed coordinate space, where \(r + 1 > 0\), and suppose \(A (p) d_r (p) = 0\) for every real \(p\).

For one coordinate \(a\), let \(A_0 (p) = A (p_1, \ldots, 0, \ldots, p_4)\). The difference \(A - A_0\) is divisible by \(q_a\) as a Laurent row. To see this coefficientwise, replace each monomial in that coordinate by

\(\displaystyle \frac{e^{inp_a} - 1}{e^{ip_a} - 1} = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \sum_{h = 0}^{n - 1} e^{ihp_a}, & n > 0,\\ 0, & n = 0,\\ - \sum_{h = n}^{- 1} e^{ihp_a}, & n < 0. \end{array} \right.\)

These are finite identities, including at \(p_a = 0\) by continuity. If the original row has weighted norm at width \(\eta\), the quotient has weighted norm at width \(\eta / 2\) bounded by \(2 / \eta\) times the original one. Each monomial produces \(|n|\) terms of no greater absolute displacement, and

\(\displaystyle |n| e^{(\eta / 2) |z|_1} \leqslant (2 / \eta) e^{\eta |z|_1} \quad (|n| \leqslant |z|_1) .\)

This proves both convergence and the claimed bound.

Let \(E_a\) denote exterior multiplication by the \(a\)th basis vector, and \(\iota_a = E_a^{\textsf{T}}\) the corresponding contraction. The exterior identity \(d_r \iota_a + \iota_a d_{r + 1} = q_a I\) follows on every coordinate face by distinguishing whether that face contains \(a\); the two possible orders of adjoining another coordinate have opposite signs. Set

\(\displaystyle R_a = \frac{A - A_0}{q_a} \hspace{0.17em} \iota_a, \qquad S_a = A_0 (I - E_a \iota_a) .\)

Then

\(\displaystyle R_a d_{r + 1} + S_a = A.\) (3.60)

For \(q_a \ne 0\), multiply this identity by \(q_a\) and use \(Ad_r = 0\) and \(A_0 d_r = q_a A_0 E_a\); the latter follows from the cycle identity at \(p_a = 0\). This proves it there, and continuity proves it also at \(q_a = 0\). Multiplication on the right by \(d_r\) shows that \(S_a\) remains a cycle. The row \(S_a\) has zero columns on faces containing \(a\), and on the other columns it simply evaluates \(p_a\) at zero.

Apply this operation successively in the four coordinate directions. Previously vanished columns remain zero. Every positive-degree face contains some coordinate, so the final residual is zero. Summing the four successive \(R_a\) gives a row \(\mathcal{L} (A)\) with

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{L} (A) d_{r + 1} = A.\)

There are only four operations. The width is reduced a fixed number of times and the cost is multiplied by a fixed constant; any scalar scale factor in the initial bound is retained. Transposition gives the left-handed lift. All these identities hold on every finite period by Fourier diagonalization. Conversely, an exponentially summable row identity valid on all finite periods holds for all real symbols: the finite Fourier frequencies are dense and the symbols are continuous.

We shall also need a two-sided version. Suppose a two-form row \(E\) satisfies \(Ed_1 = 0\) and \(d_1^{\textsf{T}} E = 0\) on every period. Let \(W\) be a left lift, so \(d_2^{\textsf{T}} W = E\), and put \(V = Wd_1\). Then \(d_2^{\textsf{T}} V = 0\). Lift once more on the left to obtain \(T\) with \(d_3^{\textsf{T}} T = V\). The row \(Td_0\) is annihilated on the left by \(d_3^{\textsf{T}}\). A further left lift would take values in five-forms, whose coordinate space is zero in dimension four. Hence \(Td_0 = 0\). The right lift therefore gives \(Xd_1 = T\). Consequently

\(\displaystyle W' = W - d_3^{\textsf{T}} X \quad \text{satisfies} \quad W' d_1 = 0, \qquad d_2^{\textsf{T}} W' = E.\)

A final right lift \(C\) with \(Cd_2 = W'\) gives

\(\displaystyle d_2^{\textsf{T}} Cd_2 = E.\) (3.61)

If \(E\) is symmetric, replace \(C\) by \((C + C^{\textsf{T}}) / 2\). The identity and exponential row bounds are preserved, and the new \(C\) is symmetric.

3.10.2The unamended defect and its scale factor

For one flight let \(H_c, B_c\) and \(H_f, B_f\) be the reciprocal pulse and dual-pulse rows on the coarse and fine lattices. Let \(T\) be its two-form transport and \(A_0\) its one-form transport. The conditional mean is denoted by \(Q\). The exact unamended transport identities are

\(\displaystyle T^{\textsf{T}} d_{1, f} = d_{1, c} Q, \qquad TH_c d_{1, c} = H_f d_{1, f} A_0, \qquad QA_0 = I - k^{- 1} d_{1, c}^{\textsf{T}} H_c d_{1, c} .\) (3.62)

These identities were proved for the original kernel in Subsection 3.7; the amendment does not alter them on exact forms, by (3.49) and \(Gd_1 = Hd_1\). Define

\(\displaystyle Z_0 = B_c - T^{\textsf{T}} B_f T - k^{- 1} d_{1, c} d_{1, c}^{\textsf{T}}, \qquad E = H_c Z_0 H_c .\)

The matrices are symmetric. Moreover

\(\displaystyle T^{\textsf{T}} B_f TH_c d_{1, c} = T^{\textsf{T}} d_{1, f} A_0 = d_{1, c} (I - k^{- 1} d_{1, c}^{\textsf{T}} H_c d_{1, c}) .\)

Substituting this in \(Z_0 H_c d_{1, c}\) gives zero. Thus \(Ed_{1, c} = 0\), and symmetry gives the left cycle identity. The two-sided lift yields a symmetric row \(C\) such that

\(\displaystyle Z_0 = (d_{2, c} B_c)^{\textsf{T}} C (d_{2, c} B_c) .\) (3.63)

Indeed multiply (3.61) by \(B_c\) on both sides and use \(B_c H_c = H_c B_c = I\).

We check uniformity in the block side \(L\). The dual transport and the row \(B_f T\) have entrywise strip cost \(O (L^{- 2})\), including their required cover differences, by the wire estimates of Sections 3.5 and 3.6. The Gram product \(T^{\textsf{T}} B_f T\) sums over \(L^4\) fine residues and a fixed number of form labels. Its entrywise cost is consequently \(O (L^4 L^{- 2} L^{- 2}) = O (1)\). The other rows in \(E\) have uniform costs. All lifts use fixed exterior index sets on the coarse side, so the resulting cost of \(C\) is uniform in \(L\) too.

There is a second lift with a stronger scale gain. Put

\(\displaystyle Y = d_{2, f} B_f TH_c .\)

The second identity in (3.62) gives \(Yd_{1, c} = d_{2, f} d_{1, f} A_0 = 0\). Its right lift is a row \(S\) with \(Sd_{2, c} = Y\). One fine exterior difference is a signed sum of at most four cover differences of \(B_f T\); the first difference estimate supplies one additional \(L^{- 1}\). Thus each entry of \(Y\), and then of \(S\), has strip cost \(O (L^{- 3})\). Multiplying the lift identity by \(B_c\) gives

\(\displaystyle Sd_{2, c} B_c = d_{2, f} B_f T.\) (3.64)

For this mixed kernel, perform the right lift separately for each fine residue and fine three-form label. These indices belong to the output space of the lift; the division and contraction act only on its coarse form columns. No sum over the \(L^4\) output residues occurs in the lift bound. Thus its constant remains independent of \(L\). The row \(S^{\textsf{T}} S\) now has cost \(O (L^{- 2})\): its summation has \(L^4\) residues, while both factors cost \(L^{- 3}\). More explicitly, if each row entry at a fixed residue has weighted coefficient sum at most \(cL^{- 3}\) at width \(\alpha\), then an entry of the Gram row has weighted sum at most

\(\displaystyle L^4 n_3 (cL^{- 3})^2 = n_3 c^2 L^{- 2},\)

where \(n_3\) is the fixed number of three-form labels. For each residue this is the convolution inequality \(e^{\alpha |x - y|_1} \leqslant e^{\alpha |x|_1} e^{\alpha |y|_1}\), followed by Tonelli; transposition reverses a displacement and does not change its weight. The same argument with two \(L^{- 2}\) factors gives the earlier uniform bound on \(T^{\textsf{T}} B_f T\). These are bounds on cover rows before periodization, so they hold simultaneously on every period. Retaining the residue count is essential.

3.10.3Making the curvature row positive

Choose amendment coefficients \(m, l \geqslant 0\) for the fine and coarse beds. With \(D_c = (I + ld_{2, c} B_c d_{2, c}^{\textsf{T}})^{- 1}\), set

\(\displaystyle R_0 = C + lI - mS^{\textsf{T}} S, \qquad R = D_c^{\textsf{T}} R_0 D_c .\)

The uniform bounds just proved give constants \(C_0, C_1 > 0\), independent of the kernel history and \(L\), with

\(\displaystyle | \langle u, Cu \rangle | \leqslant C_0 \|u\|^2, \qquad \langle u, S^{\textsf{T}} Su \rangle \leqslant C_1 L^{- 2} \|u\|^2 .\)

Choose \(l \geqslant C_0 + 2\) and \(L^2 \geqslant mC_1\). Then

\(\displaystyle \langle u, R_0 u \rangle \geqslant \|u\|^2 .\)

This is positivity on the entire three-form space. No projection onto a momentum-dependent range is needed. The row \(D_c^{- 1} = I + ld_{2, c} B_c d_{2, c}^{\textsf{T}}\) has uniformly bounded operator norm, say at most \(K\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \langle u, Ru \rangle \geqslant \|D_c u\|^2 \geqslant K^{- 2} \|u\|^2 .\)

The inverse construction of Subsection 3.8, applied to \(I + ld_{2, c} B_c d_{2, c}^{\textsf{T}} \geqslant I\) as in (3.48), supplies a common exponential row bound for \(D_c\). Here \(l\) is already fixed; the original row has an upper bound uniform over the kernel histories. Together with the preceding bounds on \(C\) and \(S^{\textsf{T}} S\), this proves a uniform exponential row bound for every product defining \(R\). Subsection 3.8 supplies its positive square-root row \(P\), uniformly over all flights of these stages.

It remains to identify this factor with the actual amended curvature defect. Write

\(\displaystyle A_c = B_c + l (d_{2, c} B_c)^{\textsf{T}} (d_{2, c} B_c), \qquad A_f = B_f + m (d_{2, f} B_f)^{\textsf{T}} (d_{2, f} B_f), \qquad G_c = A_c^{- 1} .\)

Equations (3.63) and (3.64), followed by expansion, give

\(\displaystyle A_c - T^{\textsf{T}} A_f T - k^{- 1} d_{1, c} d_{1, c}^{\textsf{T}} = (d_{2, c} B_c)^{\textsf{T}} R_0 (d_{2, c} B_c) .\)

Using \((d_{2, c} B_c) G_c = D_c d_{2, c}\) from (3.49), multiply by \(G_c\) on both sides to obtain

\(\displaystyle G_c (A_c - T^{\textsf{T}} A_f T - k^{- 1} d_{1, c} d_{1, c}^{\textsf{T}}) G_c = d_{2, c}^{\textsf{T}} P^{\textsf{T}} Pd_{2, c} .\) (3.65)

This is precisely the curvature factorization in Subsection 3.9, with the actual amended beds. All formulas hold on every finite period, since they were constructed from the same Laurent rows.

3.10.4Choosing the bounds before the scale sequence

For each adjacent pair of stages \(j, t\), where \(t = j\) or \(t = j - 1\), let \(a_{j, t} > 0\) be the lower requirement on the coarse amendment coefficient just obtained. Select

\(\displaystyle l_i = \max (a_{i, i}, a_{i + 1, i}) .\)

Then every permitted transition has \(l_t \geqslant a_{j, t}\) and \(l_j \geqslant 0\). Its required block-size floor is finite because the fine amendment \(m = l_j\) is now fixed. Combine the two permitted flight floors at stage \(j\) by their maximum. Combine bed and flight strip widths and positive lower bounds by minima, and upper bounds by maxima. All widths and lower bounds stay positive, since each combination is finite.

Choose the stage's direct precision below its positive bed ceiling. Choose its block length above the flight floor. These are requirements on the original stage profiles and pre-length floors; they are imposed before the common schedule of Subsection 19.2. They hold for both within-stage steps and a change to the next stage, however many times either occurs.

At a terminal cutoff \(N\), start with the top kernel history and descend along those very block lengths. At each level use its positive bed from Subsection 3.8; at each step use (3.65) and the exact block completion of Subsection 3.9. The latter's strip calculation inherits the prescribed iterated difference bounds for its unchanged \(A_0, T\) rows, narrowing their width if necessary, and proves the displayed strip bounds for its remaining blocks. This changes neither the bed rows nor the original one-form and two-form transports \(A_0, T\). It only constructs the remaining three blocks from their common positive curvature factor. Taking the indicated finite minima and maxima at each stage gives stage-indexed bounds valid for every \(N\), every intermediate level and every finite period. These bounds are fixed before the level schedule, but need not have a common positive lower width or a common upper cost over all stages. This proves the bed and curvature suppliers used by the horizon construction, using the original pulse transport identities proved in (3.42). The attachment and bounds of the nonlinear path remain the separate construction step.

3.11Signed lattice symmetry of the quadratic precision

The path attachment uses the quadratic precision of the selected bed. We verify its invariance under signed permutations of the coordinate axes, including the change of anchor caused by reversing a bond. This is a property of the bilinear form on one-cochains; the curvature metric need not satisfy a stronger symmetry away from exact curvatures.

Let \(s\) be a signed permutation of the four axes, acting on sites and momenta by the same orthogonal integer matrix. A positive bond \((x, a)\) becomes the directed bond from \(sx\) to \(sx + se_a\). If \(se_a = - e_b\), its value in positive-bond coordinates is minus the value at \((sx - e_b, b)\). This defines the pullback \(R_s\) on real one-cochains. For a canonical plaquette \(p = (a, b)\), \(a < b\), let \(r_s (x, p)\) be its image with its two axes reordered increasingly and its lower vertex shifted for each reversed axis. Its orientation sign is

\(\displaystyle \epsilon_s (p) = \epsilon_s (a) \epsilon_s (b) \text{sgn} (s_{\text{perm}} (b) - s_{\text{perm}} (a)),\)

where \(\epsilon_s (a) \in \{- 1, 1\}\) is the sign of the image axis. In the permutation subscripts, only the unsigned permutation is meant. Equivalently the last factor is \(1\) if the two image axes are in increasing order and \(- 1\) otherwise. The map \(r_s\) is a permutation of canonical plaquettes on every finite torus and \(\epsilon_s (p)^2 = 1\).

Let \(d\) denote the discrete exterior derivative. The boundary of the image of an oriented plaquette is the image of its boundary, with the same orientation sign, so

\(\displaystyle (dR_s a) (x, p) = \epsilon_s (p) (da) (r_s (x, p)) .\) (3.66)

One can verify the identity directly by substituting \((da) (x, a, b) = a (x, a) + a (x + e_a, b) - a (x + e_b, a) - a (x, b)\). Reversing an axis replaces each affected bond by its negative at the shifted lower endpoint; exchanging the two axes reverses the whole expression. These two operations generate all the signs and permutations, so the formula holds also across the torus seam and for even periods.

Assume the blocking penalty is positive and the kernel level is at least four, as in the retained construction. Let \(P\) be the unamended curvature row from Subsection 3.8. Its Fourier symbol is diagonal in the plaquette axes. For \(q \in [- \pi, \pi]^4 \setminus \{0\}\), its entry at \(p = (a, b)\) is

\(\displaystyle \hat{P}_{pp} (q) = \rho (q)^{- 1} D_a (q)^{- 1} D_b (q)^{- 1}, \qquad \rho (q) = \sum_c \frac{4 \sin^2 (q_c / 2)}{D_c (q)} .\)

Here \(D_c = \mathscr{D}_{v, c}\) is the positive level response of the selected kernel history away from zero. We verify \(D_{s_{\text{perm}} (c)} (sq) = D_c (q)\) using the feed formula in Subsection 3.5. The reciprocal divisor is invariant because it is the reciprocal of the sum of the four chip squares \(4 \sin^2 (q_a / 2)\). In the two-sided drive product, the centered scalar filters are products of the same even factors in the four coordinates, and the two rails for direction \(c\) are evaluated at opposite momenta. A sign flip exchanges those rails and a permutation carries the distinguished coordinate to \(s_{\text{perm}} (c)\). Thus the two-sided drive product is equivariant. For mass side \(M\), the alias set consists of all solutions of \(Mz = q\) modulo \(2 \pi \mathbb{Z}^4\). The signed permutation maps this set bijectively to the alias set over \(sq\), so reindexing the feed sum preserves the response. This uses reciprocal periodicity of the summand, even if centered lifts at a seam change by a reciprocal vector. The same argument applies to the constant-input feeds in the noise recursion: the new noise is the invariant constant \(1\), and the earlier noise is carried by the equivariant alias sum. Induction over the finite kernel history proves the identity. The sine squares are unchanged by sign and permuted with the same axes. Consequently \(\rho (sq) = \rho (q)\) and

\(\displaystyle \hat{P}_{pp} (q) = \hat{P}_{p', p'} (sq), \qquad p' = sp\)

with the image axes canonically ordered. At \(q = 0\) the unamended symbol is the identity matrix, so the same identity holds there too. Off-diagonal entries vanish.

The Laurent rows are absolutely summable. Uniqueness of Fourier coefficients and the change of variable \(q \mapsto sq\) therefore give

\(\displaystyle P_{pp} (z) = P_{p' p'} (sz), \qquad P_{pq} = 0 \quad (p \ne q) .\)

Let \(P_n\) denote the periodized row on a torus of side \(n\). The lower vertex of an image plaquette has the form \(sx + t_p\), where \(t_p\) depends only on its axes. For two plaquettes with the same axes, this shift cancels from their separation. Reindexing the absolutely convergent periodization sum by \(z \mapsto sz\) thus proves

\(\displaystyle P_n ((x, p), (y, q)) \epsilon_s (p) \epsilon_s (q) = P_n (r_s (x, p), r_s (y, q)) .\) (3.67)

For unequal axes both sides are zero; for equal axes the two signs multiply to one. This cancellation is why the anchor shifts cannot be ignored before passing to the bilinear form.

Using (3.66) and (3.67), expand the finite double sum:

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \langle dR_s a, P_n dR_s b \rangle & = \sum_{u, v} \epsilon_s (u) \epsilon_s (v) (da) (r_s u) P_n (u, v) (db) (r_s v)\\ & = \sum_{u, v} (da) (r_s u) P_n (r_s u, r_s v) (db) (r_s v)\\ & = \langle da, P_n db \rangle . \end{array}\)

The last equality reindexes by the permutation \(r_s\).

The positive amendment (3.48) of Subsection 3.8 vanishes on exact curvatures. In the notation there, \(P\) is the pulse row \(H\) and the amended metric is \(M = G\); the identity \(Gd_1 = Hd_1\) proved after (3.48) holds on every period. Hence \(d^{\ast} M_n d = d^{\ast} P_n d\), and the quadratic bilinear form of \(M\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \langle R_s a, d^{\ast} M_n dR_s b \rangle = \langle a, d^{\ast} M_n db \rangle .\)

For a Lie-algebra-valued field, expand in an orthonormal colour basis. The signed lattice pullback acts separately in every colour, and the gauge quadratic form is the sum of the real bilinear forms just considered. Summing their equalities proves the required invariance for every pair of such fields and every period. The positive square root used to represent the amended metric is not needed in this symmetry argument.

3.12Finite-scale propagation of the neutral curvature representative

The organization by successive localized effective actions follows Bałaban's small-field renormalization group [Bał87, §§0–2]. The scale selection and the estimates for the marked observables used here are developed below.

This section records the finite-scale construction that supplies the scalar recurrence used in Subsection 3.4. It starts from a terminal difference of two quadratic precisions, propagates a representative of that difference modulo linear-curvature null directions, and controls its cost at every earlier level. The one-step operation is the local Schur and matching argument of Subsection 3.3; the present section supplies its actual finite-history inputs and closes the resulting recurrence. No limiting argument is used here.

3.12.1Data on a finite interval of levels

For each \(x\) in an index set \(X\), fix one schedule, a measuring level \(k_x\), and finite indices \(i_0 (x) \leqslant N_x \leqslant n_x\). In formulas for one fixed \(x\), suppress its subscript and write

\(\displaystyle J_i = J (i), \qquad L_i = | \text{block at step } i|, \qquad t_i = \log a_i^{- 1}, \qquad \Omega_i = \Omega (J_i), \qquad \sigma_i = w (J_i),\)

where \(a_i\) is the positive length at level \(i\). The schedule identities are

\(\displaystyle t_{i + 1} - t_i = \log L_i, \qquad L_i \geqslant 3.\)

\(\displaystyle F_j (U) = \sum_A (\lim_{t \downarrow 0} g_{j, t} (A, U)) \prod_{i \in A} z_i .\)

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{N}_r \left( \prod_{i \in A} z_i \right) \leqslant 1.\)

where \(P_i (\theta)\) is the final response produced from the single leaf \(M_i\) at amplitude \(\theta\). These are not approximations: with no active switch the selected color is the vacuum color, and with just switch \(i\) its color is the single-leaf color at amplitude \(r_i t\).

\(\displaystyle \left( \prod_{k = 1}^N f_k \right)' (0) = \sum_{k = 1}^N f_k' (0) \prod_{\ell \ne k} f_{\ell} (0),\)

by repeated use of the product rule. \(\displaystyle D_{i, j} (U) = \left. \frac{d}{d \theta} P_i (\theta)_j (U) \right|_{\theta = 0} .\)

This is exactly the hinge of the selected gauge interface, since that interface is defined by differentiating the same one-leaf final response.

\(\displaystyle g_{j, U, t} (F) = t^{- |F|} \left( K (t, F)_j (U) - \sum_{A \subsetneq F} t^{|A|} g_{j, U, t} (A) \right) .\)

\(\displaystyle K (t, \{i\})_j (U) = g_{j, U, t} (\varnothing) + t \hspace{0.17em} g_{j, U, t} (\{i\}) .\)

At \(F = \varnothing\) the same representation gives \(K (t, \varnothing)_j (U) = g_{j, U, t} (\varnothing)\). Subtract these equations and use (3.90):

\(\displaystyle t \hspace{0.17em} g_{j, U, t} (\{i\}) = P_i (r_i t)_j (U) - P_i (0)_j (U) .\)

Fix a block side \(L\), an integer occupancy bound \(N\), a positive strength \(\mu\), a positive relative weight \(\lambda\), an exponent \(\rho \in (- 1, 0)\), and a fixed cost \(C > 0\). Write

\(\displaystyle b (x) = x + c_L, \qquad R (x) = \lceil (\log x)^2 \rceil, \qquad M (x) = \exp ((\log \log b (x))^2),\)

where \(c_L\) is fixed once \(L\) is fixed. Set

\(\displaystyle f (x) = b (x)^{- 1}, \qquad y (x) = b (x)^{- 2},\)
\(\displaystyle k (x) = (N\mathfrak{s}(R (b (x))) + 4) y (x), \qquad z (x) = 2 \exp (- \mu M (x)^2 / 16) + N\mathfrak{s} (R (x)) y (x) .\)

Here

\(\displaystyle \mathfrak{s} (r) = e \hspace{0.17em} (2 (16 r + 9))^4, \qquad \mathcal{R} (x) = b (x)^{\rho} e^{\lambda C} .\)

Lemma 3.38. (Rate calculus and decaying costs) For a function \(u\), say that it has rate \(a\) if, for every \(\delta > 0\), eventually \(|u (x) | \leqslant b (x)^{a + \delta}\). Products add rates, sums of finitely many terms keep the larger rate, and multiplication by a fixed constant does not change a rate. Moreover, if \(u\) has rate \(a < 0\), then \(u (x) \to 0\).

The functions \(R (x)\) and every fixed polynomial in \(R (x)\) have rate zero. The same is true of \(\mathfrak{s} (R (x))\) and \(\mathfrak{s} (R (b (x)))\). If \(\mu > 0\), then the first summand in \(z (x)\) has rate less than every real number; in particular it has rate \(- 2\). The fixed shift yields

\(\displaystyle R (b (x)) \leqslant 2 R (x) \quad \text{eventually} . \) (3.92)

Consequently \(k\) and \(z\) have rate \(- 2\), as does

\(\displaystyle (2 R (x) + 1)^4 z (x) .\)

Proof. For products, apply the two rate bounds with exponent slack \(\delta / 2\) and use

\(\displaystyle b (x)^{a + \delta / 2} b (x)^{c + \delta / 2} = b (x)^{a + c + \delta} .\)

For sums, use the same slack and the fact that \(b (x)^{\delta / 2}\) eventually exceeds the fixed number of summands. Fixed constants are absorbed by any positive slack. Since \(b (x) \to \infty\), a negative rate implies convergence to zero by choosing an intermediate exponent still below zero.

Since \(b (x) = x + c_L\), the ratio \((\log b (x))^2 / (\log x)^2\) tends to one. Thus, eventually, \((\log b (x))^2 \leqslant (3 / 2) (\log x)^2\) and \((\log x)^2 \geqslant 2\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle R (b (x)) \leqslant (\log b (x))^2 + 1 \leqslant (3 / 2) (\log x)^2 + 1 \leqslant 2 (\log x)^2 \leqslant 2 R (x),\)

as claimed. Also, the definition \(R (x) = \lceil (\log x)^2 \rceil\) and the fixed-shift relation show that \(R (x)\) is bounded by a constant times \((\log b (x))^2\) for large \(x\). Applying this same comparison with argument \(b (x)\) controls \(R (b (x))\). Every fixed power of \(\log b (x)\) is smaller than \(b (x)^{\delta}\) for every \(\delta > 0\); hence \(R\) and its fixed polynomials have rate zero. The site factor is a fixed quartic polynomial in its argument, so it also has rate zero, including after the comparison (3.92).

Finally, \(M (x)^2 = \exp (2 (\log \log b (x))^2)\). For every \(A > 0\),

\(\displaystyle \frac{\mu}{16} M (x)^2 \geqslant A \log b (x)\)

for all sufficiently large \(x\), since with \(t = \log b (x)\) the logarithm of the ratio \(M (x)^2 / t\) is \(2 (\log t)^2 - \log t \to \infty\). Taking \(A\) as large as desired proves the claimed decay of the Gaussian summand. The remaining rate statements follow by the product and sum rules just proved.\(\Box\)

Proposition 3.39. (Eventually purchased tariff) Assume \(\rho \in (- 1, 0)\), \(\lambda > 0\), and \(\mu > 0\). Then, for all sufficiently large \(x\), the following inequalities hold simultaneously:

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} 0 & \leqslant f (x), & 0 & < y (x) \leqslant 1, \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.93)} \\ N\mathfrak{s} (R (x)) e^{(\lambda + 1) C} f (x) & \leqslant \mathcal{R} (x), & & \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.94)} \\ k (x) + e^{k (x)} k (x) & \leqslant f (x), & & \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.95)} \\ (2 R (x) + 1)^4 z (x) & \leqslant \tfrac{1}{2}, & e^{z (x)} - 1 & \leqslant 1. \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.96)} \end{array}\)

The lodging and credit cost is \(z (x)\), whose Gaussian term is

\(\displaystyle 2 \exp (- \mu M (x)^2 / 16) .\)

Here the fine monitor equals \(M (x)\). Thus these are exactly the nonnegativity, probe, shipment, purchase, lodging, and credit conditions required by the local tariff.

Proof. For \(x\) large, \(b (x) > 1\), so \(f = b^{- 1} > 0\) and \(0 < y = b^{- 2} \leqslant 1\). The polynomial factor has rate zero, while \(f\) has rate \(- 1\). Thus

\(\displaystyle N\mathfrak{s} (R (x)) e^{(\lambda + 1) C} f (x)\)

has rate \(- 1\). Choose \(q_0\) strictly between \(- 1\) and \(\rho\). Eventually the left side is at most \(b (x)^{q_0}\), which is at most \(b (x)^{\rho}\) for large \(x\). Multiplying the fixed factor \(e^{\lambda C}\) on the right gives

\(\displaystyle N\mathfrak{s} (R (x)) e^{(\lambda + 1) C} f (x) \leqslant b (x)^{\rho} e^{\lambda C},\)

as claimed.

By the rate-calculus lemma, \(k\) has rate \(- 2\). In particular, eventually \(0 \leqslant k \leqslant b^{- 3 / 2} \leqslant 1\). On a further tail, \(b^{1 / 2} \geqslant 1 + e\), and therefore

\(\displaystyle k + ek \leqslant (1 + e) b^{- 3 / 2} \leqslant b^{- 1} .\)

Since \(e^k \leqslant e\) for \(k \leqslant 1\), this proves (3.95). The product \((2 R + 1)^4 z\) has rate \(- 2\), so it is eventually at most \(1 / 2\). Also \(z \to 0\) and \(z \geqslant 0\); hence eventually \(z \leqslant \log 2\), which gives \(e^z - 1 \leqslant 1\). This proves all the claims.\(\Box\)

Remark 3.40. (Placement in the selected construction) On the selected run, the Gaussian pressure estimate supplies this tariff. The arrival receipt supplies local activity, allowance, and gain bounds; the reset receipt supplies separate rate payments for soft response. The delay is enlarged to satisfy all finitely many transitions at each stage. The finite-menu estimate uses arrival and reset as distinct inputs: neither is a consequence of the tariff. Its shipment budget is the tariff inequality proved above, combined with the predecessor's paid activity bounds.

3.16The uniform finite-source response on the selected history

The centered-field argument requires the menu response and the ordinary transition to belong to one history. We now identify their parameters and bounds. The analytic inputs are the funded path and Gaussian data used in the preceding one-step estimates; this step makes no new choice of a background transition or of an interface transporting insertions.

Fix a finite history, adjacent scales \(m + 1, m\), and one point of its interpolating path. Denote the fine representation by \(R_f\), the result by \(R_c\), and the corresponding local coordinate change and insertion transport by \(\mathcal{D}\). The construction of the unmarked result fixes all three. Use the same tuned representation, forced array and derivative of the one-leaf response when constructing the menu terms of Sections 3.13 and 3.14. Their quiet identity is therefore the profile of \(R_c\), and their singleton identity uses the hinge of precisely \(\mathcal{D}\).

Write \(b_f, b_c \geqslant 100\) for the support exponents, \(N\) for the finite occupancy bound, and \(u \geqslant 0\) for the menu shipment allowance. A menu offer is measured at exponent \(b_f - 4\) and allowance \(u\). Its marker weights are positive, its modes nonempty, and its named sites lie in the corresponding fine crests. The occupancy bound is on their images under the parent map. It also bounds occupancy at each fine site, because markers with the same fine site have the same parent. The birth-region conversion subsequently places a chosen parent image in the corresponding coarse birth support. The shipment inequality of Subsection 3.15, together with the birth-region conversion in Theorem 3.33, gives the rooted majorants required for the paid component construction. This is uniform over every finite menu satisfying the offer bound.

Let \(a\) be the earned exponent and \(e > 0\) the additional reserve, so \(a \geqslant b_c + 3\). Let \(P_0, P_1 \geqslant 0\) be the ordinary and flagged component budgets, \(0 \leqslant \rho \leqslant 1 / 2\) the cluster ratio, and \(E_0 \geqslant 0\) the marked exponential cost. The field-bound calculation gives

\(\displaystyle 4 e^{E_0} P_1 + 4 \frac{P_0}{1 - \rho} \leqslant V_Q \leqslant \tfrac{1}{2} .\) (3.97)

Here the first inequality follows from the same component majorants used in the cluster sums. To make its numerical content explicit, the total pledge is \(V_Q = 10 (P_0 + E')\), with \(P_1 = E' e^{- E_0}\), \(E' \geqslant 0\). Since \((1 - \rho)^{- 1} \leqslant 2\), its left side is at most \(4 E' + 8 P_0 \leqslant 10 (E' + P_0)\). The upper bound \(V_Q \leqslant 1 / 2\) is purchased by increasing the entry coupling: both \(P_0\) and \(E'\) have the fixed negative upper exponent of the component estimates. This is the same arrival threshold used to pay their degree bounds, so all inequalities hold together.

The logarithmic response has the rooted bound \(2 P_0 / (1 - \rho)\) at exponent \(a + e\), by (3.80). In particular it is at most \(1\), indeed at most \(1 / 4\), by (3.97). Replacing its exponent by \(b_c + 1 / 2\) only decreases each nonnegative summand, since \(b_c + 1 / 2 \leqslant a + e\) and all cover numbers are nonnegative. The flagged response has the separate rooted bound

\(\displaystyle t = 2 \exp \{- \mu \Delta_{G, L} (\beta)^2 / 16\}\)

at exponent \(b_c + 1\). This is the second, larger-reserve response from Subsection 3.13, paid by the two successive absorptions of Subsection 19.1. It is not deduced from the logarithmic bound.
