7Local fields and the operator product expansion

7.1One field prescription for every truncation degree

For comparison with remainder estimates involving spectator fields, see [HK, §1], which treats the OPE at fixed perturbative order in massive Euclidean \(\phi^4\) theory. Here the coefficients and estimates are constructed on the limiting Wilson hierarchy at each fixed truncation degree.

The coefficients must agree at different truncation degrees. Choosing a new basis independently at each finite degree would not ensure this. We construct a single compatible system of fields and finite correlation tests, one degree at a time. The tests are fixed before choosing the cluster geometry or its spectators.

Let \(E_n\) be the finite-dimensional complex space of physical fields through degree \(n\). Denote its injective inclusion maps by \(\iota_{mn} : E_m \to E_n\), \(m \leqslant n\), with \(\iota_{nn} = I\) and \(\iota_{nk} \iota_{mn} = \iota_{mk}\). These are the physical filtration maps, so inserting a field and its image in a higher degree gives the same separated correlations.

An exterior test \(\lambda\) consists of finitely many fixed spectator labels and a separated configuration including a distinguished first position. Inserting \(v \in E_n\) at that first position and evaluating the mixed Euclidean correlation defines a linear functional \(\lambda_n (v)\). The physical field quotient has been defined by vanishing of all such correlations. Thus these functionals separate \(E_n\):

\(\displaystyle [\lambda_n (v) = 0 \text{ for every exterior test } \lambda] \Longrightarrow v = 0.\) (7.1)

Their compatibility with filtration is \(\lambda_n \iota_{mn} = \lambda_m\). For a finite linear combination \(b = \sum_{\lambda \in F} b_{\lambda} \lambda\) use the notation \(b_n = \sum_{\lambda \in F} b_{\lambda} \lambda_n\). The same finite combination then defines a test on every level.

Lemma 7.1. Every linear functional on \(E_n\) is \(b_n\) for some finite combination \(b\) of exterior tests.

Proof. Let \(V\) be the span of the exterior functionals inside \(E_n^{\ast}\). If \(V\) were proper, a basis of \(V\) would consist of fewer than \(\dim E_n\) functionals. The linear map from \(E_n\) to their coordinate values would then have a nonzero kernel, by rank–nullity. A nonzero vector in that kernel would be annihilated by all of \(V\), hence by every exterior test, contrary to (7.1). Therefore \(V = E_n^{\ast}\). Membership in a span means a finite linear combination, as required.\(\Box\)

Put \(E_{- 1} = 0\) and \(d_n = \dim (E_n / \iota_{n - 1, n} E_{n - 1})\), where the denominator is zero when \(n = 0\). A channel is a pair \(\alpha = (n, j)\) with \(1 \leqslant j \leqslant d_n\); its degree is \(| \alpha | = n\). We seek fields \(u_{\alpha} \in E_{| \alpha |}\) and finite exterior tests \(b_{\alpha}\) such that, for every \(n\),

\(\displaystyle b_{\alpha, n} (\iota_{| \beta |, n} u_{\beta}) = \delta_{\alpha \beta} \quad (| \alpha |, | \beta | \leqslant n),\) (7.2)

and

\(\displaystyle v = \sum_{| \alpha | \leqslant n} b_{\alpha, n} (v) \iota_{| \alpha |, n} u_{\alpha} \qquad (v \in E_n) .\) (7.3)

The sums contain finitely many channels. No assertion of a convergent infinite expansion is involved.

Proposition 7.2. There is one choice of all the fields \(u_{\alpha}\) and tests \(b_{\alpha}\) satisfying (7.2) and (7.3) simultaneously for every \(n\). Moreover \(b_{\alpha, m} = 0\) whenever \(m < | \alpha |\).

Proof. At degree zero choose a basis of \(E_0\) and represent each coordinate functional by a finite exterior test, using Lemma 7.1. The two identities are then the usual basis identities.

Suppose the fields and tests through degree \(n\) have been chosen. Write \(R = \iota_{n, n + 1}\), let \(q : E_{n + 1} \to E_{n + 1} / R (E_n)\) be the quotient map, and set

\(\displaystyle Bv = \sum_{| \alpha | \leqslant n} b_{\alpha, n + 1} (v) \iota_{| \alpha |, n} u_{\alpha} .\)

Test compatibility and (7.3) give \(BR = I_{E_n}\). Choose a linear right inverse \(S\) of \(q\); it exists by lifting a finite basis of the quotient. Correct this arbitrary lift by putting

\(\displaystyle F = S - RBS.\)

Then \(qF = I\) and \(BF = 0\). The old tests vanish on the new image \(F\). Indeed (7.2) gives \(b_{\alpha, n} B = b_{\alpha, n + 1}\) for every old channel \(\alpha\), so \(b_{\alpha, n + 1} F = b_{\alpha, n} BF = 0\).

Every \(v \in E_{n + 1}\) has the exact decomposition

\(\displaystyle v = Fqv + RBv.\) (7.4)

To prove it, \(q (v - Fqv) = 0\) implies \(v - Fqv = Ra\) for some \(a \in E_n\). Applying \(B\) and using \(BF = 0\), \(BR = I\) gives \(a = Bv\). Now choose a basis \((e_j)\) of the quotient. The new fields are \(Fe_j\). For each new channel choose a finite exterior test representing \(e_j^{\ast} q\) on \(E_{n + 1}\). Leave every old field and every old test unchanged. The new tests annihilate \(R (E_n)\), are dual to the new fields because \(qF = I\), and the old tests annihilate the new fields as just proved. Old fields and tests retain their mutual duality by compatibility. This proves all four old/new cases of (7.2). Expanding the two terms in (7.4) proves (7.3) at degree \(n + 1\).

Iterate. A channel and its finite test, once chosen, never change. Their value in the resulting infinite list is the one fixed at their birth degree. Any identity at degree \(n\) involves only the finitely many channels already fixed by stage \(n\), so it remains valid in the final list. Finally take \(m < | \alpha |\) and expand \(v \in E_m\) using (7.3). Lift that identity to degree \(| \alpha |\) and apply \(b_{\alpha}\). Biorthogonality makes every term zero, because all its channels have degree at most \(m\). Compatibility then gives \(b_{\alpha, m} (v) = 0\).\(\Box\)

The subtraction \(RBS\) is the reason this construction works at all degrees. It places every new quotient lift in the exact kernels of all old tests, while retaining the old tests as actual finite correlation evaluations.

7.1.1Testing a product at the cluster center

For each exterior test \(\lambda\), translate its distinguished position to zero. Write its other positions as \(z_{\lambda, j}\) and its spectator labels as \(w_{\lambda, j}\). These positions are pairwise distinct and none is zero. For a cluster of fields \(v_i\) at \(x_i\) and center \(o\), define

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R}_{\lambda} (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) = S_{\text{mix}} ((v_i, x_i - o)_i, (w_{\lambda, j}, z_{\lambda, j})_j)\)

when the combined configuration is separated, and assign value zero otherwise. This convention gives a function everywhere; every asymptotic use below is in its separated region. Define

\(\displaystyle c_{\alpha}^{\text{raw}} (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) = \sum_{\lambda \in \text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} b_{\alpha}} b_{\alpha, \lambda} \mathcal{R}_{\lambda} (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o),\) (7.5)

and the finite field-valued expression

\(\displaystyle H_n (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) = \sum_{| \alpha | \leqslant n} c_{\alpha}^{\text{raw}} (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) \iota_{| \alpha |, n} u_{\alpha} .\) (7.6)

Each coefficient is fixed independently of the truncation degree, the phase, or any later choice of exterior spectators. It is multilinear in the cluster labels on its separated region. The separation condition depends only on positions, so the zero extension is multilinear as well. Permutation symmetry of the correlations gives simultaneous permutation symmetry in cluster positions and labels. Simultaneous translation of the cluster and center leaves their differences unchanged.

For a single field \(v \in E_m\) placed at \(o\), translation invariance and the definition of the exterior test give

\(\displaystyle c_{\alpha}^{\text{raw}} ((o), (v) ; o) = b_{\alpha, m} (v) .\)

All the detector configurations in this evaluation are separated. Hence, for \(m \leqslant n\), (7.3) implies

\(\displaystyle H_n ((o), (v) ; o) = \iota_{mn} v.\) (7.7)

The same testing operation is compatible with replacing any input field by its filtration image, since separated correlations have that compatibility.

For every fixed finite list of coefficients there is a common positive detector clearance. To see this, take the minimum of the finitely many nonzero lengths \(|z_{\lambda, j} |\) occurring in their finite test supports; if there are no spectator positions, there is no such restriction. If \(|x_i - o|\) is less than half that minimum, no cluster point can meet a detector position. Distinctness among cluster points and among detector points supplies the remaining separation conditions. The same argument works after any orthogonal rotation of the relative cluster, because rotations preserve \(|x_i - o|\). Smoothness of the mixed correlations on separated configurations makes each such finite coefficient collection smooth on this common region. This is a finite-degree statement; it gives no uniform clearance for infinitely many degrees.

The raw expressions above do not yet have the required rotational and reality properties, because the finite tests were chosen by linear algebra. Averaging the field-valued expression over \(O (4)\) and symmetrizing conjugation supplies those properties. The subsequent homogeneous projection must be made using these averaged tests themselves, so that its lower-degree components agree exactly. These operations and their quantitative error estimates are the next steps of the OPE construction.

7.2Covariant homogeneous OPE coefficients

The finite tests of Subsection 7.1 are chosen for exact compatibility, without imposing rotational symmetry. We first average their field-valued output, retaining both the input and output rotations. We then construct homogeneous complements using these averaged tests. This order ensures that the final coefficients have compatible finite truncations as well as the required symmetries.

Write \(R_n (K)\) for the continuous \(O (4)\) action on \(E_n\) and \(J_n\) for complex conjugation of fields. Assume, as supplied by the field structure, that these actions commute with the inclusions, that \(J_n\) is an antilinear involution, and that \(J_n R_n (K) = R_n (K) J_n\). Assume moreover that the mixed correlations are Euclidean covariant and real on separated configurations: for every finite label list \(\boldsymbol{v}\), every separated position list \(\boldsymbol{x}\), every \(K \in O (4)\) and every \(s \in \mathbb{R}^4\),

\(\displaystyle S_{\text{mix}} (R (K)\boldsymbol{v}; K\boldsymbol{x}) = S_{\text{mix}} (\boldsymbol{v}; \boldsymbol{x}), \qquad S_{\text{mix}} (\boldsymbol{v}; \boldsymbol{x}+ s) = S_{\text{mix}} (\boldsymbol{v}; \boldsymbol{x}), \qquad S_{\text{mix}} (J\boldsymbol{v}; \boldsymbol{x}) = \overline{S_{\text{mix}} (\boldsymbol{v}; \boldsymbol{x})},\) (7.8)

where \(R (K)\) and \(J\) act on each label with its own degree. These laws are not consequences of the preceding assumptions on the actions: they are properties of the correlations and are inputs to this section; they are to be supplied, for the constructed correlations on the same hierarchy, by the producer of the Euclidean field structure. Subsection 15.7 uses all three. Haar measure \(dK\) on \(O (4)\) is normalized to have mass one.

7.2.1Averaging on a common separated region

For a fixed degree \(n\), let \(\mathcal{U}_n\) be the set of cluster configurations \((\boldsymbol{x}, o)\) for which every exterior test occurring through degree \(n\) remains separated from the relative cluster after every orthogonal rotation. This is an open set. Indeed, for a fixed finite test, separation is an open condition in the configuration and the rotation. If it holds for every rotation at a configuration, compactness of \(O (4)\) supplies finitely many rotation neighborhoods and a common configuration neighborhood on which it still holds. Intersect over the finitely many tests. The set is invariant under simultaneous translations, rotations and permutations of the cluster. It contains every sufficiently small separated cluster around its center, by the clearance argument of the preceding section.

On \(\mathcal{U}_n\) define

\(\displaystyle A_n (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) = \int_{O (4)} R_n (K) H_n (K^{- 1} \boldsymbol{x}, R (K^{- 1})\boldsymbol{v}; K^{- 1} o) \hspace{0.17em} dK,\) (7.9)

where the action on each input label uses its own degree. Set \(A_n = 0\) outside \(\mathcal{U}_n\). The integrand is continuous in \(K\), takes values in a finite-dimensional space, and is therefore integrable on the compact group. Multilinearity passes through the integral; the zero extension preserves it because the domain condition is independent of labels.

The map \(A_n\) is smooth in positions on \(\mathcal{U}_n\). Here one must not assume differentiability of the representation in \(K\) to justify position derivatives. Expand the input labels and output in finite bases. Each matrix entry of \(R (K)\) is continuous and bounded on the compact group. The remaining factors are derivatives in positions of separated correlations evaluated at rotated coordinates. On a sufficiently small closed configuration neighborhood times \(O (4)\), all finitely many separations have a positive minimum. The correlation derivatives of each fixed order are therefore continuous and bounded on this compact set. The chain rule contributes only bounded entries of the rotation matrix. Thus each position derivative has a uniform integrable bound. Differentiation under the integral, repeated for every finite order, proves smoothness. The same finite-basis expansion justifies smooth variation of labels when needed.

Left invariance of Haar measure gives rotation covariance. For a fixed \(L \in O (4)\) substitute \(K = LH\) in \(A_n (L\boldsymbol{x}, R (L)\boldsymbol{v}; Lo)\). Since \((LH)^{- 1} L = H^{- 1}\), the integrand becomes \(R_n (L)\) times the integrand in (7.9). Hence

\(\displaystyle A_n (L\boldsymbol{x}, R (L)\boldsymbol{v}; Lo) = R_n (L) A_n (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) .\)

This also holds outside the domain by its invariance. Translation and permutation identities follow directly from the corresponding identities for \(H_n\) inside the integral. For a single field \(v \in E_q\) at its center, with \(q \leqslant n\), (7.7) shows that the integrand is the constant \(R_n (K) \iota_{qn} R_q (K^{- 1}) v = \iota_{qn} v\). Therefore \(A_n ((o), (v) ; o) = \iota_{qn} v\).

Now define the real-symmetric readout

\(\displaystyle C_n (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) = \tfrac{1}{2} (A_n (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) + J_n A_n (\boldsymbol{x}, J\boldsymbol{v}; o)) .\) (7.10)

It is still complex multilinear: an input scalar is conjugated by the input \(J\) and conjugated back by the output \(J_n\). It is smooth on \(\mathcal{U}_n\), has the same translation, permutation and rotation identities, and satisfies

\(\displaystyle C_n (\boldsymbol{x}, J\boldsymbol{v}; o) = J_n C_n (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o), \qquad C_n ((o), (v) ; o) = \iota_{qn} v \quad (q \leqslant n) .\)

The first identity uses \(J^2 = I\) and exchanges the two summands. The second follows from the corresponding identity for \(A_n\) and compatibility of \(J\) with inclusions.

7.2.2Compatible projections onto new degrees

For every pair \(m, q \geqslant 0\), define a linear map

\(\displaystyle L_{mq} : E_q \to E_m, \qquad L_{mq} v = C_m ((0), (v) ; 0) .\)

The centered single-field configuration always belongs to \(\mathcal{U}_m\). The preceding identities give

\(\displaystyle L_{mq} = \iota_{qm} (q \leqslant m), \qquad L_{mq} \iota_{rq} = L_{mr} (r \leqslant q) .\) (7.11)

For the second equality, every exterior correlation used in the raw tests is unchanged under the physical inclusion. This equality passes through rotation averaging, since the inclusions commute with rotations, and through conjugation averaging for the same reason. It holds even when \(m < r\); no extension of a low-degree inverse is being assumed. The maps \(L_{mq}\) intertwine rotations and conjugation as well.

Define recursively

\(\displaystyle K_m = I_{E_m} - \sum_{i < m} \iota_{im} K_i L_{im}, \qquad P_{mq} = K_m L_{mq}, \qquad V_m = \bigcap_{i < m} \ker P_{im} .\)

The empty sum makes \(K_0 = I\). These are finite sums of fixed linear maps. From (7.11), for \(q \leqslant m\),

\(\displaystyle P_{mq} v = \iota_{qm} v - \sum_{i < m} \iota_{im} P_{iq} v, \qquad \sum_{i \leqslant m} \iota_{im} P_{iq} v = \iota_{qm} v.\) (7.12)

Also \(P_{mq} \iota_{rq} = P_{mr}\) whenever \(r \leqslant q\). If \(q < m\), apply the second identity at degree \(m - 1\) and then include in \(E_m\). Its sum is the one subtracted in the first identity, so

\(\displaystyle P_{mq} = 0 \quad (q < m) .\) (7.13)

For \(v \in V_m\), the recursion with \(q = m\) gives \(P_{mm} v = v\).

Lemma 7.3. The range of \(K_m\) is contained in \(V_m\). If \(v \in V_j\), then \(P_{mj} v = \delta_{mj} v\), interpreting the right side as zero when \(m \neq j\). Consequently

\(\displaystyle E_m = \bigoplus_{j \leqslant m} \iota_{jm} V_j .\)

Proof. We prove the range assertion by induction on \(m\). Fix \(i < m\) and apply \(P_{im}\) to the defining formula for \(K_m x\). Compatibility gives

\(\displaystyle P_{im} K_m x = P_{im} x - \sum_{j < m} P_{ij} (K_j L_{jm} x) .\)

By the induction hypothesis \(K_j L_{jm} x \in V_j\). If \(i < j\), its image under \(P_{ij}\) is zero by the definition of \(V_j\); if \(i > j\), it is zero by (7.13). At \(i = j\), the identity on \(V_i\) proved above makes the term \(K_i L_{im} x = P_{im} x\). The sum therefore cancels the first term. This proves \(K_m x \in V_m\). The asserted action on \(V_j\) follows from the same three cases.

Take \(q = m\) in (7.12). Each \(P_{jm} x = K_j L_{jm} x\) belongs to \(V_j\), so this identity proves spanning. If \(\sum_{j \leqslant m} \iota_{jm} v_j = 0\) with \(v_j \in V_j\), apply \(P_{km}\) and use inclusion compatibility. All terms vanish except \(v_k\). Thus every \(v_k = 0\), proving directness.\(\Box\)

Induction in the formula for \(K_m\) shows that it commutes with rotations and conjugation: the inclusions and \(L_{im}\) intertwine them, and the previous \(K_i\) do so by induction. It follows that \(V_m\) is invariant under both operations, since all its defining tests intertwine them. No claim that the original arbitrary quotient lifts were covariant was needed.

Define the homogeneous output at degree \(m\) by

\(\displaystyle F_m (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) = K_m C_m (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) \in V_m .\)

It is multilinear, has the same symmetries as \(C_m\), and is smooth on \(\mathcal{U}_m\). At a single centered field \(v \in E_q\) it equals \(P_{mq} v\). In particular the finite packet

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{P}_D (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) = \sum_{m \leqslant D} \iota_{mD} F_m (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o)\)

satisfies the exact compatibility

\(\displaystyle P_{jD} \mathcal{P}_D = F_j \qquad (j \leqslant D),\) (7.14)

by Lemma 7.3. On one centered field of degree \(q \leqslant D\), the packet equals \(\iota_{qD} v\) by (7.12).

7.2.3Real channel bases and scalar coefficients

The conjugation \(J_m\) restricts to an antilinear involution of \(V_m\). Its fixed real vector space has a real basis \((e_{m, \alpha})\); this is also a complex basis of \(V_m\). Indeed every \(v \in V_m\) decomposes as

\(\displaystyle v = \frac{v + J_m v}{2} + i \frac{v - J_m v}{2 i},\)

and both displayed components are fixed by \(J_m\). This proves complex spanning. If \(\sum_{\alpha} z_{\alpha} e_{m, \alpha} = 0\), applying \(J_m\) and then adding and subtracting the two equations shows separately that the real and imaginary coefficient sums vanish. Real linear independence forces every \(z_{\alpha} = 0\).

Choose these bases once for every \(m\) and write

\(\displaystyle F_m (\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) = \sum_{\alpha} c_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{m, \alpha} (\boldsymbol{x}; o) e_{m, \alpha} .\) (7.15)

The coordinate functionals are continuous because the spaces are finite dimensional. Thus the coefficients inherit smoothness, multilinearity, translation and permutation symmetry; reality gives conjugation of their values under conjugation of all input labels. Rotation covariance holds for the field-valued output, or equivalently through the finite matrix of \(R_m\) on this basis. The individual basis vectors need not be rotation invariant.

Let \(\ell_{\chi, m, o, \boldsymbol{y}, \boldsymbol{w}}\) be the linear functional obtained by inserting an \(E_m\) field at \(o\) alongside separated spectators, in any phase \(\chi\) or in the mixed state. Physical inclusion compatibility gives \(\ell_{\chi, D} \iota_{mD} = \ell_{\chi, m}\). Therefore evaluation of the packet is exactly

\(\displaystyle \ell_{\chi, D} (\mathcal{P}_D) = \sum_{m \leqslant D} \sum_{\alpha} c_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{m, \alpha} (\boldsymbol{x}; o) S_{\chi} (e_{m, \alpha}, \boldsymbol{w}; o, \boldsymbol{y}) .\)

This identifies the final scalar coefficients with one fixed covariant field prescription, independent of phase, spectators and truncation. It does not yet estimate the difference from the original cluster correlation. That quantitative difference is the next proof obligation.

7.3Centered bounds, phase bounds, and matched discrepancies

The raw OPE estimates must hold in every character phase, including phases of zero measure for the original mixture. An estimate of the mixed-state expectation alone does not give this. We prove the required transfer by bounding all even moments of a compressed whole-word operator. The cutoff argument keeps a matched discrepancy as a single observable throughout; it never replaces its small bound by separate bounds for its two terms.

We use the finite-volume planted inequality and the change-of-cell root estimate established in Subsection 4.1. In the latter, a centered observable whose root norm is at most \(B\) can be moved, with the corresponding reflection or conjugation, into a coarser cell at a margin of three of its original cell spans, still with root norm at most \(B\). The proof uses the entire reflected copy orbit and coordinatewise log convexity. These statements apply to any observable satisfying the centered interior and root conditions, including a difference of two observables.

7.3.1A mixed two-scale chessboard estimate

Let \(W_0, W\) be aligned link tilings of a finite torus, with the side of \(W_0\) dividing that of \(W\). Write \(\mathcal{R}_W\) for the root norm. Suppose fine-group observables \(F_j\) have centered bounds \(A_j \geqslant 0\) at a span dividing the side of \(W_0\), and their positions \(u_j\) lie in the required three-span margins of \(W_0\). Suppose further that

\(\displaystyle \text{dist}_{\infty} (u_j, u_{j'}) > \text{side} (W_0) \quad (j \ne j') .\)

For one fixed cell \(C\) of \(W\), collect the fine-group observables lying in \(C\). Their product is a copy in \(C\) of an interior observable \(Z_C\), and

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R}_W (Z_C) \leqslant \prod_{u_j \in C} A_j .\) (7.16)

Indeed pull each factor back to the home cell of \(W\), with the reflection and conjugation determined by \(C\). Now copy this product into every cell of \(W\). The copied positions still lie in the corresponding fine-tiling margins, since the tilings are aligned and their sides divide. They occupy distinct cells of \(W_0\). Points copied into different cells of \(W\) cannot share a fine cell; within one coarse cell, reflection preserves distances, and the displayed separation prevents sharing a fine cell. The planted inequality on \(W_0\) therefore bounds the full copied-product expectation by

\(\displaystyle \left( \prod_{u_j \in C} A_j \right)^{\# \text{Cell} (W)} .\)

Taking the defining root proves (7.16). Interiority of the home product follows by multiplying interior factors.

Let a second group \(G_i\) have centered bounds \(B_i \geqslant 0\) at spans dividing the side of \(W\), with positions \(v_i\) in the corresponding margins of \(W\). Assume their mutual distances, and their distances to all \(u_j\), exceed \(\text{side} (W)\). The \(G_i\) then occupy distinct coarse cells, and none of those cells contains an \(F_j\). Apply the planted inequality on \(W\) to the individual \(G_i\) and the products \(Z_C\). This gives

\(\displaystyle \left. \left. \left| \mathbb{E} \left[ \prod_i G_i \right( \cdot + v_i \right) \prod_j F_j (\cdot + u_j) \right] \right| \leqslant \prod_i B_i \prod_j A_j .\) (7.17)

Grouping by cells loses no factors: the disjoint groups of indices \(\{j : u_j \in C\}\) partition the fine-group list. Empty groups contribute one.

Here are sufficient geometric conditions for placing both groups. Use depths \(b \leqslant f \leqslant l \leqslant n\) and \(f \leqslant q\). The coarse tiling is at depth \(b\), the fine tiling at depth \(l\), the centered bounds for the coarse group at depth \(q\), and those for the fine group at depth \(n\). If the group sizes are \(I_0, J_0\) and

\(\displaystyle 10 I_0 a_f < a_b, \qquad 6 J_0 a_n < a_l,\) (7.18)

there is a common origin for both tilings placing all coarse-group points at margin \(3 a_q\) and all fine-group points at margin \(3 a_n\). To prove this, first choose the coarse origin coordinate by coordinate. For each point, origins giving distance less than \(5 a_f\) from a cell boundary occupy an interval of length at most \(10 a_f\) in the circle of possible origins modulo \(a_b\). The first inequality leaves an allowed origin. Next choose the fine origin modulo \(a_l\) avoiding the intervals of total length at most \(6 J_0 a_n\) forbidden by the fine-group points. It can be reached from the chosen coarse origin by a shift of coordinate size at most \(a_l \leqslant a_f\). This shift decreases the coarse margins by at most \(2 a_f\) even with the endpoint conventions for link cells, leaving \(3 a_f \geqslant 3 a_q\). On the finite lattice, if a cell side is \(B\) and the desired integer margin is \(r\), a point forbids the stripe residues \(0, \ldots, r - 1\) and \(B - r, \ldots, B - 1\): at most \(2 r\) residues of the origin modulo \(B\). Summing over the points leaves a permitted origin whenever their total count is less than \(B\). Multiplying (7.18) by \(a_N^{- 1}\) gives precisely these strict inequalities, first with margin \(5 a_f / a_N\), then with margin \(3 a_n / a_N\). To attain the permitted fine origin, choose a common lattice shift with \(0 \leqslant s_{\mu} < a_l / a_N\) in each coordinate. The coarse \(5 a_f / a_N\) margin loses at most \(2 a_f / a_N\), leaving the required three spans. Nested mesh divisibility makes all sides and margins integers.

A Euclidean separation greater than \(2 a_b\) implies a maximum-coordinate separation greater than \(a_b\) in four dimensions; likewise for \(a_l\). For fixed physical grid points these inequalities persist on the periodic lattice for all sufficiently large periods: their finitely many coordinate differences then do not wrap. They persist at all finer cutoffs by the exact mesh embedding. Thus Euclidean separation at \(2 a_b\) within and across the coarse group, and at \(2 a_l\) within the fine group, suffices for (7.17) on the ordered cutoff tail.

7.3.2Even replicas and the norm in the ground algebra

Let \(Z\) be a finite linear combination of products of local fields, at fixed coordinate-generic grid configurations. Write \(A \in \mathcal{A}\) for the same linear combination of their compressed whole-word operators. The cutoff version \(Z_{N, p}\) may use labels converging in their fixed finite-dimensional label spaces along a nontrivial refinement of the ordered cutoff limit.

Suppose each cutoff \(Z_{N, p}\) factors as the product of two groups as in (7.17), with the same centered estimates and geometry. The statement includes a group consisting of a single matched discrepancy. Set \(C = \prod_i B_i \prod_j A_j\). For each \(m \geqslant 1\), translate \(2 m\) copies of \(Z_{N, p}\) by the even coarse-cell displacements

\(\displaystyle d_j (t) = 2 tj \hspace{0.17em} a_b (1, 1, 1, 1), \qquad t \in \mathbb{N}, \quad 0 \leqslant j < 2 m,\)

conjugating the copies with even \(j\). These translations preserve all tilings' margins, because they are multiples of the coarse cell side, and this side is a multiple of the fine side. For fixed \(m\), copies at distinct indices become separated in every coordinate as \(t \to \infty\). For each sufficiently large fixed \(t\), take the period large enough to avoid wrapping, and then the cutoff fine enough to use all the centered bounds. Applying (7.17) to the replicated lists gives

\(\displaystyle \left| \mathbb{E}_{N, p} \prod_{j = 0}^{2 m - 1} \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \overline{Z_{N, p} (\cdot + d_j (t))}, & j \text{ even},\\ Z_{N, p} (\cdot + d_j (t)), & j \text{ odd} \end{array} \right. \right| \leqslant C^{2 m} .\) (7.19)

Only the two original arities occur in (7.18). Once an origin works for one copy, it works for all its even-cell translates. The period and the translation threshold may depend on \(m\); the bound \(C\) does not.

Expand the finite polynomial in (7.19) and first pass to the ordered cutoff limit at fixed \(t\). For sufficiently large \(t\) each expanded word is coordinate-generic, so the point-limit theorem applies. Converging labels cause no extra difficulty: in a fixed basis a multilinear correlation is a finite sum of basis correlations times products of label coordinates. The basis correlations converge and the coordinates converge, proving convergence of each term. This also works on any finer nontrivial cutoff filter. It does not assume that the moving labels have separate limits in the two cutoff indices.

Next let \(t \to \infty\). The conjugated copies are translated, not reflected, so the adjoint law \(M (\theta w) = M (w)^{\ast}\) of Subsection 5.2, which involves reflection, does not apply to them directly. We use instead the unreflected conjugation identity (16.72), \(M (\bar{w}) = M (w)^{\ast}\) for ordinary conjugation of all labels at unchanged points. As recorded there, it is a substantive input obtained from the ordinary-reflection identity \(M (\rho w) = M (w)\) of Proposition 5.26, under that proposition's hypotheses, on this same hierarchy; it is an input of the present section as well. With it, the separated-cluster limit of Lemma 5.9 identifies the limit as

\(\displaystyle \langle \Omega, (A^{\ast} A)^m \Omega \rangle .\)

For a linear combination this follows by expanding the ordered product; its alternating factors are exactly \(A^{\ast}, A, \ldots, A^{\ast}, A\). Thus

\(\displaystyle \int_{\Xi} | \chi (A) |^{2 m} \hspace{0.17em} d \mu (\chi) \leqslant C^{2 m} \quad (m \geqslant 1) .\)

Full support of \(\mu\) now gives \(| \chi (A) | \leqslant C\) for every character. If one value exceeded \(C\), continuity would give an open set of positive measure on which it exceeded some \(C' > C\). Then \(\mu (O) (C')^{2 m} \leqslant C^{2 m}\) for every \(m\), a contradiction. The same argument covers \(C = 0\). The isometric Gelfand transform consequently gives

\(\displaystyle \|A\| \leqslant C.\) (7.20)

Both every character evaluation and the mixed-state functional have norm one, so the same bound holds for their evaluations of \(A\).

7.3.3Removing coordinate ties

To extend (7.20) to arbitrary separated configurations, first approximate all their points by the generic grids of (15.28). Strict Euclidean separations persist. For each fixed character, its correlation is continuous on separated configurations by Proposition 5.20, so the generic bound passes to the limit. This continuity is needed for every character, not only for almost every character, because the conclusion is used for every character and along arbitrary sequences of phases; full support and the barycentre formula alone do not supply it. Proposition 5.20 is conditional on the all-copy bound and the slot identity (5.21); those remain inputs here. Taking the supremum over characters then proves the ground-algebra norm bound at the original configuration. This uses scalar continuity in each phase, not norm continuity of the ground-algebra-valued correlation.

For a matched discrepancy whose cluster is already fixed and generic, only the spectator positions are approximated. They must remain coordinate-generic relative to both that cluster and the origin. Here is the required extension of the grid construction. Let \(W\) be the finite set consisting of the cluster points and the origin, \(I_0\) the number of spectators (the coarse group of (7.18)), and \(K = (I_0 + 1) (|W| + 1)\). In each coordinate choose for spectator \(j\) a residue from the block

\(\displaystyle \{j (|W| + 1), \ldots, j (|W| + 1) + |W|\}\)

which avoids the residues \(\lfloor w_{\mu} / h \rfloor \bmod K\) for \(w \in W\). There are \(|W| + 1\) candidates and at most \(|W|\) forbidden residues. The blocks are disjoint, so the chosen residues distinguish all spectators. Adding the chosen residue to the integer \(K \lfloor y_{j, \mu} / (hK) \rfloor\) and multiplying by the mesh \(h\) gives a grid point within \(hK\) in each coordinate, and it cannot equal any \(w_{\mu}\). Thus these spectator grids converge and retain all required coordinate inequalities. If the new grid is finer than the fixed cluster grid, reexpress the cluster points on it by the integer mesh ratio; their physical positions and cutoff observables are unchanged. Apply the preceding replica argument on that common grid, then take the spectator approximation limit in each phase. This proves the complete-discrepancy bound at arbitrary separated spectator configurations.

7.3.4Engineering rates from centered bounds

We now use the centered estimate in its full quantifier order: for a fixed degree \(d\), bounded set of labels and every \(\epsilon > 0\), one constant controls the centered root bound at all depths after the reference depth, with an eventual cutoff tail uniform in period and those labels. In particular a fixed field of degree \(d_i\) has a bound

\(\displaystyle B_i (k) = (1 + C_i) a_k^{- d_i - \epsilon} + 1\)

for a suitable \(C_i \geqslant 0\). If \(s_{k_n} / t_n \to u \geqslant 0\), this positive bound has logarithmic rate \((d_i + \epsilon) u\). For a finite group the product therefore has rate \((\sum_i d_i + \epsilon \#I) u\).

More generally, suppose two groups have mutual separations at upper scales \(0 \leqslant a \leqslant b\), and cross separation at scale \(a\). This means that for every positive slack the corresponding distances eventually exceed \(e^{- (a + \delta) t_n}\) or \(e^{- (b + \delta) t_n}\). Choose mesh depths of logarithmic rates

\(\displaystyle a + \delta / 3, \quad a + \delta / 2, \quad a + \delta, \quad b + 3 \delta / 4, \quad b + \delta\)

for the coarse placement, buffer, coarse-group bound, fine placement, and fine-group bound respectively. The inequalities of the rates imply all depth orderings and (7.18) eventually. The separation assumptions imply the required strict gaps at the placement meshes. Let \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) be the total field degrees of the coarse group (separated at scale \(a\), of size \(I_0\)) and of the fine group (separated at scale \(b\), of size \(J_0\)). The bounds just proved yield ground-algebra upper rate

\(\displaystyle (w_1 + \epsilon I_0) (a + \delta) + (w_2 + \epsilon J_0) (b + \delta) .\)

Let \(\epsilon, \delta \downarrow 0\) by allocating half of any desired rate slack to these choices and half to the eventual estimate. The result is the upper rate \(w_1 a + w_2 b\), uniformly along every sequence of phases, because the ground-algebra norm controls them all.

For the OPE, take the fixed-clearance spectators as the first group (\(a = 0\)) and the scale-\(x\) cluster as the second (\(b = x\)). Their cross clearance is fixed for all sufficiently large indices. This proves the rough rate \(xw (p)\) used in Subsection 15.6. Taking every group at scale zero proves rate zero for the fixed single-field spectator insertion in Input 15.3.

Finally suppose a cutoff matched discrepancy is centered and priced by \(A_n\) at a depth of logarithmic rate \(b > 0\), and its coefficient label converges along a refinement of the same ordered cutoff limit. Assume \(A_n\) has upper rate \(\gamma\). Choose, for any small \(0 < \delta < b\), placement, buffer, fine-placement and spectator-bound depths of rates \(\delta / 4, \delta / 2, 3 \delta / 4, \delta\). The centered discrepancy bound at rate \(b\) fits this geometry. Equation (7.20) and the spectator approximation give, with the spectators as the coarse group (total degree \(w_1\), number \(I_0\)) and the discrepancy as the single-member fine group, upper rate at most \(\gamma + (w_1 + \epsilon I_0) \delta\) for the entire discrepancy, in every phase and at arbitrary fixed-clearance spectator positions. Sending \(\delta \downarrow 0\) proves rate \(\gamma\). Thus the finite-cutoff match supplies exactly the complete-discrepancy part of Input 15.2, once its bound and convergent label are constructed. Neither this transfer nor the even-moment argument creates that finite-cutoff match.

7.4Triangular transport and the physical filtration

The distance estimate in (15.30) concerns the physical filtration. A bare coefficient of low engineering degree need not stay in the corresponding coordinate plane under renormalization. We first estimate its transported plane, then identify limits of that plane by their correlations. The two parts use different information: triangular estimates for the first, and the comparison of local observables for the second.

7.4.1Expansion of the low coordinates

Fix a degree \(d\), its finite coefficient space \(F\), and an integer \(0 \leqslant e < d\). Each coordinate has an engineering weight in \(\{0, \ldots, d\}\). Write \(\mu (v) = \sum_{\alpha} |v_{\alpha} |\) and let \(P\) retain the coordinates of weight at most \(e\). For a block ratio \(L \geqslant 1\), let

\(\displaystyle (\textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L} v)_{\alpha} = L^{d - \deg \alpha} v_{\alpha} .\)

Thus \(P\) commutes with \(\textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L}\) and

\(\displaystyle \mu (P \textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L} v) \geqslant L^{d - e} \mu (Pv), \qquad \mu ((1 - P) \textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L} v) \leqslant L^{d - e - 1} \mu (v) .\) (7.21)

Both inequalities follow by summing the coordinate inequalities.

Here is the exact one-step input needed from the calibrated construction. At consecutive levels \(i + 1, i\), the readouts of the same bare vector are \(a, b\). There is an invertible linear map \(B\) preserving \(\ker P\), with

\(\displaystyle \mu (Bv), \mu (B^{- 1} v) \leqslant K \mu (v), \qquad \mu (b - B \textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L} a) \leqslant \epsilon \mu (a) .\)

The last estimate follows in the construction from the stronger bound with the nonconstant-coordinate part of \(a\) on the right. Its numerical conditions are

\(\displaystyle K \geqslant 1, \quad s_0, s \geqslant 64 K^2, \quad s \leqslant L, \quad \epsilon \geqslant 0, \quad 2 K \epsilon (1 + s) \leqslant 1.\) (7.22)

Here \(s_0 = \zeta_i\) and \(s = \zeta_{i + 1}\) are the column allowances. These triangular estimates concern the actual readouts, including their nonlinear corrections. On the selected family their hypotheses follow from the linear coefficient map and the stored backward transfer, as follows.

Lemma 7.4. (The triangular step on the selected family) Use the reference-normalized transfer of Proposition 4.16 at grade \(d\) and an adjacent step above its reference level. Its consecutive readouts satisfy the preceding one-step hypotheses and (7.22) for every \(e < d\), with the same readouts and coefficient matrix for all these \(e\).

Proof. The matrix \(B\) in Subsection 10.9 is the grade-mixing factor of this transition. Its off-diagonal entry \(B_{bi}\) can be nonzero only when \(g_i < g_b\); its diagonal entries are nonzero. Consequently a vector supported on grades greater than \(e\) is sent to one supported on grades greater than \(e\). This proves preservation of \(\ker P\). Equation 10.44 bounds both \(B\) and its inverse by a stage constant \(K \geqslant 1\), which the purchased matrix bound \(D\) dominates. The full linear map is \(B \textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L}\), with the factor \(L^{d - g_i}\) in coordinate \(i\).

Proposition 13.34 constructs the lower coefficient family from the upper one using this map and the common interpolation of Proposition 4.43. Its lead error is at most \(\epsilon \| \text{core} \hspace{0.17em} a\|_1\), where \(\epsilon = \eta (1 + s) R_F \geqslant 0\) in (4.40). Thus it is at most \(\epsilon \mu (a)\). Reference normalization precomposes both readouts with the same inverse and leaves this inequality valid for every bare vector; it does not replace \(B\) or either adjacent coefficient family.

Put \(s_0 = C_i\) and \(s = C_{i + 1}\) for the two column allowances. The room and bank estimates of Proposition 13.3, as used in Proposition 13.34, give

\(\displaystyle 64 D^2 \leqslant s_0, s, \qquad 100000 D^6 (1 + s)^2 \leqslant L^{1 / 8} .\)

In particular \(K \geqslant 1\), \(s_0, s \geqslant 64 K^2\), and \(s \leqslant L\). The interpolation purchase gives \(\epsilon \leqslant L^{- 3 / 8}\). Put \(q = L^{1 / 8} \geqslant 1\). Its bank bound implies \(q \geqslant 2 K (1 + s)\), and hence

\(\displaystyle 2 K \epsilon (1 + s) \leqslant 2 K (1 + s) q^{- 3} \leqslant q^{- 2} \leqslant 1.\)

Thus the factor \(1 + s\) in the actual lead error and the further factor \(1 + s\) in the cone condition have both been paid. These are all the numerical conditions in (7.22). The construction fixes the heading, coefficient families and bounds before \(e\) is selected, so the same bounds apply to each of the finitely many low-coordinate planes.\(\Box\)

Since \(B\) maps the finite-dimensional space \(\ker P\) injectively into itself, it maps it onto itself. Its inverse therefore also preserves \(\ker P\). Applying this observation to \(Bv - PBv\) gives

\(\displaystyle \mu (Pv) = \mu (PB^{- 1} PBv) \leqslant K \mu (PBv) .\) (7.23)

Suppose now that \(\mu (a) \leqslant s \mu (Pa)\), and set \(Y = \mu (P \textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L} a)\). By (7.21) and \(s \leqslant L\),

\(\displaystyle \mu (\textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L} a) \leqslant Y + L^{d - e - 1} s \mu (Pa) \leqslant 2 Y, \qquad \mu (a) \leqslant sY.\)

The error \(E = \mu (b - B \textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L} a)\) consequently satisfies \(2 KE \leqslant Y\). On the other hand, (7.23) gives \(Y \leqslant K (\mu (Pb) + E)\), so \(Y \leqslant 2 K \mu (Pb)\). It follows that

\(\displaystyle \mu (b) \leqslant 3 KY \leqslant 6 K^2 \mu (Pb) \leqslant s_0 \mu (Pb), \qquad L^{d - e} \mu (Pa) \leqslant s \mu (Pb) .\) (7.24)

If instead \(Pa = 0\), (7.21), \(\epsilon \leqslant 1\), and \(\mu (a) \leqslant \mu (\textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L} a)\) give

\(\displaystyle \mu (b) \leqslant (K + 1) \mu (\textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L} a) \leqslant sL^{d - e - 1} \mu (a) .\) (7.25)

The lost power of \(L\) distinguishes the transverse estimate from the expansion of the low coordinates.

7.4.2Subtracting the transported low plane

Fix a finest level \(N\), a period, and a reference level \(U \leqslant N\). Let \(T_k : F \to F\) be the linear readout at level \(k\), for \(U \leqslant k \leqslant N\). Thus \(T_N = 1\) and \(T_U = K_N\), where \(K_N\) is the invertible map to reference coefficients. For each adjacent pair assume the preceding one-step estimates, with \(a_k = L_k a_{k + 1}\) for the meshes. Define

\(\displaystyle J_k = \prod_{i < k} \zeta_{i + 1}, \qquad H_k = \prod_{i < k} (1 + \zeta_i) \zeta_{i + 1} .\)

All these factors are at least one. If \(Pv = v\), backward induction in (7.24) gives

\(\displaystyle \mu (T_k v) \leqslant \zeta_k \mu (PT_k v), \qquad J_k (a_k / a_N)^{d - e} \mu (v) \leqslant J_N \mu (PT_k v) .\) (7.26)

For the second inequality, multiply the one-step lower expansion by \(J_i\), use \(J_{i + 1} = \zeta_{i + 1} J_i\), and telescope the mesh ratios. The terminal equality \(T_N v = v\) starts both inductions.

In particular \(PT_k\) restricted to \(\text{ran} \hspace{0.17em} P\) is injective. Its domain and codomain have equal finite dimension, so it is onto. For any \(y \in F\) choose \(v \in \text{ran} \hspace{0.17em} P\) with \(PT_k v = PT_k y\). The first bound in (7.26) shows

\(\displaystyle PT_k (y - v) = 0, \qquad \mu (T_k (y - v)) \leqslant (1 + \zeta_k) \mu (T_k y) .\) (7.27)

This cancellation must be repeated after each step: the fixed coordinate complement is not asserted to be invariant under the exact readouts. If \(PT_{i + 1} y = 0\), first apply (7.25), then (7.27) at level \(i\). The cost is \((1 + \zeta_i) \zeta_{i + 1} L_i^{d - e - 1}\). Induction therefore gives, whenever \(U \leqslant k \leqslant j \leqslant N\) and \(PT_j y = 0\), a \(v \in \text{ran} \hspace{0.17em} P\) such that

\(\displaystyle PT_k (y - v) = 0, \qquad H_k \mu (T_k (y - v)) \leqslant H_j (a_k / a_j)^{d - e - 1} \mu (T_j y) .\)

At an induction step add the newly subtracted low vector to the old one; their sum remains in \(\text{ran} \hspace{0.17em} P\). The recurrence for \(H_k\) and the identity \(a_i = L_i a_{i + 1}\) give the displayed product.

Cancel first at level \(k\), then transport to \(U\), and discard the factor \(H_U \geqslant 1\). For every reference vector \(x\) this yields a vector \(y\) in the transported plane \(K_N \text{ran} \hspace{0.17em} P\) with

\(\displaystyle \|x - y\| \leqslant F_{k, d, e} \hspace{0.17em} q_k (x), \qquad F_{k, d, e} = H_k (1 + \zeta_k) (a_U / a_k)^{d - e - 1},\) (7.28)

where \(q_k (x) = \mu (T_k K_N^{- 1} x)\), and the reference norm is chosen so that \(\|z\| \leqslant \mu (z)\). Indeed apply the preceding result to \(K_N^{- 1} x\) and use \(T_U = K_N\). For comparison, the calibrated bound \(q_i (x) \leqslant \zeta_{i + 1} L_i^d q_{i + 1} (x)\) gives by forward induction

\(\displaystyle \|x\| \leqslant J_k (a_U / a_k)^d q_k (x) .\) (7.29)

At \(U\) use \(q_U (x) = \mu (x)\) and \(J_U \geqslant 1\); the recurrence for \(J\) and the mesh identity give each subsequent step.

7.4.3Subpower calibration and smeared discrepancies

The cumulative column factor used above is subpower for a precise reason. Put \(s_i = \log (1 / a_i) \geqslant 0\) and \(C_N = \sum_{i < N} \log \zeta_{i + 1} = \log J_N\). The schedule provides numbers \(h_i \to \infty\) and the bound

\(\displaystyle 0 \leqslant \log \zeta_{i + 1} \leqslant h_i^{- 2} \log L_i, \qquad s_{i + 1} - s_i = \log L_i > 0.\)

The first inequality follows also when the scheduled logarithmic cost contains other nonnegative charges, since it then dominates \(\log \zeta_{i + 1}\). Given \(\epsilon > 0\), choose \(k\) so that \(h_i^{- 2} \leqslant \epsilon / 2\) for \(i \geqslant k\). Telescoping gives

\(\displaystyle C_N \leqslant C_k + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2} (s_N - s_k) \leqslant C_k + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2} s_N .\)

Eventually \(C_k \leqslant \epsilon s_N / 2\), because \(s_N \to \infty\). Thus \(C_N = o (s_N)\). In particular \(J_N\) has logarithmic rate zero; so does \(\zeta_N\), since \(1 \leqslant \zeta_N \leqslant J_N\) for \(N \geqslant 1\).

The calibrated reverse estimate is

\(\displaystyle \mu (T_{i + 1} K_N^{- 1} x) \leqslant \zeta_{i + 1} \mu (T_i K_N^{- 1} x) .\)

Starting at \(T_U K_N^{- 1} x = x\) and enlarging the product to the full prefix gives \(\mu (T_i K_N^{- 1} x) \leqslant J_i \mu (x)\). At \(i = N\), therefore,

\(\displaystyle \|K_N^{- 1} x\| \leqslant J_N \mu (x) \leqslant J_N \hspace{0.17em} \dim (F) \hspace{0.17em} \|x\|,\)

using the coordinate supremum norm for the last inequality. For every fixed radius \(R\) and \(\epsilon > 0\), both \(J_N\) and \(\dim (F) R\) are eventually bounded by \(e^{\epsilon s_N / 2}\). Consequently

\(\displaystyle \sup_p \sup_{\|x\| \leqslant R} \|K_{N, p}^{- 1} x\| \leqslant e^{\epsilon s_N} \quad \text{eventually in } N.\) (7.30)

The same estimate applies to any bounded family along the ordered cutoff filter. The calibration inequalities are uniform in the period; no new period extraction is needed.

We will also use the following passage from separated lattice bounds to smeared tests. Let a trial \(Z_{N, p}\) replace one distinguished field in an \(n\)-point correlation, and suppose that, for each fixed spectator list and every \(\delta > 0\), its correlation has absolute value at most \(A_{\delta} |r_{N, p} |\) whenever all physical pairwise separations are at least \(\delta\). Assume \(r_{N, p} \to 0\) along the ordered cutoff filter. For every smooth test \(\varphi\) compactly supported in the separated configuration space, the corresponding lattice sum, with the factor \(a_N^{4 n}\), tends to zero.

Indeed the test support \(K\) is compact and has positive distance from every pairwise diagonal. Choose a smaller clearance \(\delta > 0\). For sufficiently large periods, its lattice representatives have that clearance also in the periodic metric: a bounded set does not meet a periodic translate of itself near a diagonal. Express the weighted lattice sum exactly as the integral of its piecewise constant function on the mesh cells in the fundamental region. A nonzero cell value has its corner in \(K\). The cell diameter tends to zero, so every such cell lies in a fixed compact enlargement \(B\) of \(K\). The absolute value of the step function is at most

\(\displaystyle \boldsymbol{1}_B \hspace{0.17em} \| \varphi \|_{\infty} A_{\delta} |r_{N, p} | .\)

Its integral is bounded by \(|B| \| \varphi \|_{\infty} A_{\delta} |r_{N, p} |\), which tends to zero. This is a norm estimate on the same ordered filter, not an interchange of mesh and volume limits. Coordinate ties do not cause a problem: the test support is separated in the Euclidean sense, and one separating coordinate for each pair suffices to control its periodic separation.

7.4.4Identifying the limiting plane by tests

We spell out the finite-dimensional limit argument, since convergence of the moving planes themselves is neither needed nor asserted. Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional complex normed space, \(p_c\) projections converging in operator norm to a projection \(p\), and \(f_{\theta, c}\) linear functionals converging in norm to \(f_{\theta}\), one for each test \(\theta\). Assume

\(\displaystyle \ker p = \bigcap_{\theta} \ker f_{\theta} .\) (7.31)

The index \(c\) tends along any nontrivial filter. Suppose vectors \(u_c\) satisfy, for every \(\theta\),

\(\displaystyle f_{\theta, c} (u_c) \longrightarrow M_{\theta}, \qquad \frac{f_{\theta, c} (u_c - p_c u_c)}{1 + \|p_c u_c \|} \longrightarrow 0.\) (7.32)

Then there is \(u \in \text{ran} \hspace{0.17em} p\) with \(f_{\theta} (u) = M_{\theta}\) for all \(\theta\).

To prove this, put \(b_c = (1 +\|p_c u_c \|)^{- 1}\) and \(z_c = b_c p_c u_c\). The pairs \((b_c, z_c)\) lie in the compact set \([0, 1] \times \bar{B}_1\). Along a finer nontrivial filter they converge to \((b, z)\), with \(b + \|z\| = 1\). Since \(p_c z_c = z_c\), convergence of the projectors gives \(pz = z\). Equations (7.32) imply \(f_{\theta} (z) = bM_{\theta}\). If \(b = 0\), (7.31) forces \(pz = 0\), hence \(z = 0\), contradicting \(b + \|z\| = 1\). Thus \(b > 0\) and \(u = b^{- 1} z\) proves the claim. There is only one compact extraction; the limits for every test pass to its refinement. No extraction indexed by the tests is used.

Here is how the required hypotheses arise for the transported plane. A local trial at degree \(e\) is a family of observables \(Z_{N, p}\), indexed by cutoff and period along the ordered cutoff filter, with the following properties on the late tail of that filter. (i) Locality: there is one fixed finite stencil \(X\) containing the origin such that each \(Z_{N, p}\) is a measurable gauge-invariant function of the link variables in the translate of \(X\) at its lattice origin, and the observable at any other origin is the literal translate of the one at the origin. (ii) Eventual continuity: \(Z_{N, p}\) is continuous for all sufficiently late cutoffs. (iii) Two bounds: there are nonnegative bounds \(c_{N, p}\) and \(b_{N, p}\) with subpower upper bounds along that filter: for every \(\epsilon > 0\) both are eventually at most \(a_N^{- \epsilon}\). On a common late tail, \(a_N^e |Z_{N, p} | \leqslant b_{N, p}\) everywhere, and \(a_N^e Z_{N, p}\) satisfies the prescribed left-derivative bounds with constant \(c_{N, p}\) on the healthy configurations, those at which no site of the stencil is incident to a plaquette whose chord distance from the identity exceeds the health threshold of the cutoff; no derivative bound is required on unhealthy configurations. In addition, at every healthy configuration \(U\), the scaled trial \(u = a_N^e Z_{N, p}\) has a \(C^{q_N}\) germ at zero in the full real left-increment variables \(A\), namely \(A \mapsto u (\exp (A) U)\), where \(q_N\) is the purchased order. This condition includes configurations at equality in the health threshold. For every ordered list of fewer than \(q_N\) directions and every further direction, the derivative at zero of the iterated left derivative along that increment exists and equals the recursively defined next left derivative. These regularity and recursive derivative conditions are separate from the bound bounds; they are not required at unhealthy configurations. These bounds are uniform in the lattice origin. Here the derivative order, spatial weight power and direction threshold are the purchased cutoff-dependent parameters: the bound holds simultaneously for every ordered left derivative of length at most that cutoff's purchased order, with directions satisfying its weighted threshold. Bounds only for each fixed derivative order would not suffice. The local comparison construction supplies degree-\(e\) labels \(u_c\) with subpower bare norm, whose difference from that trial has a centered bound tending to zero. It also supplies the projections \(p_c \to p\) of (7.31). Labels in \(\ker p_c\) with subpower bare norm have vanishing tested correlations. Bounded reference labels have subpower bare norm by (7.30). The local comparison is proved below from the protected local construction. The projections come from the equation-kernel and rank construction on this same table, as specified in the next paragraph. All statements use the same ordered cutoff filter.

Subsection 16.12 supplies projections of this kind: its idempotents \(A_c\) converge to \(T\) with \(\ker T = \bigcap_{\theta} \ker f_{\theta}\), by (16.91), so one may take \(p_c = A_c\) and \(p = T\) in (7.31). Its vanishing statement (16.86) is displayed for bounded families. The argument there applies verbatim to families of subpower bare (inverse-calibrated) norm, since its local equation input is stated for such families and boundedness is used only to enter that input. The use here requires that subpower form, for the same projections, tests and filter. That section in turn rests on its own inputs: its local equation input, the purchased comparison (16.88) and the equation count (16.90). The local trial comparison is the following application of the protected insertion construction.

Proposition 7.5. (Comparison of a protected local trial) On the same funded calibrated family used in Subsection 16.11, let \(Z_{N, p}\) be a degree-\(e\) local trial satisfying (i)–(iii) above. There are grade-\(e\) reference labels \(u_{N, p}\) with subpower inverse terminal coordinates such that, for every fixed opened pair of depths eligible at grade \(e\), the centered root bound of \(Z_{N, p} - O_e (u_{N, p})\) tends to zero along the ordered cutoff filter. The discrepancy tends to zero uniformly on each fixed separated configuration region after multiplying by any fixed finite spectator list. It also tends to zero after smearing with any compactly supported smooth separated test. The same labels serve every such test and all refinements of the original order.

Proof. Set \(f_{N, p} = a_N^e Z_{N, p}\) at the lattice origin. Conditions (i)–(iii) are precisely the two protected bounds and the local regularity required in Subsection 16.11. The regularity is available through the actual purchased order, not merely any previously fixed finite order. Enlarge the fixed trial stencil by the grade-\(e\) section stencil; this set is still finite and independent of cutoff and period. On a late tail the purchased order is large enough to take the grade-\(e\) readout.

Apply the terminal construction (16.84) to this \(f\). It subtracts its own leading section so that the regular remainder has zero readout; no zero-readout condition is imposed on the original trial. Its exact density is \(a_N^e R_N Z_{N, p}\), with the actual unmarked top representative \(R_N\). The total terminal bound is \(E_{N, p}\) of (16.85). That formula is a finite sum and product of the trial's two bounds, the purchased column and threshold bounds, the support weight, and the paid reserve inverse. Every one of these factors is subpower on this family, so \(E_{N, p}\) is subpower on the same ordered filter. The actual top keeper supplies the off-health attachment. The trial itself is eventually continuous, even though individual masked pieces need not be; this is the continuity required in the descent.

The descent in Subsection 16.11 now produces one reference label \(u_{N, p}\), with

\(\displaystyle |X_{N, p}^{- 1} u_{N, p} |_1 \leqslant 2 E_{N, p}, \qquad \mathcal{R}_{m, N, p} \{Z_{N, p} - O_e (u_{N, p})\} \leqslant C_{e, m, k} E_{N, p} e^{- s_N / 8}\)

for every fixed eligible opened pair \((m, k)\) on its eventual tail. The label is defined by the descent before an opened pair or a spectator test is chosen. At excluded initial cutoffs take it to be zero. To verify decay, use subpower with exponent \(1 / 16\) and \(s_N \to \infty\): the last bound is eventually at most \(C_{e, m, k} e^{- s_N / 16} \to 0\). Constants and mesh thresholds may depend on the fixed opened pair and period as prescribed by the ordered filter. No period-uniform mesh threshold is being introduced.

Both terms of the discrepancy have one fixed translated stencil. The common-tiling argument and (16.82) therefore bound a correlation with any fixed separated spectator tuple by the same vanishing root bound times a constant depending on that tuple and its separation. The step-integral estimate proved above gives vanishing of its smeared correlations. Choosing a refinement preserves the eventual inequalities, the subpower bounds and these limits; it does not require a new comparison label.\(\Box\)

For clarity, these inputs imply the second limit in (7.32) without assuming \(u_c\) bounded. Multiplication by \(b_c = (1 +\|p_c u_c \|)^{- 1} \leqslant 1\) preserves subpower bare norm. The vectors \(b_c p_c u_c\) are bounded, so also have subpower bare norm. Their difference \(b_c (u_c - p_c u_c)\) consequently has subpower bare norm and belongs to \(\ker p_c\), because \(p_c^2 = p_c\). The vanishing-test assertion gives exactly the normalized residual limit. The first limit follows by comparing the trial with \(u_c\): the centered estimate gives a separated correlation bound by a constant times the bound, and the step-integral estimate just proved sends this bound to zero in every smeared correlation. Linearity here is legitimate at finite cutoff, since the trials are bounded measurable functions and the remaining fields are continuous on the compact configuration space.

It remains to check the degree of a bounded transported low vector \(v_c \in K_N \text{ran} \hspace{0.17em} P\). Write \(x_c = K_N^{- 1} v_c\). Taking \(k = U\) in (7.26) gives

\(\displaystyle a_N^{- (d - e)} \|x_c \| \leqslant a_U^{- (d - e)} J_N \mu (v_c) .\)

Subsubsection 7.4.3 gives zero logarithmic rate for \(J_N\). Thus the bare coefficients of \(w_c = a_N^{- (d - e)} v_c\) have subpower norm. We check all the local-trial conditions for this moving row. Put \(\xi_c = X_{d, N, p}^{- 1} w_c\), so the preceding bound makes \(\xi_c\) subpower. Its unnormalized local function is \(L_d (\Delta_d (\vartheta_N^{- 1}) \xi_c)\) on the fixed section stencil. The paid column estimate gives derivative bound \(C_N | \xi_c |_1\) through the actual order \(q_N\) and at its actual spatial power. The global bound is \(A_d | \Delta_d (\vartheta_N^{- 1}) \xi_c |_1\), where \(A_d\) is the fixed cylinder bound of Subsection 16.11. Both are subpower: \(C_N\) and every fixed threshold power are subpower, and there are finitely many factor degrees at grade \(d\). This uses the paid column estimate at \(q_N\), not a sequence of bounds with uncontrolled constants at increasing orders. The section is a polynomial in finitely many links. Thus it is continuous, literally translates with its anchor, and in all real left-increment variables it is smooth on a neighborhood of every configuration. In particular the required \(C^{q_N}\) germ and the recursive ordered-left-derivative identities hold at healthy configurations, including their boundary. Gauge invariance is preserved by the scalar coefficient row. Finally linearity gives

\(\displaystyle a_N^d \mathcal{O}_d (w_c) = a_N^e \mathcal{O}_d (v_c) .\)

Thus the literal observable \(\mathcal{O}_d (v_c)\), not a replacement with different support, is a degree-\(e\) trial. Apply Proposition 7.5. If \(v_c \to v\) along a refinement, its tested correlations converge to those of the reference label \(v\): convergence of linear tests on a fixed basis is convergence in operator norm. Apply the preceding comparison and compact argument to obtain a degree-\(e\) label having all the same smeared correlations. The smooth correlation kernels consequently agree: their difference integrates to zero against every compactly supported smooth test on the separated configuration domain, hence vanishes there. By the physical null-space definition, the class of \(v\) belongs to \(E_e\).

Only boundedness and low-plane membership on the refined filter are needed. To apply comparison inputs stated on the original filter, replace \(v_c\) by zero whenever either \(\|v_c \| > \|v\| + 1\) or low-plane membership fails. This modified family is bounded and in the plane on the original filter, and equals \(v_c\) eventually on the refinement. It has the same limit and the same limiting correlations there.

7.4.5The distance estimate after extraction

Suppose reference labels \(v_c \to v\) on a refinement of the ordered cutoff filter and \(q_k (v_c) \leqslant V\) eventually, with \(k\) fixed. Choose \(y_c \in K_N \text{ran} \hspace{0.17em} P\) by (7.28). They are eventually bounded, because \(\|y_c \| \leqslant \|v_c \| + F_{k, d, e} V\). Extract a further nontrivial filter on which \(y_c \to y\). The preceding argument puts \([y]\) in \(E_e\), and continuity of the norm gives

\(\displaystyle [y] \in E_e, \qquad \|v - y\| \leqslant F_{k, d, e} V.\) (7.33)

The whole-vector bound (7.29) passes to the limit directly. Both estimates hold for the same limit \(v\); only the auxiliary approximant \(y\) requires a further extraction.

Finally these finite-scale factors have the advertised rates. By Subsubsection 7.4.3, \(\log J_k = o (s_k)\) and \(\log \zeta_k = o (s_k)\), where \(s_k = \log (1 / a_k)\). The column allowances also satisfy \(\zeta_k \geqslant 2\). In fact

\(\displaystyle H_k \zeta_k \leqslant J_k^3 \zeta_0 .\)

This holds at \(k = 0\). For the induction step use \(1 + \zeta_k \leqslant \zeta_k \zeta_{k + 1}\) and the recurrences for \(H, J\):

\(\displaystyle H_{k + 1} \zeta_{k + 1} \leqslant H_k \zeta_k \zeta_{k + 1}^3 \leqslant J_k^3 \zeta_0 \zeta_{k + 1}^3 = J_{k + 1}^3 \zeta_0 .\)

Thus \(H_k\) also has zero logarithmic rate. If \(k_n \to \infty\), \(t_n \to + \infty\), and \(s_{k_n} / t_n \to a\), then \(F_{k_n, d, e}\) has rate \((d - e - 1) a\), while the factor in (7.29) has rate \(da\). Taking \(d = q\) and \(e = m - 1\) proves precisely the factors in (15.29)–(15.30), once a cutoff match with quota \(V_n\) and convergent reference labels has been produced. This argument preserves the exact reference labels and physical filtration; it does not require convergence of the individual renormalization matrices.

7.5The weighted support estimate for grouping

The loss in (15.21) pays for two effects. Moving several marks to a common representative enlarges the supports carrying them. Expanding the resulting product then creates connected unions of these enlarged supports. We prove the two counting estimates that control these operations. They apply to distinct activity labels; no claim about a logarithmic expansion with repeated labels is needed.

7.5.1Connected unions with distinct labels

Let \(X_i\) be finite supports in a four-dimensional periodic lattice, indexed by an arbitrary set, and let \(c_M (X)\) be their connected covering number at a positive integer block size \(M\): the least number of lattice balls of radius \(M\) whose union covers \(X\) and is connected by unit steps. This is the covering number in the support bounds of the local representations. We use its elementary properties

\(\displaystyle |X| \leqslant (2 M + 1)^4 c_M (X), \qquad c_M (X \cup Y) \leqslant c_M (X) + c_M (Y) \quad \text{if } X \cap Y \ne \varnothing .\)

Let \(f_i \geqslant 0\), \(a \geqslant 0\), \(\gamma \geqslant 0\), and suppose that, for every site \(x\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i : x \in X_i} f_i e^{(a + v) c_M (X_i)} \leqslant \gamma, \qquad (2 M + 1)^4 \gamma \leqslant v.\) (7.34)

All sums of nonnegative terms have their supremum-of-finite-sums meaning. A nonempty finite label set \(A\) is connected when its intersection graph, with an edge for overlapping supports, is connected. Then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\text{\scriptsize{$\begin{array}{c} A \text{ finite, connected}\\ x \in \bigcup_{i \in A} X_i \end{array}$}}} e^{ac_M (\bigcup_{i \in A} X_i)} \prod_{i \in A} f_i \leqslant e^{\gamma} - 1.\) (7.35)

We prove this by a finite recursion. For a finite set of allowed labels \(D\) and a support \(Y\), let \(\mathcal{P} (D, Y)\) consist of all subsets of \(D\) whose every connected component has a support meeting \(Y\); the empty subset is included. Set

\(\displaystyle F (D, Y) = \sum_{A \in \mathcal{P} (D, Y)} \prod_{i \in A} p_i, \qquad p_i = f_i e^{ac_M (X_i)} .\)

The following inequality retains each label at most once:

\(\displaystyle F (D, Y) \leqslant \prod_{\text{\scriptsize{$\begin{array}{c} i \in D\\ X_i \cap Y \ne \varnothing \end{array}$}}} (1 + p_i F (D \setminus \{i\}, X_i)) .\) (7.36)

To see it, choose for each connected component of \(A\) one label whose support meets \(Y\). Different components choose different labels. Deleting the chosen label leaves a set every one of whose connected components meets its support: follow a path to the deleted vertex in the old component. Record that remaining set at the chosen label, and record the empty option at every unchosen label. The union of the recorded sets with their chosen labels recovers \(A\), so the encoding is injective. Its weight is exactly \(\prod_{i \in A} p_i\). Expanding the product on the right of (7.36) permits additional choices, possibly sharing labels between different options; all their weights are nonnegative. This proves the inequality without introducing factorial or ordering losses.

Strong induction on \(|D|\) gives

\(\displaystyle F (D, Y) \leqslant e^{|Y| \gamma} \leqslant e^{vc_M (Y)} .\)

Indeed insert the induction bound in (7.36) and use \(1 + r \leqslant e^r\):

\(\displaystyle F (D, Y) \leqslant \exp \hspace{-0.17em} \left( \sum_{\text{\scriptsize{$\begin{array}{c} i \in D\\ X_i \cap Y \ne \varnothing \end{array}$}}} f_i e^{(a + v) c_M (X_i)} \right) \leqslant e^{|Y| \gamma} .\)

The last inequality sums (7.34) over sites of \(Y\); a support meeting \(Y\) is counted at least once. The cardinality bound and (7.34) prove the second inequality, which closes the induction. Now take \(Y = \{x\}\) and remove the empty subset, whose weight is one. For a connected \(A\), order its vertices so that each new support meets the preceding union; a spanning tree supplies such an order. Repeated covering subadditivity gives \(c_M (\bigcup_{i \in A} X_i) \leqslant \sum_{i \in A} c_M (X_i)\). Thus each term in (7.35) is bounded by its corresponding product of \(p_i\). This proves the desired estimate for every finite family of connected sets: take \(D\) to contain all their labels. Taking the supremum over finite families proves the full assertion and its absolute summability.

7.5.2Paying for the new representative sites

Let a finite set of old marks \(I\) be partitioned into groups \(V\), with a chosen old mark in each group and a positive integer bound \(h\) on the group size. Write \(r_i\) for the old site, \(c_v\) for its group's representative site, and \(\pi : I \to V\) for the partition. Assume that at most \(h\) old marks belong to a group and at most \(\ell\) old sites occur at any one lattice site. The same occupancy bound holds for the representatives, since their selected old marks are distinct. Suppose

\(\displaystyle c_M (\{r_i, c_{\pi (i)} \}) \leqslant k.\)

An activity indexed by \(\alpha\) has a mark set \(A_{\alpha} \subseteq I\) and support \(X_{\alpha}\) containing every \(r_i\) for \(i \in A_{\alpha}\). Enlarge it to

\(\displaystyle \tilde{X}_{\alpha} = X_{\alpha} \cup \{c_{\pi (i)} : i \in A_{\alpha} \} .\)

Adding the pairs one at a time gives

\(\displaystyle c_M (\tilde{X}_{\alpha}) \leqslant c_M (X_{\alpha}) + k |A_{\alpha} |,\) (7.37)

because each pair meets the original support at \(r_i\). For positive mark weights \(w_i\) and \(0 < \eta \leqslant 1\), put

\(\displaystyle \tilde{w}_i = (\eta / h) w_i e^{- \sigma k}, \qquad \sigma \geqslant 0.\)

For every nonnegative coefficient majorant \(q_{\alpha}\),

\(\displaystyle \left( \prod_{i \in A_{\alpha}} \tilde{w}_i \right) q_{\alpha} e^{\sigma c_M (\tilde{X}_{\alpha})} \leqslant (\eta / h)^{|A_{\alpha} |} \left( \prod_{i \in A_{\alpha}} w_i \right) q_{\alpha} e^{\sigma c_M (X_{\alpha})} .\) (7.38)

This follows immediately from (7.37); the exponential bound paid by each mark cancels its added connecting cover.

We need a local summation version of this observation. Suppose \(b, g \geqslant 0\) and nonnegative quantities \(F_{\alpha}\) have the bound \(\sum_{\alpha : y \in X_{\alpha}} F_{\alpha} \leqslant g\) at every site. Suppose new weighted quantities \(W_{\alpha} \geqslant 0\) satisfy \(W_{\alpha} \leqslant bF_{\alpha}\) for all \(\alpha\), and the stronger \(W_{\alpha} \leqslant (b / h) F_{\alpha}\) whenever \(A_{\alpha} \ne \varnothing\). Then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\alpha : y \in \tilde{X}_{\alpha}} W_{\alpha} \leqslant (1 + \ell) bg.\) (7.39)

The old-support contribution is at most \(bg\). If \(y\) is a new site of \(\tilde{X}_{\alpha}\), some group \(v\) has \(c_v = y\) and some \(i \in A_{\alpha} \cap \pi^{- 1} (v)\) has \(r_i \in X_{\alpha}\). Bound this contribution by summing over all such \(v\) and all its old marks. For fixed \(i\) the sum is at most \(bg / h\); there are at most \(h\) marks in that group, and at most \(\ell\) groups at \(y\). This proves (7.39). Activities with no marks have no added representative sites and are accounted for entirely in the old-support contribution. This is why the factor \(1 / h\) is needed only for activities carrying marks.

7.5.3The resulting local majorant

Take nonnegative budget constants \(T, A, B\). There are three kinds of entries in the regrouping expansion. The first are singleton coefficients \(u_i\), with \(w_i |u_i | \leqslant T\). Their local sum, including the support weight, is at most \(\ell Te^{\sigma}\), because \(c_M (\{r_i \}) \leqslant 1\) and the occupancy is at most \(\ell\). The second are the nonempty marked coefficients of the ordinary activity, whose local weighted sum is at most \(A\). Together these have bound

\(\displaystyle g_0 = \ell Te^{\sigma} + A.\)

Both carry a nonempty mark set. Equation (7.38) bounds their new weight by \(\eta / h\) times the old weight. Applying (7.39) with \(b = \eta\) gives \((1 + \ell) \eta g_0\). Each entry is split into its healthy and unhealthy support cases by indicator functions. Each piece is bounded by the same coefficient majorant, so their combined bound is twice this.

The third kind comprises the activity on flagged supports, including its zero-mark part. Let its original local weighted sum be at most \(B\). For these entries (7.38) gives a factor at most one in general, and at most \(1 / h\) for a nonempty mark set. Thus (7.39) with \(b = 1\) gives \((1 + \ell) B\). The complete local majorant is therefore

\(\displaystyle \Gamma = 2 (1 + \ell) \eta (\ell Te^{\sigma} + A) + (1 + \ell) B.\) (7.40)

This bound is independent of the total number of cells and groups.

Apply (7.35) with \(a = \nu\) and support exponent \(\sigma = \nu + v\), provided \((2 M + 1)^4 \Gamma \leqslant v\). Since the forest estimate assumes \(a \geqslant 0\), this application requires \(\nu \geqslant 0\); the purchased support exponents of the schedule satisfy it. Every connected set of distinct entries then has local summed bound at most \(e^{\Gamma} - 1\) with exponent \(\nu\). For a component whose mark set is a union of complete groups, let \(W_v = \prod_{i \in \pi^{- 1} (v)} \tilde{w}_i\). The product of its old mark weights is exactly the product of these \(W_v\) over its new group marks. Distinct constituent entries have disjoint marks when their squarefree marker product is nonzero; there is no multiplicity correction in this equality. Each constituent-label set must yield at most one output entry across the ordinary and flagged kinds; its support and prescribed flag set are fixed by its constituents. This uniqueness is proved in Subsection 7.6. Grouping the connected terms by their resulting support and group mark set, the triangle inequality preserves the local bound \(e^{\Gamma} - 1\). Restrictions to the healthy or prescribed-flag events only reduce the coefficient majorants. Thus both the ordinary and flagged connected arrays have this same bound.

For (15.21), the old weights are \(\delta W_i\), \(\eta = \delta\), \(\sigma = \nu + 1 / 2\), and the maximum group size is \(h\). The new weight of an old mark is precisely \(\delta^2 W_i e^{- (\nu + 1 / 2) k} / h\). Their product over a group is the numerator in (15.21). The purchased smallness conditions are \((2 M + 1)^4 \Gamma \leqslant 1 / 2\) and \(e^{\Gamma} - 1 \leqslant 1\). They provide the claimed unit bound for both connected arrays before the subsequent factor \(1 / 8\) is reserved for their local presentation. This proves the quantitative support and coefficient estimates used by regrouping. The exact polynomial decomposition, its gauge/locality properties and the local projection estimate are separate steps; these counting bounds do not by themselves establish those identities.

7.6The exact regrouping identity

The estimates of Subsection 7.5 concern an exact reorganization of the marked density. We now prove the identity. In particular the coefficient of the empty mark set, which determines the vacuum activity, is preserved.

7.6.1Squarefree marks and complete groups

For a finite mark set \(I\), use the commutative algebra with basis \(e_A\), \(A \subseteq I\), and multiplication

\(\displaystyle e_A e_B = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} e_{A \cup B}, & A \cap B = \varnothing,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise} . \end{array} \right.\)

Its unit is \(e_{\varnothing}\). The ideal spanned by the nonempty \(A\) is nilpotent, so its exponential is a finite sum. In particular, for finitely many nonempty \(A_r\),

\(\displaystyle \exp \left( \sum_r z_r e_{A_r} \right) = \prod_r (1 + z_r e_{A_r}),\)

since each individual summand squares to zero and all commute. Repeated use of a mark thus vanishes automatically.

Let \(\pi : I \to V\) be the partition from the previous section, with nonempty groups \(I_v = \pi^{- 1} (v)\). Define a linear map \(\mathcal{R}\) to the analogous algebra on \(V\) by

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R}e_A = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} e_B, & A = \bigcup_{v \in B} I_v \text{ for some } B \subseteq V,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise} . \end{array} \right.\)

Equivalently, the coefficient at \(B\) after applying \(\mathcal{R}\) is the old coefficient at \(\pi^{- 1} (B)\). This map is not asserted to preserve arbitrary products. It does preserve a product of monomials whose mark sets are individually unions of complete groups. If these sets are disjoint, the assertion follows from their unions; if they overlap, both products vanish. We will apply multiplicativity only after the connected-component decomposition has made every component complete in this sense.

7.6.2Ordinary and flagged entries

Fix a lattice configuration and let \(F\) be its set of flags. Take finite families of ordinary and flagged entries. An ordinary entry \(r\) has a nonempty old mark set \(A_r\), a support \(X_r\) and a coefficient \(z_r\). A flagged entry \(r\) has, in addition, a nonempty prescribed flag set \(Z_r \subseteq X_r\), and its coefficient vanishes unless \(X_r \cap F = Z_r\). Its mark set may be empty. The flagged part of the density is the sum over selections of flagged entries with pairwise disjoint supports and prescribed flag sets whose union is \(F\). Multiply this sum by the exponential of the ordinary entries. The finite exponential formula shows that the product is a sum over sets \(S\) of distinct ordinary and flagged entries, with weight

\(\displaystyle \prod_{r \in S} z_r e_{A_r},\)

subject to the flagged-support and flag-inventory conditions. Terms with overlapping old mark sets vanish.

Enlarge each support as in (7.37), writing \(\tilde{X}_r\) for the result. Split each ordinary entry into two entries with the same marks, multiplied respectively by \(\boldsymbol{1}_{\tilde{X}_r \cap F = \varnothing}\) and its complement. Call the second kind dirty; all flagged entries are dirty too. The sum of the two pieces is the original entry, so this split leaves the density unchanged. At most one piece is nonzero at any fixed configuration. Every enlarged support contains the representative \(c_v\) of each group mentioned by its old marks. A flagged entry with no marks is not enlarged.

We describe a canonical partition of a selected set \(S\). First join entries which mention a common group. Call a component of this first graph hot if it contains a dirty entry. Next join hot components whenever their enlarged supports intersect. The resulting components are the components used below. Equivalently each entry always carries its group names as graph coordinates, and carries its enlarged support as additional graph coordinates precisely when its first component is hot. Connected components are then taken in this single intersection graph. Distinct components have disjoint group-name sets, and distinct hot components have disjoint enlarged supports. Every component is connected by ordinary support overlaps: a common group supplies its representative site as a common point.

This partition is unchanged by restricting to one of its components. Indeed a path using group names never leaves that component, so its hot status can be determined within it. Conversely, a family of internally connected components with disjoint group-name sets and pairwise disjoint hot supports is exactly the component partition of its union. No group-name path or hot-support edge can cross between members of the family. These two observations prove the uniqueness needed for the reorganization.

7.6.3Reconstructing the selected density

Consider a nonzero old term which survives \(\mathcal{R}\). Its total mark set is a union of complete groups. Every one of its components also has this property. For if one mark in a group occurs in that component, every other selected mark of the same group is joined to it through their common group name. Since the total selection contains the whole group, the component contains the whole group.

A cold component contains only healthy ordinary entries, so its coefficient is supported on configurations with no flags in its combined enlarged support. It has a nonempty group mark set. A hot component necessarily contains a flagged entry. To prove this last assertion, choose a dirty ordinary entry if no flagged entry has yet been found. Its enlarged support meets \(F\). The global flag-inventory condition supplies a selected flagged entry whose prescribed flag set contains that point. Both entries are hot and their supports intersect, so they are in the same component, a contradiction.

For each possible cold component, make a new ordinary entry: its support is the union of its enlarged supports, its coefficient is the product of its constituent coefficients, and its new marks are its group names. For each possible component containing a flagged entry, make a new flagged entry with that same combined support, flag set \(Z\) equal to the union of its old prescribed flag sets, and coefficient

\(\displaystyle \boldsymbol{1}_{\tilde{X} \cap F = Z} \prod_{r \in S} z_r .\)

Only internally connected components with disjoint old mark sets, complete group marks and pairwise disjoint old flagged supports are included. The new flag set is nonempty.

For the components of a nonzero old selection, the displayed indicator is one. Any flag in the enlarged support of a hot component belongs to a selected flagged entry somewhere in the old term; intersection of the two hot supports puts that entry in this same component. The converse inclusion follows from the old flag conditions. Distinct hot supports are disjoint, and their prescribed flag sets still partition \(F\). Distinct components have disjoint new mark sets. The old term therefore gives an allowed new term with exactly the same coefficient after \(\mathcal{R}\).

Conversely, take a nonzero term in the new ordinary-exponential and flagged-selection expansion. Its component entries have disjoint new mark sets and pairwise disjoint hot supports. Their inventories of old labels are disjoint. A shared old label carrying a mark would force a shared group name; a shared label without marks must be a flagged entry and would force intersecting hot supports. Thus no old label is counted twice. Within each new component the old flagged supports are disjoint; between components they lie inside disjoint hot supports. Their old flag sets have union \(F\). The old mark sets are disjoint, since they are disjoint within components and complete disjoint groups between components. Hence their union gives an allowed old selection. The uniqueness of the component partition proves that this construction inverts the preceding one.

The products of coefficients agree in both directions. On complete group components, \(\mathcal{R}\) preserves their monomial product by the first subsection. Summing the resulting weight-preserving bijection proves the exact regrouping identity. All sums here are finite: the torus, mark sets, supports and their admissible patterns are finite. No convergence or rearrangement assumption enters this algebraic step. The connected-array estimates of Subsection 7.5 apply to these very component entries. The ordinary and flagged kinds cannot represent the same constituent set, since one is cold and the other contains a flagged entry; thus summing their bounds does not hide an additional multiplicity.

7.6.4Vacuum, locality, and covariance

A new entry with no group marks must be a single old zero-mark flagged entry. In fact no ordinary entry has empty marks. All entries of such a component are therefore flagged entries with no marks, whose supports were not enlarged. Their old supports are pairwise disjoint, so no support edge connects two different entries; and there are no group-name edges. Connectedness forces a singleton. Its new support, flag set and coefficient coincide with the old ones; the indicator is already implied by the old flag condition. Thus the zero-mark coefficient of every flagged support is unchanged. The ordinary array has no zero-mark coefficient. The same vacuum activity is retained by the regrouping.

The construction also preserves the required local dependence. Each coefficient is a finite product of original coefficients, with indicators determined by the flags in the combined support. It therefore depends only on the original field and flag data in that support. Products, finite sums and these measurable indicators preserve measurability. If the original coefficients and flags are gauge invariant, so are the new coefficients. Healthy ordinary components vanish when their support meets the flags; flagged components vanish unless the intersection with the flags is their specified flag set. These are the support conditions used in the local representations. They do not assert smoothness across a flag boundary.

Finally we state covariance. Let \(a\) be a permutation of the old marks and \(b\) a permutation of the group names, and let a translation or signed coordinate change, with the accompanying conjugation, act on positions and flags. Assume: (a) \(a\) and \(b\) are compatible with the partition, \(\pi (a (i)) = b (\pi (i))\) for every old mark \(i\), and they carry the chosen representative of each group to the chosen representative of its image; (b) for a fixed copied family \(v\) of group names with image \(u\), one has \(b (i) \in u\) if and only if \(i \in v\); (c) for marks in groups of \(v\), the positions and flag data of \(a (i)\) are the transformed positions and flag data of \(i\); and (d) the original coefficient identities hold for every mark set contained in the copied family. Compatibility (a) is an assumption on the relabelling, not a consequence of (d): a permutation of old marks that does not map groups to groups does not intertwine the regrouping. In the application the same partition is used in each copy, as in Subsection 15.5, and (a)–(c) hold by construction. Under (a)–(d) the transformation relabels entries bijectively, preserves group-name incidence, health and flag inventory, and sends support intersections to support intersections. Hence they send the canonical components to the corresponding components after transformation. Reindexing the finite sums proves covariance of the new arrays for every coefficient whose group names lie in the copied family \(v\); no statement is made for other coefficients. This keeps the replication identities through repeated regrouping. To obtain the local normalized-field representation used in (15.21), one must in addition extract its singleton coefficients and apply the purchased local analytic projection bounds; those estimates are not consequences of this finite algebraic identity.

7.7Recovering the local fields after regrouping

The preceding regrouping gives a polynomial density with controlled coefficients. To continue the scale induction, its linear coefficients must again be presented as local fields, with the remaining terms assigned to interactions involving at least two marks. We give this passage explicitly. It uses no Taylor approximation: the separation by the number of marks is exact.

Fix a finite periodic lattice, a finite mark set \(V\), and positive weights \(w_v\). Write \(e_A\) for the squarefree monomial belonging to an allowed mark set \(A\), as in the preceding section. Ordinary polynomial activities \(F_X\) are indexed by supports \(X\); flagged activities \(K_{X, Z}\) are indexed by pairs with \(\varnothing \ne Z \subseteq X\). Their coefficients are measurable, gauge invariant, and depend only on the field and flag data in \(X\). A nonzero coefficient with mark set \(A\) requires \(\{o_v : v \in A\} \subseteq X\). Ordinary activities have zero constant coefficient and vanish unless \(X\) is healthy. Flagged activities vanish unless the flags in \(X\) are exactly \(Z\).

Put \(\nu = \nu_R\), \(M = M_R\) for the support parameters of the current representation. Suppose the ordinary and flagged arrays have the weighted coefficient bounds from Subsection 7.5, with local hit bounds \(a\) and \(b\), respectively. In concrete terms there are nonnegative coefficient majorants \(t_{X, A}\) and \(s_{(X, Z), A}\), valid for every field configuration, such that for every site \(x\)

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \sum_{X \ni x} e^{\nu c_M (X)} \sum_A t_{X, A} \prod_{v \in A} w_v & \leqslant a, \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.41)} \\ \sum_{(X, Z) : x \in X} e^{\nu c_M (X)} \sum_A s_{(X, Z), A} \prod_{v \in A} w_v & \leqslant b. \end{array}\)

The underlying lattice is finite, so the support indices in these expressions are finite. The activity dictionary used to represent them may nevertheless be countable.

We use the following precise property of that dictionary. Every nonempty support has an unused ordinary slot with that support and zero background coefficient. Every admissible flagged pair has a flagged slot with that pair, eligible whenever the specified flag condition holds. A real measurable gauge-invariant function \(h\) in such a slot is an allowed elementary insertion when it has the required local dependence and vanishes off the slot's eligibility set. If \(|h| \leqslant t\), its cost is

\(\displaystyle e^{\nu c_M (X)} t.\)

This is a property of the available representation, not a consequence of the support count. Its production in the evolving activity dictionary remains an input to this local argument.

7.7.1The bound of a singleton

Fix a mark \(v\). In the first inequality of (7.41), take \(x = o_v\) and retain only the coefficient of \(e_{\{v\}}\). Coefficients on supports not containing \(o_v\) vanish. Dividing by \(w_v > 0\) gives majorants \(t_X \geqslant 0\) with

\(\displaystyle | [e_{\{v\}}] F_X (U) | \leqslant t_X, \qquad \sum_X e^{\nu c_M (X)} t_X \leqslant a / w_v .\)

The same argument for the flagged array gives majorants \(s_{X, Z}\) with weighted sum at most \(b / w_v\). There is no factor \(|V|\) in these bounds: only one coordinate of the bound at \(o_v\) was retained.

For an ordinary singleton function \(f\) supported in \(X\), use the unused ordinary slot and write \(f = \text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} f + \text{iIm} \hspace{0.17em} f\). Both real functions have bound \(t_X\), retain local dependence, measurability and gauge invariance, and vanish outside the healthy set. They are therefore two elementary insertions, with coefficients \(1\) and \(\text{i}\), of total cost at most \(2 e^{\nu c_M (X)} t_X\). They contribute \(f\) to the ordinary field and zero to its flagged profile. The same construction in the flagged slot contributes zero to the ordinary field and \(f\) at the specified flagged pair, at cost at most \(2 e^{\nu c_M (X)} s_{X, Z}\). Zero functions require no slot.

Consequently, for arbitrary constants \(u_v \in \mathbb{C}\), the pair

\(\displaystyle g_v (U) = u_v + \sum_X [e_{\{v\}}] F_X (U), \qquad q_v (X, Z ; U) = [e_{\{v\}}] K_{X, Z} (U)\) (7.42)

has a finite elementary representation anchored at \(o_v\), with constant part \(u_v\) and cost at most

\(\displaystyle 2 a / w_v + 2 b / w_v .\) (7.43)

Indeed concatenate the finite lists just constructed. Adding lists adds their costs, and multiplication by a scalar multiplies cost by its modulus. The constant \(u_v\) is recorded separately and costs nothing in this representation; its later affordability is a separate condition.

Each flagged elementary insertion is carried by its chosen index in the countable activity dictionary. Adding the finitely many such indexed functions gives a family \(k_{v, j} (U)\) with a summable uniform majorant. Summing it over all indices with flagged pair \((X, Z)\) gives exactly \(q_v (X, Z ; U)\). Thus a finite local representation also provides the dominated indexed family required by the transfer formula. The estimates concern the local representation and do not require a canonically chosen index for each singleton.

7.7.2Exact separation of the higher terms

Let \(k_j^0 (U)\) be the real background flagged activities and let \(E (U)\) be the ordinary background exponent. For a fixed configuration \(U\), denote its flag set by \(\mathcal{Z} (U)\). For an array \(L\) indexed by flagged pairs set

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{G}_U (L) = \sum_{\text{\scriptsize{$\begin{array}{c} \mathcal{P} \text{ a set of flagged pairs}\\ \text{supports pairwise disjoint}, \hspace{0.27em} \bigcup_{(X, Z) \in \mathcal{P}} Z =\mathcal{Z} (U) \end{array}$}}} \prod_{(X, Z) \in \mathcal{P}} L_{X, Z} .\)

The current density is

\(\displaystyle D (U) = e^{E (U)} \exp \left( \sum_v u_v e_{\{v\}} + \sum_X F_X (U) \right) \mathcal{G}_U (K (U)) .\) (7.44)

Assume the constant coefficient of \(K_{X, Z}\) is the sum of \(k_j^0\) over dictionary indices with pair \((X, Z)\); assume also the background activities are absolutely summable. These are the vacuum consistency and validity conditions retained in the preceding regrouping.

Let \(F_X^{\geqslant 2}\) and \(K_{X, Z}^{\geqslant 2}\) denote the arrays obtained by discarding coefficients with fewer than two marks. Then, coefficient by coefficient,

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \sum_v u_v e_{\{v\}} + \sum_X F_X & = \sum_v g_v e_{\{v\}} + \sum_X F_X^{\geqslant 2},\\ K_{X, Z} & = \sum_{j : \hspace{0.17em} (X_j, Z_j) = (X, Z)} k_j^0 + \sum_v q_v (X, Z) e_{\{v\}} + K_{X, Z}^{\geqslant 2} . \end{array}\)

Substitution in (7.44) proves the desired local representation of the same density, with fields \(g_v\), flagged profiles \(q_v\), and interactions of order at least two. Discarding coefficients cannot increase a sum of nonnegative coefficient majorants. Hence the two higher arrays retain hit bounds \(a\) and \(b\). Their support, eligibility and invariance properties are retained as well, since each remaining coefficient is unchanged.

For completeness, this representation has the expected linear density. Put \(r (U) = e^{E (U)} \mathcal{G}_U (k^0)\), the unmarked density. Expanding a product to first order gives

\(\displaystyle [e_{\{v\}}] D (U) = r (U) g_v (U) + e^{E (U)} \sum_{S \text{ admissible}} \sum_{j \in S} k_{v, j} (U) \prod_{i \in S \setminus \{j\}} k_i^0 (U) .\) (7.45)

Here admissibility means that the indexed supports are disjoint and their flags cover \(\mathcal{Z} (U)\). The exponential contributes its singleton coefficient \(g_v\), while the flagged product contributes exactly one differentiated factor. Replacing sums over flagged pairs by sums over dictionary indices is legitimate: if \(B_0 = \sum_j |k_j^0 (U) |\) and \(B_v = \sum_j |k_{v, j} (U) |\), the absolute sum of all differentiated products, even without admissibility, is bounded by \(B_v \prod_j (1 + |k_j^0 (U) |) \leqslant B_v e^{B_0}\). The corresponding vacuum sum is bounded by \(e^{B_0}\). Finite expansions followed by absolute convergence establish the identity.

Equation (7.45) also explains why the ordinary singleton is determined by its density and flagged profile whenever \(r (U) \ne 0\): subtract the flagged term and divide by \(r (U)\). This is the uniqueness used when a transfer step first identifies the singleton density rather than the ordinary field itself.

7.7.3Returning to the scale induction

Apply this construction to the regrouped arrays of the preceding section. If both hit bounds are at most \(Z\), each grouped field has cost at most \(4 Z / w_v\), and the higher interactions have hit bound \(Z\). The regrouping creates no separately recorded linear constant, so here \(u_v = 0\). In particular, when \(Z = 1\), reducing the mark weight from \(w_v\) to \(w'_v = w_v / 8\) leaves

\(\displaystyle \text{singleton cost} \leqslant \frac{4}{w_v} = \frac{1}{2 w'_v} .\)

The coefficient bounds of the higher interactions only decrease under this reduction. This proves the representation and the singleton budget needed after a grouping operation, conditional on the stated dictionary and background-validity properties.

Assume the input arrays and constants have the covariance inherited from the preceding regrouping. Translations and coordinate symmetries then preserve the ordinary singletons by reindexing the finite support sum in (7.42); conjugating symmetries also conjugate its scalar coefficients. The flagged profiles transform in the same way. No equivariance of the auxiliary choice of dictionary indices is needed, because only their summed profiles occur in the representation and in (7.45). This argument applies to any finite number of slots in each copied cell. The remaining analytic step is to replace these local fields by degree-bounded coefficient fields while controlling the error under transfer to the next scale. The singleton extraction alone does not provide that projection estimate.

7.8Transporting a local representation through one scale

We now explain the common transport argument used by the centered estimate and the local OPE construction. Its analytic input is a bound for integrating finitely many elementary insertions against one fixed background transition. The argument below turns that bound into a transition for arbitrary local fields represented by absolutely summable elementary insertions. It also restores the prescribed singleton profiles after integration. The same background transition is used throughout.

7.8.1From an anchored estimate to a site estimate

The first estimate accounts for summing over all named sources. Write

\(\displaystyle S_M = e \{2 (16 M + 9)\}^4 .\)

Suppose at most \(N\) marks are anchored at any one site. Let \(X_i\) be supports, \(A_i\) their nonempty sets of marks, and \(f_i \geqslant 0\). Every \(i\) with \(f_i > 0\) is assumed to have some \(v \in A_i\) with \(o_v \in X_i\). If

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i : v \in A_i} e^{(a + 1) c_M (X_i)} f_i \leqslant c \quad \text{for every } v,\)

where \(c \geqslant 0\), then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i : x \in X_i} e^{ac_M (X_i)} f_i \leqslant NS_M c \quad \text{for every } x.\) (7.46)

Indeed, the defining connected-cover bound gives, for \(x, y \in X\),

\(\displaystyle \text{dist} (x, y) \leqslant (4 M + 2) c_M (X) + 2 M.\)

Hence a term on the left of (7.46) is at most

\(\displaystyle \sum_{v \in A_i} e^{1 - \text{dist} (x, o_v) / (4 M + 2)} e^{(a + 1) c_M (X_i)} f_i .\)

Retain the anchored \(v\) to verify this inequality; all other terms are nonnegative. Sum first over \(i\), use the anchored bound, and then the occupancy bound. It remains to check

\(\displaystyle \sum_y e^{- \text{dist} (x, y) / (4 M + 2)} \leqslant \{2 (16 M + 9)\}^4 .\)

Lift \(y - x\) to its minimal integer representative. These lifts are distinct, and their \(\ell^1\) norm is at most four times their periodic maximum distance. With \(b = 1 / (4 (4 M + 2))\), the sum is therefore bounded by

\(\displaystyle \sum_{z \in \mathbb{Z}^4} e^{- b |z|_1} = \left( 1 + \frac{2}{e^b - 1} \right)^4 \leqslant \{2 (1 + b) / b\}^4 = \{2 (16 M + 9)\}^4 .\)

This proves the estimate, uniformly in the period. The argument also proves summability for countable activity families by bounding every finite subsum.

7.8.2Preparing a finite family of insertions

Consider a fine representation \(R\) and a coarse representation \(E\). Let \(\mathcal{T}\) be their block-integration operator. For a fine elementary insertion \(M\), write \(\mathcal{Q} (M)\) for its quoted coarse flagged profile. We assume this map is real linear under absolutely summable combinations in a fixed slot, with a uniform bound by the elementary insertion cost. These are properties of the fixed transition; their analytic construction is not being asserted here.

For each mark \(v\), suppose we have an anchored expansion, using only finitely many distinct slots but possibly countably many functions in each slot,

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} g_v - u_v & = \sum_j z_{vj} \hspace{0.17em} \text{ord} (M_{vj}), & q_v & = \sum_j z_{vj} \hspace{0.17em} \text{flag} (M_{vj}),\\ q'_v & = \sum_j z_{vj} \hspace{0.17em} \mathcal{Q} (M_{vj}) +\mathcal{H}_E (\psi_v) . & & \end{array}\)

Here \(\sum_j |z_{vj} | \text{cost} (M_{vj}) \leqslant y_v\), and \(\psi_v (X, U)\) is a coarse response anchored at the parent of \(o_v\). It has local dependence, is measurable and gauge invariant, and vanishes when \(X\) is healthy. Its cost hypothesis is

\(\displaystyle \sum_X e^{(p + 1) c_{M_E} (X)} w_v \sup_U | \psi_v (X, U) | \leqslant W.\) (7.47)

An available majorant can replace the displayed supremum. The operator \(\mathcal{H}_E\) will be described below. The constant \(u_v\) is outside the priced sum.

Compress the countable sum separately in each slot. Its real and imaginary parts converge uniformly, since their absolute values are bounded by the summable insertion weights. The sums are real measurable local gauge-invariant functions in that same slot. Their bounds are the respective sums of absolute coefficient weights. Thus two real insertions, multiplied by \(1\) and \(\text{i}\), replace the whole complex series. Both the old profiles and their quoted profiles are preserved, by the assumed continuity and linearity. The resulting finite list for mark \(v\) has weighted cost at most \(2 w_v y_v\). If \(2 w_v y_v \leqslant d_0\) and the fine-site occupancy is at most \(N_f\), (7.46) bounds its site bound at exponent \(a\) by \(N_f S_{M_R} d_0\), provided \(a + 1 \leqslant \nu_R\).

Suppose the higher ordinary and flagged arrays in the fine local representation each have site bound at most \(q\) at exponent \(a\). The real/imaginary construction of Subsection 7.7, now applied to every coefficient of order at least two, represents them by finite elementary insertions at total site bound at most \(4 q\). The unused-slot property is used here. Combining the two lists gives a finite list with bound

\(\displaystyle T = N_f S_{M_R} d_0 + 4 q.\) (7.48)

Its ordinary sum is \(\sum_v (g_v - u_v) e_{\{v\}} + H\), and its flagged sum is \(\sum_v q_v e_{\{v\}} + K\), where \(H, K\) are the higher arrays. All marks of each insertion lie in its support.

The elementary integration input takes a family of linear slot insertions. To turn the exponential insertions in the last display into that form, adjoin, at each ordinary slot, the product of every compatible set of at least two members of the list. Compatibility means that their mark sets are disjoint and their union is an allowed monomial. Keep all original members. The added product uses the same slot and support, and its bound is the product of the constituent bounds. No product is added to a flagged slot.

This operation has bound at most

\(\displaystyle T + e^T T.\) (7.49)

To verify it, let \(r_j \geqslant 0\) be the product of the marker toll, the modulus of the complex coefficient, and the elementary supremum-norm weight, excluding the support exponential \(e^{ac_M (X)}\). For one ordinary slot put \(s = \sum_j r_j\). Because every member has at least one mark in that support and \(a \geqslant 0\), the site bound at any point of the support implies \(s \leqslant T\). The sum of the product bounds of all nonempty subsets is at most

\(\displaystyle \prod_j (1 + r_j) - 1 \leqslant e^s - 1 \leqslant se^s \leqslant e^T s.\)

The added compatible subsets of size at least two form a subfamily. Multiplying by the support exponential and summing over distinct slots meeting a given site gives at most \(e^T T\). Adding the original bound proves (7.49).

The density identity is exact. For each ordinary slot, the sum of all the compatible products is its squarefree exponential minus one: expand \(\prod_j (1 + m_j) = \exp (\sum_j m_j)\), where each nonempty monomial \(m_j\) has square zero. Terms with incompatible marks vanish in the algebra. Thus replacing the exponential at that slot by one plus the expanded list changes no coefficient. Flagged slots have not changed. Denote the resulting elementary density by \(\mathcal{E}\). The fine density \(X\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle X = \exp \left( \sum_v u_v e_{\{v\}} \right) \mathcal{E}.\) (7.50)

Every added insertion has at least two marks, so the sum of quoted singleton profiles is still \(q'_v -\mathcal{H}_E (\psi_v)\).

7.8.3Absorbing the coarse response

We state explicitly the nonlinear integration input used at this point. For every prepared finite list of bound at most \(M_{\ast}\), the operator \(\mathcal{T}\) produces ordinary and flagged arrays \(\hat{F}, \hat{K}\), with the support and eligibility properties of Subsection 7.7, and

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{T}\mathcal{E}= e^{E_E} \exp \left( \sum_X \hat{F}_X \right) \mathcal{G} (\hat{K}) .\) (7.51)

The ordinary array has no constant term and site bound at most \(k\) at exponent \(t + \eta\); the flagged array has site bound at most \(u\) at exponent \(p\). Its vacuum coefficient is the coarse background profile, and its singleton coefficient is precisely the sum of the quoted singleton insertions. This estimate is required uniformly in the finite list and period, subject to the specified parent-site occupancy. Its production is a separate analytic obligation.

Assume \(T + e^T T \leqslant M_{\ast}\). Require also that every prepared elementary density is integrable under the fine prior and in each transition slice. The lawful background representation and its transition-kernel property supply these integrability conditions in the application. Integration is componentwise on the finite-dimensional polynomial algebra, so its continuous linear maps commute with an integrable transition. In particular the constant polynomial exponential in (7.50) can be pulled out. We obtain the same representation as (7.51), with exponent \(P = \sum_v u_v e_{\{v\}} + \sum_X \hat{F}_X\).

Set \(J (U) = \sum_{v, X} \psi_v (X, U) e_{\{v\}}\). We next move \(J\) from the ordinary exponent into the flagged array, without changing the density. In a term of

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{G} (\hat{K}) \exp (J),\)

regard each selected flagged pair and each selected response insertion \((v, X)\) as a vertex, and join vertices whose supports overlap. Every nonzero response insertion meets a flag, because it vanishes on healthy supports. That flag is covered by a selected flagged pair in the gas term. Hence every connected component contains a flagged vertex. Within a component the flagged supports are disjoint; give the component the union support and the union of their flag sets. Define \(\bar{K}\) as the sum of these component products with that output pair, retaining its required flag indicator. Every flag in a response support is covered by an old flagged vertex meeting that support, hence by a vertex in the same component. Thus the component flag set is exactly the set of flags in its union support.

Connected-component decomposition is a bijection on nonzero terms. In the forward direction, different components have disjoint supports and their flag sets cover all flags. In the reverse direction, the constituent vertices of components with disjoint output supports cannot coincide or acquire a new overlap edge. Their union recovers the original selection. The products of their coefficients agree. Thus

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{G} (\bar{K}) =\mathcal{G} (\hat{K}) \exp (J), \qquad e^P \mathcal{G} (\hat{K}) = e^{P - J} \mathcal{G} (\bar{K}) .\) (7.52)

All these expansions are finite in support and mark indices.

With no response vertex a connected component is a single old flagged vertex, since old flagged supports are disjoint. This proves preservation of the vacuum. With exactly one response vertex \((v, X)\), every flagged vertex in its component meets \(X\): there are no edges between distinct flagged vertices, so any path to the response must be a direct edge. Let \(\Pi_E (X ; Y, Z ; U)\) be the sum of products of the background flagged activities over these nonempty, pairwise-disjoint collections meeting \(X\), with union support \(Y\) after adjoining \(X\) and union flag set \(Z\), including the output flag indicator. Then

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{H}_E (\psi_v) (Y, Z ; U) = \sum_X \psi_v (X, U) \Pi_E (X ; Y, Z ; U) .\)

This defines the operator in the transition expansion above. Two or more response vertices have degree at least two and cannot contribute a singleton. Therefore

\(\displaystyle [e_{\{v\}}] \bar{K} = [e_{\{v\}}] \hat{K} +\mathcal{H}_E (\psi_v) = q'_v .\)

Replacing the background profiles in \(\Pi_E\) by their countable indexed sums is justified by absolute convergence, bounded by the product of \(1 + |k_j^0 |\), as in (7.45).

The response insertions have site bound at most \(N_c S_{M_E} W\) at exponent \(p\), by (7.47) and (7.46), where \(N_c\) is the parent-site occupancy. Put

\(\displaystyle A = u + N_c S_{M_E} W.\)

If

\(\displaystyle t + \eta \geqslant 0, \qquad (2 M_E + 1)^4 A \leqslant p - (t + \eta),\) (7.53)

the connected-support estimate (7.35) bounds the site bound of \(\bar{K}\) at exponent \(t + \eta\) by \(e^A - 1\). Its coefficients are products of old coefficient majorants, and the map from a constituent set to its output pair is unique; thus the counting lemma applies without a multiplicity factor. The ordinary higher coefficients retain bound \(k\), since \(J\) is linear. Local dependence, gauge invariance, measurability and the flag condition follow from finite products and support-local flag indicators.

Finally suppose the singleton coefficient of the actual integrated density has already been identified as the linear density of a prescribed pair \((g'_v, k'_v)\), with \(k'_v\) dominated and profile \(q'_v\). Assume the coarse unmarked density is nonzero. The uniqueness following (7.45) identifies the ordinary singleton of \(P - J\) with \(g'_v\). Removing all coefficients of degree zero and one now yields a local representation with these prescribed fields, with ordinary bound \(k\) and flagged bound \(e^A - 1\) at exponent \(t + \eta\).

7.8.4Changing the mark weights

The final operation pays for changing weights. Suppose \(w_v, w'_v > 0\) and for every allowed monomial \(B\) with \(|B| \geqslant 2\) supported in \(X\),

\(\displaystyle \prod_{v \in B} w'_v \leqslant a_{\ast} \prod_{v \in B} w_v \hspace{0.17em} e^{\eta c_{M_E} (X)}, \qquad a_{\ast} \geqslant 0.\) (7.54)

Multiply this inequality by each coefficient majorant and sum. It changes an exponent-\(t + \eta\) bound \(C\) into an exponent-\(t\) bound at most \(a_{\ast} C\). Hence the transported representation has both higher-array bounds at most \(g\) whenever

\(\displaystyle a_{\ast} k \leqslant g, \qquad a_{\ast} (e^A - 1) \leqslant g.\)

Here is the specialization used in the scale induction. Start with weights \(I_v > 0\), higher-array bound \(1\), and exponent at least \(\nu_R - 4\). Choose \(0 < d \leqslant 1\) and first reduce the weights to \(w_v = dI_v\). A monomial with at least two marks acquires the factor \(d^{|B|} \leqslant d\), so the higher-array bound becomes \(q = d\). Suppose the singleton transition costs obey \(2 I_v y_v \leqslant 1\) and \(I_v r_v \leqslant 1\), with response cost \(r_v\) measured at the full coarse exponent plus two. After multiplying by \(w_v\), the fine singleton budget is \(d_0 = d\) and the response budget is \(W = d\) at \(p = \nu_E + 1\).

Assume \(\nu_R, \nu_E \geqslant 100\), and write \(N\) for a bound on both occupancies. The two numerical conditions are

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlllll} T & = NS_{M_R} d + 4 d, & T + e^T T & \leqslant M_{\ast}, & & \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.55)} \\ A & = u + NS_{M_E} d, & (2 M_E + 1)^4 A & \leqslant \tfrac{1}{2}, & e^A - 1 & \leqslant 1. \end{array}\)

Use the nonlinear input with fine exponent \(\nu_R - 4\), ordinary output exponent \(\nu_E + 1 / 2\) and bound \(1\), and flagged output exponent \(\nu_E + 1\) and bound \(u\). The fine anchored estimate requires only \(\nu_R - 3 \leqslant \nu_R\), and all exponents used for support counting are nonnegative. Equation (7.55) therefore verifies (7.53) with \(t = \nu_E\) and \(\eta = 1 / 2\). Any new weights satisfying (7.54) with \(a_{\ast} = 1\) give a coarse local representation at exponent \(\nu_E\) and bound \(1\). This proves the one-scale propagation from the stated elementary transition estimate and singleton transition expansions. It does not replace the proof of that analytic estimate or the construction of those expansions.

7.9Local jet extraction and exact masked splitting

A transferred local field is not yet a coefficient field. Its low-order jet has to be extracted, and subtracting that jet changes the support of its healthy-set indicator. The difference of indicators is retained as an explicit flagged response. We give the extraction and this exact splitting before applying it to the one-step integral.

Fix a finite lattice, an anchor \(o\), a degree \(d\), and a coefficient frame \(V_d\) for the gauge-invariant curvature-jet polynomials of weighted degree at most \(d\). A curvature derivative of order \(j\) has weight \(j + 2\). Write \(\mu (v) = \sum_{\alpha} |v_{\alpha} |\), \(e_0\) for the unit coordinate, \(v^{\circ} = v - v_0 e_0\), and \(D_t\) for the diagonal map multiplying a coordinate with \(r\) curvature factors by \(t^r\). The budget consists of a threshold \(0 < t \leqslant 1\), derivative order \(q \geqslant d\), spatial power \(p \geqslant d\), positive support exponent \(u\), and integer covering radius \(B \geqslant 1\).

A regular field on a support \(X\) is a measurable, gauge-invariant function depending only on its links, whose exponential-coordinate germ is \(C^q\) at every configuration healthy on \(X\). Its ordered left derivatives exist through order \(q\), including at the threshold boundary. Write \(\|f\|_{o, X} \leqslant c\) when \(c \geqslant 0\) and all these derivatives of orders at most \(q\) have modulus at most \(c\), for direction lists whose every entry satisfies

\(\displaystyle |A (e) | \leqslant t (1 + \text{dist} (e_-, o))^p .\)

In particular the zeroth-order estimate bounds \(f\) on the healthy set. No derivatives of a healthy-set indicator occur in this definition.

7.9.1Reading the unmasked jet

Let \(H_o (J, h)\) be the local test holonomy for a curvature jet \(J\), and write \(h \cdot J\) for weighted scaling, with component \(h^{j + 2} J_j\). Its defining local identities are

\(\displaystyle H_o (J, 0) = 1, \qquad H_o (J, h) = H_o (h \cdot J, 1), \qquad H_o (gJ, h) = g \hspace{0.17em} H_o (J, h) \hspace{0.17em} g^{- 1} .\)

Here only the components of weights at most \(d\) are retained on the right. The finite-dimensional map \(Z \mapsto H_o (Z, 1)\) is analytic near \(Z = 0\) in the genuine group exponential chart. These properties of the chosen test holonomy, and the existence of the local polynomial section below, are the geometric inputs used in this section.

Here a support \(X\) is nonwrapping at \(o\) precisely when \(10 \text{dist} (x, o) < n\) for every \(x \in X\), where \(n\) is the lattice side. For such a support, define \(\Pi_{o, X} f\) by requiring that for every \(J\), the functions

\(\displaystyle h \longmapsto f (H_o (J, h)), \qquad h \longmapsto (\Pi_{o, X} f) (h \cdot J)\)

have the same derivatives at zero through order \(d\). For a wrapping support the convention is \(\Pi_{o, X} f = f (1) \hspace{0.17em} 1\).

We first prove existence in the nonwrapping case. In the finite space of truncated jets put \(F (Z) = f (H_o (Z, 1))\). The identity configuration is healthy, and the local logarithm of \(H_o (Z, 1)\) is analytic and vanishes at zero. Thus \(F\) is \(C^d\) near zero. Its Taylor polynomial

\(\displaystyle T (Z) = \sum_{r = 0}^d \frac{1}{r!} D^r F (0) [Z, \ldots, Z]\)

is a polynomial in the real coordinates of the curvature jets. Delete its monomials of weighted degree greater than \(d\), obtaining \(P\). The ordinary Taylor polynomial has the same derivatives as \(F\) through order \(d\). Composing with the polynomial curve \(h \cdot J\) preserves that equality: in the chain rule for a derivative of order \(k \leqslant d\), only derivatives of \(F\) of orders at most \(k\) enter. A deleted monomial becomes a multiple of \(h^r\) with \(r > d\), so contributes nothing through order \(d\). Hence \(P\) has exactly the required test jets.

Gauge invariance of \(f\) and covariance of \(H\) give the same test jets for \(P (gJ)\) and \(P (J)\). Their difference has weighted degree at most \(d\). For each fixed \(J\), all its coefficients as a polynomial in \(h\) vanish, since its derivatives at zero vanish. Taking \(h = 1\) proves \(P (gJ) = P (J)\). Thus \(P\) belongs to the gauge-invariant label space. The same argument applied to two candidates proves uniqueness. Subtraction and scalar multiplication of the test identities now show that \(\Pi_{o, X}\) is linear on regular fields with a fixed support. The wrapping convention has these properties as well.

For later use set

\(\displaystyle v (f) = D_t \text{coord} (\Pi_{o, X} f) .\)

The uniform estimate for this map follows from a precise local bound on test holonomies. Namely, for every fixed \(J, d\) and \(r \leqslant d\), suppose the logarithmic test curve \(Z (h) = \log H_o (tJ, h)\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle |Z^{(r)} (0) (e) | \leqslant a_r (J, d) \hspace{0.17em} t (1 + \text{dist} (e_-, o))^{\max (r - 1, 0)} .\) (7.56)

The constants are independent of the period, anchor, support and threshold. We prove this estimate for the actual radial test below; first we explain precisely how it is used.

Here is why it gives a constant \(R_d \geqslant 1\) such that

\(\displaystyle \mu (v (f)) \leqslant R_d c.\) (7.57)

First bound a mixed exponential-coordinate derivative of order \(k\) on directions \(w_1, \ldots, w_k\) bounded by fixed multiples \(a_1, \ldots, a_k\) of the allowed direction weight. Restrict the symmetric \(k\)th Fréchet derivative at zero to their span, using the map \(Lx = \sum_i x_i w_i\) from \(\mathbb{R}^k\). Its diagonal at \(x\) is bounded by \((1 + \sum_i |x_i |a_i)^k c\): normalize \(Lx\) by this positive factor and use the repeated same-direction left derivative along \(\exp (sLx)\). Such a repeated derivative equals the derivative of \(f (\exp (sLx))\); the one-parameter group identity proves this by induction. Only the Fréchet derivative in this finite-dimensional chart is being treated as symmetric.

Diagonal evaluations separate symmetric tensors on \(\mathbb{R}^k\). A finite number of them therefore bound any fixed mixed evaluation. For completeness, if separating linear functionals \(\ell_i\) on a finite-dimensional space obey \(| \ell_i A| \leqslant b_i c\), but no uniform norm bound follows, choose \(A_n, c_n\) with \(\|A_n \| > (n + 1) c_n\). A subsequence of \(A_n / \|A_n \|\) converges on the unit sphere. Every \(\ell_i\) vanishes on its limit, a contradiction. Apply this argument to the symmetric tensors just described. The resulting mixed bound depends on \(k\) and the \(a_i\), not on the lattice.

The finite-order chain rule for \(f (\exp Z (h))\), together with (7.56) and \(p \geqslant d\), now bounds each fixed jet probe by \(C_{J, k} c\). These probes, for \(k \leqslant d\), separate \(V_d\): a weighted polynomial all of whose test derivatives vanish is zero, by the uniqueness argument above. Apply the same finite-dimensional argument to \(V_d\) to obtain (7.57). The weights are exactly \(D_t\), since evaluating a polynomial at \(tJ\) multiplies a monomial with \(r\) curvature factors by \(t^r\). For a wrapping support only \(f (1)\) remains, and \(|f (1) | \leqslant c\).

7.9.2Uniform bounds for the logarithmic test links

We establish (7.56) by the same rescaling used to construct the test. A truncated radial connection is a finite sum of homogeneous connection tensors \(J_j\) of degree \(j + 1\). On a positive link starting at a covering-space point \(x\) in direction \(e_i\), its coefficient at test scale \(h\) and amplitude \(t\) is

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j = 0}^{d - 2} th^{j + 2} J_j (e_i) [x + se_i, \ldots, x + se_i], \qquad 0 \leqslant s \leqslant 1.\)

An empty sum is allowed. The link is the endpoint of the right-multiplication equation \(U' = UA\), \(U (0) = 1\), with this Lie-algebra-valued coefficient. The local logarithm is taken only near \(h = 0\).

We need an elementary parameter regularity fact for this equation. On continuous matrix paths with the supremum norm define \(T_A V (s) = \int_0^s V (r) A (r) \hspace{0.17em} dr\). Its norm is at most \(\|A\|_{\infty}\). If \(A\) depends linearly on a finite-dimensional parameter \(z\), then \(T_A\) is a continuous linear function of \(z\). For \(z\) near zero, \(\|T_A \| < 1\), and the integral equation has the unique solution

\(\displaystyle U = (I - T_A)^{- 1} 1 = \sum_{k = 0}^{\infty} T_A^k 1.\)

The convergent operator power series makes this an analytic function of \(z\). The given link solution satisfies the same integral equation, so agrees with it. Endpoint evaluation and the analytic local group logarithm therefore give an analytic logarithmic endpoint map \(E (z)\) near zero, with \(E (0) = 0\). This argument applies to the finite-dimensional space of all polynomial-in-\(s\) connection profiles of the indicated degrees, not only to profiles coming from a particular link.

Put \(R = 1 + \text{dist} (e_-, o)\), \(r = R^{- 1}\) and \(y = r \hspace{0.17em} \text{lift} (e_- - o)\). The centered lift has each coordinate at most \(R\) in modulus, so \(|y|_2 \leqslant 2\), while \(0 < r \leqslant 1\). Consider the polynomial profile

\(\displaystyle P (y, r, a, h) (s) = \sum_{j = 0}^{d - 2} ah^{j + 2} J_j (e_i) [y + rse_i, \ldots, y + rse_i] .\)

For all parameters \((y, r, a)\) the profile is zero at \(h = 0\). Consequently

\(\displaystyle Q_k (y, r, a) = \left. \frac{d^k}{dh^k} E (P (y, r, a, h)) \right|_{h = 0}\)

is smooth in \((y, r, a)\): near each point it is obtained by composing a polynomial map with the analytic germ \(E\), then differentiating a fixed finite number of times. Also \(Q_k (y, r, 0) = 0\). On the compact set \(|y|_2 \leqslant 2\), \(0 \leqslant r, a \leqslant 1\), its derivative with respect to \(a\) is bounded. Integrating that derivative from zero to \(a\) gives \(|Q_k (y, r, a) | \leqslant C_{J, d, k, i} a\). This compactness is in the fixed profile-parameter space and has no dimension dependence on the lattice.

Homogeneity of \(J_j\) shows that the actual link at scale \(hr\) and amplitude \(t\) has profile \(P (y, r, tr, h)\): both coefficients are \(th^{j + 2} r^{j + 2} J_j (e_i) [x + se_i, \ldots, x + se_i]\). Uniqueness of the link equation identifies their endpoints. Differentiating this identity at zero gives

\(\displaystyle r^k |Z^{(k)} (0) (e) | \leqslant C_{J, d, k, i} tr.\)

For \(k \geqslant 1\) this is precisely \(|Z^{(k)} (0) (e) | \leqslant C_{J, d, k, i} tR^{k - 1}\). For \(k = 0\) the logarithm is zero. Taking the maximum over the four directions proves (7.56). The argument uses only derivatives at zero; it asserts no common analytic radius for links arbitrarily far from the anchor.

7.9.3The support correction

Choose a linear local section of the labels, supported on a fixed stencil \(Y\) containing \(o\). Its placed field \(P_t (v)\) represents \(D_t^{- 1} v\), has the prescribed jet, and satisfies

\(\displaystyle \|P_t (v)\|_{o, Y} \leqslant w \mu (v), \qquad w \geqslant 0.\) (7.58)

The stencil is assumed to fit without wrapping. The section sends \(e_0\) to the constant one. These are hypotheses about the chosen section; they do not follow from the abstract existence of \(\Pi\). Let \(\chi_X\) be the indicator that \(X\) is healthy, and put

\(\displaystyle p_t (v) = v_0 + \chi_Y P_t (v^{\circ}), \qquad Z = X \cup Y.\)

Then \(|p_t (v) | \leqslant (1 + w) \mu (v)\) everywhere, and \(p_t (v) = P_t (v)\) when \(Y\) is healthy. Define

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} g & = f - P_t (v (f)), \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.59)}\\ r & = \chi_X f - p_t (v (f)) - \chi_Z g. \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.60)} \end{array}\)

These definitions give the exact, pointwise identity

\(\displaystyle \chi_X f = p_t (v (f)) + \chi_Z g + r.\) (7.61)

The field \(g\) is regular on \(Z\). It has zero identity value and, when \(Z\) is nonwrapping, zero test jets through order \(d\). Indeed \(Y\) is nonwrapping, so \(Z\) is nonwrapping exactly when \(X\) is; subtract the two equal jets in that case. In the wrapping case \(v (f) = f (1) e_0\), and the same subtraction gives the zero identity value.

The response \(r\) is measurable, gauge invariant, and determined by the links and final flags in \(Z\). It vanishes whenever \(Z\) is healthy, by (7.61) and \(p_t (v) = P_t (v)\) there. Put \(A_d = 1 + (1 + w) R_d\). Then

\(\displaystyle \|g\|_{o, Z} \leqslant A_d c, \qquad |r (U) | \leqslant A_d c.\) (7.62)

The first estimate is the triangle inequality using (7.57)–(7.58). For the second, \(r = 0\) on the healthy set of \(Z\). On its complement \(\chi_Z = 0\), so \(|r| \leqslant | \chi_X f| + |p_t (v (f)) | \leqslant c + (1 + w) R_d c\). This proves the global bound without differentiating any mask.

7.9.4Putting the response into the existing gas

Let \(K_j\) be the original flagged activities, with supports \(P_j\) and nonempty flag inventories \(I_j \subseteq P_j\). An admissible finite family has disjoint supports and its inventories cover exactly the final flags. Let \(\mathcal{G} (U)\) be its gas sum. For each response \(r_i\) supported on \(Z_i\) and each nonempty pairwise disjoint family \(q\) of old activities meeting \(Z_i\), assume a distinct available address with support and inventory

\(\displaystyle Z_i \cup \bigcup_{j \in q} P_j, \qquad \bigcup_{j \in q} I_j .\)

The address's additional eligibility predicate must hold whenever its support has exactly this flag inventory. No zero value of its old activity is required. Insert at this address the coefficient

\(\displaystyle r_i (U) \prod_{j \in q} K_j (U)\)

when eligible, and zero otherwise. Denote the resulting source array by \(\mathcal{K} (r)\). This is a linear operation in \(r\), with addresses fixed before their values are evaluated.

Its exact marked-gas sum is

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{G}^{[1]} (\mathcal{K}(r)) (U) = \left( \sum_i r_i (U) \right) \mathcal{G} (U) .\) (7.63)

To prove this, take a nonzero term on the right, consisting of \(i\) and an admissible old family \(b\). Because \(r_i\) vanishes on healthy \(Z_i\), some final flag lies in \(Z_i\). Exact inventory coverage implies that the subfamily \(q = \{j \in b : P_j \cap Z_i \ne \varnothing\}\) is nonempty. Replace precisely this subfamily by the designated new address. Every remaining support is disjoint from the new support; the inventories still cover the same flags. This produces an admissible pointed family on the left, with identical value. Conversely, expand the distinguished new address into its encoded family \(q\). The other supports are disjoint from its total support, so \(q\) is exactly the subfamily meeting \(Z_i\). This recovers \(i, b\) uniquely. Thus there is a value-preserving bijection of nonzero terms. Absolute summability, established next, permits reindexing.

Let \(C_B (X)\) be the covering number used in the local norm and set

\(\displaystyle m (X, a) = e^{uC_B (X)} a, \qquad m_+ (X, a) = e^{(u + 2) C_B (X)} a.\)

Suppose \(|K_j | \leqslant b_j\), \(b_j \geqslant 0\), and, for a site bound \(\varsigma \geqslant 0\) (not to be confused with the coefficient vectors \(v\) above), \(\sum_{j : x \in P_j} m (P_j, b_j) \leqslant \varsigma\) at every site \(x\). For one response with \(|r_i | \leqslant a_i\), the inserted array has total mass at most

\(\displaystyle m (Z_i, a_i) (e^{|Z_i | \varsigma} - 1) .\) (7.64)

Indeed the total mass of old activities meeting \(Z_i\) is at most \(|Z_i | \varsigma\), by summing their site bounds over \(Z_i\). The mass of the union is at most the product of the masses, since each old support meets \(Z_i\) and their covering numbers are subadditive along such unions. Summing products over nonempty finite subsets is bounded by \(e^{\sum b} - 1\); dropping the disjointness restriction only increases this nonnegative sum. This proves (7.64). If the sum of the right sides of (7.64) over \(i\) is finite, summing these bounds proves absolute domination of the inserted array and hence of its pointed gas series. The reserved-mass hypothesis below will establish this finiteness. The old gas series is absolutely convergent under the same site bound on the finite lattice. This justifies (7.63).

7.9.5The two-unit reserve in the support exponent

Let \(T\) bound \(C_B (Y)\) and put \(h_B = (2 B + 1)^4\). The elementary covering estimates used here are

\(\displaystyle C_B (X \cup Y) \leqslant C_B (X) + T \quad (o \in X \cap Y), \qquad |X| \leqslant h_B C_B (X) .\)

Set \(F_d = e^{(u + 2) T} A_d\). Then

\(\displaystyle m_+ (X \cup Y, A_d c) \leqslant F_d m_+ (X, c) .\) (7.65)

This follows immediately by inserting the first covering inequality in the exponential. If \(h_B \varsigma \leqslant 1\), then for \(a \geqslant 0\),

\(\displaystyle m (X, a) (e^{|X| \varsigma} - 1) \leqslant h_B \varsigma \hspace{0.17em} m_+ (X, a) .\) (7.66)

For \(D = C_B (X)\), put \(q_0 = h_B \varsigma\). We have \(|X| \varsigma \leqslant Dq_0 \leqslant D\). Using \(e^x - 1 \leqslant xe^x\) for \(x \geqslant 0\) and \(D \leqslant e^D\) gives \(e^{|X| \varsigma} - 1 \leqslant q_0 De^D \leqslant q_0 e^{2 D}\), as required.

Consider now a raw expansion with seed \(v_0\), regular fields \(f_i\) on anchored supports \(X_i\), and an existing forced source \(k\). Suppose \(\|f_i \|_{o, X_i} \leqslant c_i\) and

\(\displaystyle \sum_i m_+ (X_i, c_i) \leqslant a, \qquad \text{mass} (k) \leqslant \kappa b, \qquad \kappa = \sqrt{\varsigma} > 0, \quad h_B \kappa \leqslant 1.\)

All constants \(a, b\) are nonnegative, and \(\varsigma\) is the site bound of the original flagged activities \(K_j\) of the preceding subsection. Form \(v_i = v (f_i)\), \(g_i, r_i\) as above, and define the coefficient field by

\(\displaystyle v = v_0 + \sum_i v_i, \qquad \text{regular terms } g_i \text{ on } X_i \cup Y, \qquad \text{forced array } k +\mathcal{K} (r) .\) (7.67)

The coordinate sum converges absolutely by (7.57) and \(\sum_i c_i \leqslant a\). Its change is bounded by \(R_d a\). The regular remainder mass is at most \(F_d a\). Furthermore \(h_B \varsigma = h_B \kappa^2 \leqslant \kappa \leqslant 1\), so (7.66) and (7.65) bound the added forced mass by \(\kappa F_d a\). Measuring forced mass in units of \(\kappa\), so that the forced array has normalized mass at most \(b + F_d a\), the normalized total remainder bound is

\(\displaystyle 2 F_d a + b.\) (7.68)

The original density is preserved exactly: sum (7.61), use linearity of \(p_t\), and then (7.63) for the response terms. Multiplication by the common vacuum exponential preserves this identity. Every sum is absolutely dominated by the bounds just proved. In particular this construction uses the original vacuum activities and the original threshold predicates throughout.

Finally, collect the responses with the same support: \(\psi (Z) = \sum_{i : X_i \cup Y = Z} r_i\). Their majorants obey

\(\displaystyle \sum_Z m_+ \left( Z, \sum_{i : X_i \cup Y = Z} A_d c_i \right) \leqslant F_d a.\)

Consequently \(\psi\) is an anchored, gauge-invariant response vanishing on healthy supports, with the stated reserved mass. Its insertion profile is exactly the profile of \(\mathcal{K} (r)\): grouping the absolutely convergent terms by their encoded support and inventory proves the equality. These are the response and profile identities needed by the marked transport of Subsection 7.8. The raw expansion and its bounds still have to be produced by the one-step integral; this section proves the projection, mask correction, and cost conversion once that expansion is given.

7.10Constructing the local polynomial sections

We now construct the fixed section and its bound used in Subsection 7.9. The construction uses a finite collection of based, transported plaquettes. The finite collection and all constants are chosen before the lattice period and threshold.

7.10.1A finite sampler for radial jets

Let \(\mathcal{J}_d\) be the finite-dimensional real space of radial connection jets \(A = (A_0, \ldots, A_{d - 2})\). Here \(A_j\) is a homogeneous one-form of polynomial degree \(j + 1\) satisfying the radial identity \(\sum_i x_i A_{j, i} (x) = 0\). It is this radial description of curvature jets that underlies the label space. If \(d < 2\), the space is zero. For a lattice path from the origin to \(x\) and an oriented plaquette at \(x\), let \(s (U)\) be the Lie-algebra curvature of that plaquette, transported back to the origin. Its value is zero at the identity. Define the sampler \(S_s (A)\) by evaluating \(s\) on the test holonomy of the truncated radial connection \(A\) at scale one.

The endpoint argument in Subsection 7.9 shows that \(S_s\) is analytic near zero. Differentiating the link integral equation at the zero connection gives the line integral of \(A\). At the identity, the derivative of a product is the sum of its factor derivatives, and inversion changes their signs. The derivative of transported plaquette curvature is consequently

\(\displaystyle DS_s (0) A = \oint_{\partial \square_{x, ij}} \sum_{l = 0}^{d - 2} A_l .\)

Transport along the based path contributes nothing to this derivative: it conjugates a curvature whose value at zero is zero. The Lie projection of the linearized plaquette is its Lie-algebra value.

These linear samplers jointly separate \(\mathcal{J}_d\). To see this, for a homogeneous \(A_l\) write

\(\displaystyle N_l (x, h) = \int_0^1 \hspace{-0.17em} \int_0^1 \partial_i A_{l, j} (x + hse_j + hre_i) \hspace{0.17em} dr \hspace{0.17em} ds - \int_0^1 \hspace{-0.17em} \int_0^1 \partial_j A_{l, i} (x + hse_i + hre_j) \hspace{0.17em} dr \hspace{0.17em} ds.\)

The fundamental theorem of calculus identifies the unit-square circulation with \(N_l (x, 1)\). Also \(N_l (hx, h) = h^l N_l (x, 1)\) and \(N_l (x, 0) = \partial_i A_{l, j} (x) - \partial_j A_{l, i} (x)\). Suppose all sampled circulations vanish. If \(k\) is the largest remaining degree, multiply the identity at an integer point \(z\) by \(h^k\) and use homogeneity to get

\(\displaystyle \sum_{l = 0}^k h^{k - l} N_l (hz, h) = 0.\)

For any real \(x\), choose integer \(z_h\) with \(hz_h \to x\) as \(h \downarrow 0\). Continuity gives \(N_k (x, 0) = 0\). Thus \(dA_k = 0\). The radial identity and Euler's identity give, componentwise,

\(\displaystyle \sum_i x_i (\partial_i A_{k, j} - \partial_j A_{k, i}) = (k + 2) A_{k, j} .\)

Indeed the first term is \((k + 1) A_{k, j}\), and differentiation of \(\sum_i x_i A_{k, i} = 0\) makes the second term \(- A_{k, j}\). Hence \(A_k = 0\). Descending induction proves \(A = 0\).

A finite subcollection already separates. Start with the whole space as the common kernel. If that kernel is nonzero, one of the samplers is nonzero on a vector in it; adding that sampler strictly reduces the kernel dimension. After finitely many steps the kernel is zero. Fix the resulting slots \(s_1, \ldots, s_m\) and the analytic map \(S = (S_{s_1}, \ldots, S_{s_m})\) into the panel of \(m\) Lie-algebra values. Its derivative \(D = DS (0)\) is injective.

7.10.2Equivariant recovery and polynomial substitution

The panel has its invariant Euclidean inner product. Constant gauge transformations act orthogonally there, preserve \(\text{ran} \hspace{0.17em} D\), and intertwine \(D\) with their action on \(\mathcal{J}_d\). Let \(\pi\) be orthogonal projection onto \(\text{ran} \hspace{0.17em} D\) and put \(C = D^{- 1} \pi\). The range and its orthogonal complement are invariant, so \(C\) is equivariant and \(CD = I\). The analytic inverse function theorem applied to \(CS\) at zero gives an analytic local inverse \(g\). Set \(F = gC\). Then

\(\displaystyle F (0) = 0, \qquad F (S (A)) = A\)

near zero. For each fixed gauge transformation, equivariance of \(CS\) and uniqueness of its local inverse imply equivariance of \(F\) as germs. This assertion needs no common invariant neighborhood for all transformations simultaneously.

Let \(T_d F\) be the Taylor polynomial of this one recovery map through ordinary degree \(d\). It is equivariant: differentiate the germ identity \(F (Rx) = R' F (x)\) at zero, and use linearity of \(R, R'\) in each Taylor coefficient. It has zero constant term. For each real homogeneous basis label \(p_{\alpha}\) define a polynomial in panel coordinates by

\(\displaystyle P_{\alpha} (x) = p_{\alpha} (T_d F (x)) .\)

It is gauge invariant and real valued on real panels, and \(P_0 = 1\). If \(p_{\alpha}\) has \(r_{\alpha}\) curvature factors, every monomial of \(P_{\alpha}\) has ordinary panel degree at least \(r_{\alpha}\): each substituted coordinate has zero constant term, so each of the \(r_{\alpha}\) factors contributes at least one panel coordinate. No deletion of higher-degree terms after substitution is necessary.

These polynomials read the correct jets. Along the radial curve \(A (h) = h \cdot J\), put \(W (h) = S (A (h))\). It is smooth and \(W (0) = 0\). The Taylor polynomial \(T_d F\) and \(F\) have identical derivatives through order \(d\) at zero, so their compositions with \(W\) do too. Since \(F (W (h)) = A (h)\) near zero, composition with the polynomial \(p_{\alpha}\) gives

\(\displaystyle P_{\alpha} (s_1 (H (J, h)), \ldots, s_m (H (J, h))) \quad \text{and} \quad p_{\alpha} (h \cdot J)\)

the same derivatives through order \(d\). Linearly extending the \(P_{\alpha}\) therefore gives a local section \(T_0\) of every label in \(V_d\) at the level of test jets.

7.10.3Lattice symmetry and a common stencil

Let \(\mathcal{H}\) be the finite group of signed coordinate permutations. For its action \(\rho\) on labels, define

\(\displaystyle T (v) (U) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}|} \sum_{s \in \mathcal{H}} T_0 (\rho (s^{- 1}) v) (\text{pull}_s \hspace{0.17em} U) .\) (7.69)

The test holonomy transforms by the inverse tensor action. This can be checked directly in the link equation. Positive coordinate changes transport its coefficient. For a reversed link, if \(u' = uA\), then \(u (1)^{- 1} u (1 - h)\) solves the reversed equation with coefficient \(- A (1 - h)\) and starts at the identity. Uniqueness identifies its endpoint with the inverse link. Constant conjugation likewise conjugates the solution of the equation. These identities prove the stated tensor and gauge transformation laws of the sampler.

It follows that each summand of (7.69) has label \(v\). Their average does also. Reindexing the finite group proves the signed-permutation covariance \(T (v) (\text{pull}_s \hspace{0.17em} U) = T (\rho (s) v) (U)\). Its unit is one, and it commutes with ordinary conjugation because the basis labels and the initial panel polynomials are real. Each transported plaquette transforms into another transported plaquette, possibly with a minus sign from reversing its orientation. Take the finite union of the slots in all these terms and enclose their paths and plaquettes in a centered box \(Y\).

The average remains polynomial in this common finite list of slots. Replacing a slot by a signed slot changes no monomial degree. Moreover \(\rho (s)\) preserves the number of curvature factors in a homogeneous label. Thus the polynomial representing the column \(T (e_{\alpha})\) still has no terms of panel degree below \(r_{\alpha}\). Each slot is itself a polynomial in the positive-link matrix entries and their conjugates: use matrix multiplication for the path and plaquette, conjugate transpose for inverse links, and the real-linear Lie projection of \((M - M^{\ast}) / 2\) for curvature. A real-linear matrix functional is a linear combination of real and imaginary entry parts, each a linear combination of entries and their conjugates. Therefore \(T\) is a genuine finite polynomial cylinder on \(Y\).

7.10.4Threshold bounds at every period

Fix derivative order \(q\) and spatial power \(p\). For any period, anchor \(o\) and allowed direction \(A\), a link from the fixed stencil satisfies \(|A (e) | \leqslant C_Y t\), since its torus distance from \(o\) is at most the size of its chosen covering-space position. An ordered left derivative of a positive-link matrix for the list \((A_1, \ldots, A_k)\) is exactly \(\text{col} (A_k (e)) \cdots \text{col} (A_1 (e)) U_e\), where \(\text{col}\) is the Lie-algebra matrix representation. The inverse link is its conjugate transpose. Consequently all derivatives of order \(k \leqslant q\) of any fixed path matrix are bounded by \(Ct^k\), uniformly in the period and \(t\). The product rule has finitely many terms, and compactness of the matrix group bounds the undifferentiated factors. Smoothness holds globally in exponential coordinates.

The same derivative bound holds for each transported plaquette slot. At order zero a stronger estimate comes from health: on a configuration healthy on the projected stencil, the plaquette curvature has norm at most \(t\), and parallel transport preserves that norm. At every positive order \(k\), the previous bound is at most \(Ct\) because \(0 < t \leqslant 1\). Thus each slot and each of its tested derivatives through order \(q\) has norm at most \(Ct\) on that healthy set. This also holds when the stencil wraps: the path identities and the bound on torus distance still apply.

Apply the ordered product rule to a monomial with \(r\) slot factors. Each term assigns the derivatives to those factors in their original orders. Every factor, including those receiving no derivative, contributes a bound \(C_i t\). The monomial is therefore bounded by \(Ct^r\) through order \(q\). Finite summation and \(t \leqslant 1\) give

\(\displaystyle \|T (e_{\alpha})\|_{o, Y} \leqslant C_{d, q, p} t^{r_{\alpha}} .\) (7.70)

This proves both regularity and the exact factor-count threshold power of the constructed section. Multiplying its column coefficients by \(t^{- r_{\alpha}}\) and using the triangle inequality yields (7.58) with \(w = C_{d, q, p}\).

All choices are independent of the period and threshold. They can also be priced before choosing a scale: for an integer \(D\), choose one bound for each triple \(d, q, p \leqslant D\) and set

\(\displaystyle w_D = 1 + \sum_{d, q, p \leqslant D} \max (0, C_{d, q, p}) .\)

It bounds every requested bound in this finite range. The stencil fits whenever the side exceeds \(\max_{x \in Y, i} 20 (|x_i | + 1)\). These are fixed local requirements for the later calendar to satisfy. The construction here supplies the section and its uniform bound; it does not choose that calendar.

7.11Polynomial moments and decay of irrelevant insertions

The subtraction in Subsection 7.9 removes the low-order holonomy tests of an insertion. We now explain why this improves its coarse-to-fine estimate. The argument has two parts: a qualitative implication from holonomy tests to polynomial moments, and a quantitative decomposition of the actual interpolating field. Both use the same centered lattice lift as the local subtraction.

7.11.1From holonomy tests to polynomial moments

Let \(X\) be a nonwrapping support about \(o\) on a torus of side at least two. Let \(f\) be local on \(X\), gauge invariant, and \(C^d\) in logarithmic coordinates near the identity. Suppose that every holonomy test through degree \(d\) vanishes:

\(\displaystyle \left. \frac{d^j}{dt^j} f (H_o (J, t)) \right|_{t = 0} = 0, \qquad 0 \leqslant j \leqslant d, \quad \text{every } J.\) (7.71)

Tests with a larger available cutoff are allowed; only degrees through \(d\) enter the argument. For \(a \geqslant 1\) let \(\mathcal{M}_a\) be the space of link fields screened to the links with both endpoints in \(X\), whose value on a link in direction \(i\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(a - 1\) in \(x = \text{lift} (e_- - o)\). We claim that

\(\displaystyle D^m (f \circ \exp) (0) [v_1, \ldots, v_m] = 0 \quad \text{if } v_i |_X \in \mathcal{M}_{a_i}, \quad a_i \geqslant 1, \quad \sum_i a_i \leqslant d.\) (7.72)

The restriction means screening the link field, not restricting its support before applying the coarse-to-fine map.

Here are the details of the change of coordinates behind this claim. Introduce a site gauge parameter \(q\) and the finite list \(J\) of radial connection coefficients of weights \(2, 3, \ldots\). For each link set

\(\displaystyle \Psi (q, J)_e = \log (e^{q (e_-)} H_o (J, 1)_e e^{- q (e_+)})\)

on the links tested by \(X\), and set it to zero elsewhere. This is an analytic germ with \(\Psi (0) = 0\). The endpoint map is analytic by the integral-equation argument in Subsection 7.9. Locality and gauge invariance give, as germs,

\(\displaystyle (f \circ \exp) \circ \Psi (q, J) = f (H_o (J, 1)) .\) (7.73)

The identity uses only tested links and a finite common chart.

Give a direction \((q, J)\) filtration degree \(a\) when \(q\) is polynomial of degree at most \(a\) on \(X\) and the components of \(J\) of weight greater than \(a\) vanish. Denote this space by \(\mathcal{K}_a\). The following two properties hold:

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} D \Psi (0) \mathcal{K}_a & =\mathcal{M}_a, & & 1 \leqslant a \leqslant d, \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.74)} \\ D^r \Psi (0) [u_1, \ldots, u_r] & \in \mathcal{M}_{\sum_i a_i}, & & u_i \in \mathcal{K}_{a_i}, r \geqslant 1. \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.75)} \end{array}\)

We prove them, since the nonlinear coordinate change matters.

For the first property the linearized link is

\(\displaystyle D \Psi (0) (q, J)_{(x, i)} = q (x) - q (x + e_i) + \int_0^1 A_J (x + se_i) (e_i) \hspace{0.17em} ds.\)

Nonwrapping and side at least two identify the centered lift at the endpoint with \(x + e_i\). Every constant one-form is the gradient of a linear site polynomial, so it occurs with \(J = 0\). For a homogeneous polynomial one-form \(Q\) of degree \(k \geqslant 1\), put

\(\displaystyle r (x) = \frac{1}{k + 1} \sum_i x_i Q_i (x), \qquad K_i = Q_i - \partial_i r.\)

Euler's identity gives \(\sum_i x_i K_i (x) = 0\). Thus \(K\) is precisely an admissible radial connection coefficient of weight \(k + 1\). Taking \(q = - r\) and that coefficient gives the link average of \(Q\). The difference between this average and \(Q_i (x)\) has degree at most \(k - 1\): expand \(Q_i (x + se_i)\) as a polynomial in \(s\), and observe that its constant term is \(Q_i (x)\). Induction on the degree supplies the difference by lower-weight parameters. Splitting a general polynomial into homogeneous parts proves (7.74).

For (7.75), write the link germ in variables

\(\displaystyle q (x), \qquad q (x + e_i) - q (x), \qquad A_J (x + se_i) .\)

For a direction of degree \(a\), the first expression has degree at most \(a\) in \(x\), and each of the other two has degree at most \(a - 1\). When both of the latter expressions vanish, the link germ is identically zero, since \(e^{q (x)} 1 e^{- q (x)} = 1\). Expand any multilinear derivative according to these three components in each argument. The term using only the first components vanishes. Every remaining term contains a component with a one-degree loss; its degree is at most \(\sum_i a_i - 1\). Screening completes (7.75).

Equation (7.71) implies that all mixed derivatives of the right side of (7.73) with total filtration weight at most \(d\) vanish. To verify this precisely, take its Taylor polynomial through ordinary degree \(d\) in the finite coordinates \(J\). Substitute the weighted scaling \(J_a \mapsto t^a J_a\). The coefficient of every \(t^j\), \(j \leqslant d\), is zero for all \(J\), by (7.71). Each such coefficient is a polynomial, hence all its monomial coefficients vanish. Polarization gives the asserted mixed derivatives. Gauge-coordinate derivatives vanish because the right side is independent of \(q\).

Apply the chain rule to the left side of (7.73). Induct on the number \(m\) of arguments in (7.72), lifting each \(v_i\) to \(u_i \in \mathcal{K}_{a_i}\) by (7.74). The partition into singletons contributes exactly the desired derivative of \(f \circ \exp\). Every other partition has fewer than \(m\) blocks; by (7.75), its arguments have the block weights, whose sum is still \(\sum_i a_i\). The induction hypothesis kills all these terms. The whole derivative of the composite is zero, so the singleton term is zero too. The case \(m = 0\) is \(f (1) = 0\). Finally, locality makes \(f \circ \exp\) equal to its composition with the linear screening map. Differentiating that identity allows unscreened directions whose restrictions have the stated polynomial form. This proves (7.72).

7.11.2A discrete Taylor decomposition on the cover

Write \(|x|_1 = \sum_{i = 1}^4 |x_i |\) for \(x \in \mathbb{Z}^4\) and \(\Delta_i v (x) = v (x + e_i) - v (x)\). For a word \(w\) in the four axes let \(\Delta_w\) be the corresponding iterated difference. Suppose \(d, p \in \mathbb{N}\), \(L \geqslant 1\), \(p \geqslant d\), \(M \geqslant 0\), \(\theta > 0\), and a colour-valued cover field \(v\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \| \Delta_w v (x)\| \leqslant M \theta L^{- 1 - |w|} (1 + |x|_1 / L)^p, \qquad |w| \leqslant d.\) (7.76)

There are polynomial pieces \(P_j\), \(0 \leqslant j < d\), and a remainder \(R_d\) such that

\(\displaystyle v (x) = \sum_{j < d} P_j (x) + R_d (x), \qquad \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \deg P_j \leqslant j,\\ \|P_j (x)\| \leqslant C_{d, p} M \theta L^{- j - 1} (1 + |x|_1)^p,\\ \|R_d (x)\| \leqslant C_{d, p} M \theta L^{- d - 1} (1 + |x|_1)^p . \end{array} \right.\) (7.77)

The constant precedes the choice of \(L\) and of the period.

For completeness use the total-degree Newton polynomials

\(\displaystyle P_j (x) = \sum_{| \alpha | = j} \binom{x}{\alpha} \Delta^{\alpha} v (0), \qquad \binom{x}{\alpha} = \prod_{i = 1}^4 \frac{x_i (x_i - 1) \cdots (x_i - \alpha_i + 1)}{\alpha_i !} .\)

These formulas are polynomial identities for real \(x\), so allow negative lattice offsets. The identity \(\binom{t + 1}{a} - \binom{t}{a} = \binom{t}{a - 1}\) implies

\(\displaystyle \Delta_i P_{j + 1} (v) = P_j (\Delta_i v), \qquad P_j (v) (0) = 0 (j > 0) .\)

They are also the polynomials obtained by the recursion

\(\displaystyle P_{j + 1} (v) (x) = (j + 1)^{- 1} \sum_i x_i P_j (\Delta_i v) (x - e_i) .\)

To check this, expand the falling factorials and use \(\sum_i \alpha_i = j + 1\). Their coefficients give

\(\displaystyle \|P_j (x)\| \leqslant c_j M \theta L^{- j - 1} (1 + |x|_1)^j, \qquad c_j = [4 (j + 1)]^j .\)

Indeed each falling-factorial factor is bounded by \((1 + |x|_1 + j)^j\), while summing the reciprocal factorials at fixed degree contributes \(4^j / j! \leqslant 4^j\).

If \(|x|_1 \leqslant L\), all differences of order \(d\) in the ball of radius \(L\) are at most \(2^p M \theta L^{- d - 1}\). The remainder \(R_d = v - \sum_{j < d} P_j\) is zero at zero for \(d > 0\), and \(\Delta_i R_d (v) = R_{d - 1} (\Delta_i v)\). Induction on \(d\), followed by summation along a shortest signed coordinate path from zero to \(x\), gives

\(\displaystyle \|R_d (x)\| \leqslant 2^p M \theta L^{- d - 1} (1 + |x|_1)^d .\)

For a negative step the forward difference is evaluated at the new endpoint, which still lies in the same ball. Thus this summation does not assume positive offsets. Since \(p \geqslant d\), it is bounded by the remainder in (7.77). If \(|x|_1 > L\), then

\(\displaystyle (1 + |x|_1 / L)^p \leqslant 2^p L^{- p} (1 + |x|_1)^p\)

and, for \(j < d\), \(L^{d - j} \leqslant (1 + |x|_1)^{d - j}\). Apply these inequalities to (7.76) with the empty word and to the piece bounds, then use \(R_d = v - \sum_{j < d} P_j\). This gives (7.77) with \(C_{d, p} = 2^p + \sum_{j < d} c_j\). When \(d = 0\) only the remainder is present, and the original bound suffices.

Evaluate these pieces at the actual centered lift on the torus. The lift satisfies \(| \text{lift} (y - o) |_1 \leqslant 4 \text{dist} (y, o)\). Multiplying the constant by \(4^p\) therefore gives a global decomposition into \(d\) polynomial pieces and a last remainder. The screened \(j\)th piece belongs to \(\mathcal{M}_{j + 1}\). Nothing is required of the polynomial behavior of the last remainder, including across the lift boundary.

7.11.3Applying the decomposition to the actual kernel

The coarse-to-fine linear field is the installed \(A_0\) kernel, with the physical normalization of Subsection 3.7. We assume that its iterated spatial differences obey the strip estimates

\(\displaystyle \| \Delta_w A_0 \|_{\alpha} \leqslant AL^{- 1 - |w|}, \qquad |w| \leqslant d,\)

for some strip rate \(\alpha > 0\) and some constant \(A\), both fixed before the block factor \(L\) and independent of \(L\) and of the lattice size. These are the \(A_0\) difference bounds in Subsection 3.10. That section supplies them only for words of length at most \(j - 2\), where \(j \geqslant 4\) is the smoothing order of the stage (the order-\((j - 2)\) difference bounds of the block completion; compare Theorem 3.21, stated for words of length \(s \leqslant j - 2\)). The application below therefore requires

\(\displaystyle d \leqslant j - 2,\)

or else a separate estimate of the differences of orders \(j - 1, \ldots, d\); no such estimate is provided here. No assertion about the other tower blocks is needed. If a coarse input obeys \(\|u (y)\| \leqslant \theta (1 + \text{dist} (y, o_c))^p\), where \(o_c\) is the parent of the chosen fine anchor \(o\), use the installed row at \(o + \text{wrap} (x)\). Its summation addresses are the parent of this site plus \(\text{wrap} (z)\), where \(z\) is the cover displacement in that residue row. The parent map can cross a block boundary; its centered residue changes at the same time. The coordinate division with remainder gives

\(\displaystyle 1 + \text{dist} (\text{parent} (o + \text{wrap} (x)) + \text{wrap} (z), o_c) \leqslant 3 (1 + |x|_1 / L) (1 + |z|_1) .\)

Indeed in each coordinate the parent displacement is the integer quotient of \(x\) plus a residue correction of absolute value at most one; periodic distance can only decrease this bound. Taking the maximum of the coordinate bounds and then adding the cover shift gives the displayed factor three. Taking absolute values in the convolution now costs \(3^p (1 + |x|_1 / L)^p\) times the strip row sum with the additional weight \((1 + |z|_1)^p\). The inequality \((1 + t)^p \leqslant C_{p, \alpha} e^{\alpha t}\) absorbs that weight into the available strip estimate. Sum also over the fixed form types. Differences can be taken on the row before summation by absolute convergence; the installed odd-block carry identifies them with the cover forward differences. We obtain (7.76), with \(M\) depending only on \(A, \alpha, p\) and the fixed component count. The preceding decomposition therefore applies to this actual field, uniformly in the lattice size and block factor.

7.11.4Quantitative cancellation and displacement

Let \(\| \cdot \|_P\) denote the direction norm

\(\displaystyle \|v\|_P = \sup_e \frac{\|v_e \|}{\theta (1 + \text{dist} (o, e_-))^p} .\)

Say that a direction admits a graded decomposition at scale \(0 \leqslant \xi \leqslant 1\) and cost \(C \geqslant 1\) if

\(\displaystyle v = \sum_{a = 1}^{d + 1} v_a, \qquad \|v_a \|_P \leqslant C \xi^a, \qquad v_a |_X \in \mathcal{M}_a \quad (a \leqslant d) .\)

The preceding construction gives this property with \(\xi = L^{- 1}\). Suppose the regular derivative bound of \(F = f \circ \exp\) is

\(\displaystyle |D^m F (b) [v_1, \ldots, v_m] | \leqslant Q \tau \prod_i \|v_i \|_P, \qquad m \leqslant n,\) (7.78)

on the logarithmic region used below, with \(n \geqslant d + 1\). Here \(Q\) is the fixed conversion constant from the local regularity norm, and \(\tau\) is its bound. The chart and health conditions in that norm are retained in (7.78).

At zero, expand each argument into its \(d + 1\) pieces. All terms of total weight at most \(d\) vanish by (7.72). Every remaining term has a factor at most \(C^m \xi^{d + 1}\). There are \((d + 1)^m\) terms. For \(m \leqslant n\) this proves

\(\displaystyle |D^m F (0) [v_1, \ldots, v_m] | \leqslant Q \tau ((d + 1) C)^n \xi^{d + 1} = : S.\)

Now let the displacement \(b\) have the same decomposition, and suppose that the segment \(tb\), \(0 \leqslant t \leqslant 1\), remains in the healthy logarithmic region, with the tested links and direction widths bounded by one as required by the regular norm. At order \(n\), (7.78) bounds the derivative on this segment by \(S\), since each argument has norm at most \((d + 1) C \xi\) and \(n \geqslant d + 1\). If the order-\(m + 1\) derivative is bounded there by \((n - m) S\), the fundamental theorem of calculus applied to \(t \mapsto D^m F (tb) [v_1, \ldots, v_m]\) bounds its change from zero by \((n - m) S\). Its value at zero is at most \(S\). Descending induction gives the uniform bound \((n + 1) S\) at every order through \(n\). This proves the attenuation after displacement; it does not require differentiating the health indicator.

7.12The elementary one-step projection estimate

We connect the local splitting to the elementary insertion estimate used in Subsection 15.1. The analytic input is now the localized expansion of a real inserted field under one fixed nonlinear block integral. We state the part of that expansion used here and prove the remaining conversion and its constants.

Let \(\mathcal{T}\) be the fine-to-coarse density transfer with block ratio \(L\). Let \(M\) be one real elementary insertion. It has a local ingredient \(\phi_M\), a real function of the fine field with anchored fine support \(X\) and majorant \(c\), that is \(| \phi_M | \leqslant c\), and mass \(m = m_{\text{fine}} (X, c) \geqslant 0\). Its density \(f = \text{dens} (M)\) is the ingredient inserted in the fine background gas: the fine background weight times the sum, over admissible families of the remaining fine activities, of \(\phi_M\) times the product of those activities. The support and the majorant refer to the ingredient; the transfer \(\mathcal{T}\) acts on the density. This is the reading of the consumer in Subsection 15.1, which writes \(\text{dens} (Q_M) = L^d \mathcal{T} \text{dens} (M)\). Use the same fixed coarse background, threshold and activity alphabet as the unmarked transition. A localized expansion has real regular terms \(h_i\) on anchored coarse supports \(X_i\) and a real flagged source array \(k\) such that

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{T}f = \rho_{\text{coarse}} \sum_i \chi_{X_i} h_i + e^{E_{\text{coarse}}} \mathcal{G}^{[1]} (k) .\) (7.79)

All regular and marked series must be absolutely dominated, and the original insertion must be integrable under the fine prior and every transition slice. These conditions are part of the analytic expansion. They justify the displayed identity as an identity of actual integrals. In addition, require the marked identity: the raw forced profile of \(k\) is the transferred singleton profile of the insertion in its fixed ordinary or flagged input slot, with the same background and activity alphabet. This profile identity is a separate part of the expansion hypothesis.

Split the localized terms into the distinguished principal term, when present, and the remaining terms. The required bounds have the following form, in the coarse derivative norm and reserved support mass:

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \sum_{i \text{nonprincipal}} m_+ (X_i, \|h_i \|) & \leqslant M, \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.80)} \\ m_+ (X_{\ast}, \|h_{\ast} \|) & \leqslant V \hspace{0.17em} m / g, \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.81)} \\ \text{mass} (k) & \leqslant Ds. \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.82)} \end{array}\)

Here \(g > 0\) is the normalization gain, \(V \geqslant 0\) a fixed-order derivative bound, \(D \geqslant 0\) the insertion debt, and \(s \geqslant 0\) the flagged-response bound. For a flagged elementary insertion there is no principal term. For a regular elementary insertion there is just one, at the fixed principal location. The indices and supports are selected before forming complex linear combinations.

7.12.1The principal-term reserve

The principal estimate (7.81) follows from three more primitive bounds used by the local integral expansion. Write \(D_X = C_B (X_{\ast})\), let \(\omega \geqslant 0\) be its localized majorant, and suppose

\(\displaystyle \|h_{\ast} \| \leqslant D \omega \hspace{0.17em} \Phi (X_{\ast}), \qquad \omega e^{(a + e) D_X} \leqslant 1, \qquad D \leqslant m / g,\)

where \(e \geqslant 0\) and \(a \geqslant u + 3\). Its derivative bound has the form \(\Phi (X_{\ast}) \leqslant Ve^{D_X / 2}\). The latter is the polynomial-to-exponential bound on the support size: for a fixed integer \(r \geqslant 0\) one can take \(C_r = \sup_{z \geqslant 0} z^r e^{- z / 2} < \infty\) and bound \(z^r\) by \(C_r e^{z / 2}\). The additional fixed factor in \(V\) is the spatial weight of the derivative directions at the given coupling. Consequently

\(\displaystyle e^{(u + 2) D_X} \|h_{\ast} \| \leqslant DV \omega e^{(u + 5 / 2) D_X} \leqslant DV \omega e^{(a + e) D_X} \leqslant Vm / g.\)

If \(D_X \geqslant B\), the same calculation leaves a factor \(e^{- B / 2}\), because \(a + e - (u + 5 / 2) \geqslant 1 / 2\). This is the stronger principal estimate used when its support is large. The local derivative estimate and the weighted bound on \(\omega\) remain substantive inputs from the nonlinear expansion.

7.12.2Complex fields and the raw expansion

To treat a complex insertion, take two real elementary insertions whose ingredients have the same support, type and majorant, let \(f_1, f_2\) be their densities, and combine them as \(f = f_1 + if_2\). Their masses are the same \(m\); their principal support and index agree. For \(z \in \mathbb{C}\), define

\(\displaystyle H_i = z (h_i^{(1)} + ih_i^{(2)}), \qquad K = z (k^{(1)} + ik^{(2)}) .\)

The sum of the two real majorants times \(|z|\) dominates each complex term. Thus the nonprincipal reserved mass is at most \(2 |z| M\), the principal reserved mass is at most \(2 |z| Vm / g\), and \(\text{mass} (K) \leqslant 2 |z| Ds\). The raw seed is zero. Pad the common countable list by zero terms on the singleton anchor support if a natural-number indexing is needed; neither the represented density nor its cost changes.

Linearity of masks, marked-gas insertion and \(\mathcal{T}\), with absolute convergence of the series, gives the exact raw density \(z\mathcal{T} (f_1 + if_2)\). This is the reason the same supports and principal indices were retained. Applying the splitting of Subsection 7.9 to these raw terms preserves the identity. That splitting has two hypotheses on the coarse data, which we assume here and list among the inputs at the end of this section:

    (A)

    Addresses. For each response \(r_i\) produced by the splitting, supported on \(Z_i\), and each nonempty pairwise disjoint family \(q\) of original coarse flagged activities meeting \(Z_i\), there is a distinct available address with support \(Z_i \cup \bigcup_{j \in q} P_j\), flag inventory \(\bigcup_{j \in q} I_j\), and additional eligibility predicate satisfied whenever its support has exactly this inventory (Subsection 7.9, “Putting the response into the existing gas”).

    (B)

    Background bound. The original coarse flagged activities \(K_j\) have majorants \(|K_j | \leqslant b_j\), \(b_j \geqslant 0\), with site bound \(\varsigma = \kappa^2\): \(\sum_{j : x \in P_j} m (P_j, b_j) \leqslant \kappa^2\) at every coarse site \(x\), where \(\kappa > 0\) is the constant of (7.83).

In the elementary application take \(z = L^d\) and \(f_2 = 0\) with the same majorant as \(f_1\). The bound with the factor two remains valid and avoids making the chosen dictionary depend on whether one component happens to vanish.

7.12.3The projection allowance

Let \(F_d\) be the support-join and jet-extraction factor from (7.65), let \(C_{\text{orb}} \geqslant 1\) be the finite coefficient-action bound, and let \(C_{\text{turn}} \geqslant 1\) be the bound for transporting the local remainder norm under the required lattice motions. Put

\(\displaystyle H_d = 1 + 2 F_d (1 + C_{\text{orb}} + C_{\text{turn}}), \qquad \eta_0 = \text{rate} (L) / H_d .\)

Assume the already chosen coupling satisfies

\(\displaystyle 2 L^d M \leqslant \eta_0 m, \qquad 2 \kappa^{- 1} L^d Ds \leqslant \eta_0 m, \qquad (2 B + 1)^4 \kappa \leqslant 1,\) (7.83)

where \(\kappa > 0\) is the square root of the coarse forced allowance, that is of the site bound \(\varsigma\) in hypothesis (B). The first two inequalities are required uniformly over insertions. They are paid by the same coupling delay as the chosen nonlinear transition; choosing a different history here is not allowed.

Set

\(\displaystyle E = 2 L^d V / g, \qquad e_0 = (\eta_0 + |E|) m.\)

Then \(e_0 \geqslant \eta_0 m \geqslant 0\). The principal bound is at most \(e_0\), and the nonprincipal bound is at most \(\eta_0 m\). The total raw regular reserved mass is therefore at most \(\eta_0 m + e_0 \leqslant 2 e_0\). Write \(p_0 = F_d (\eta_0 m + e_0) \leqslant 2 F_d e_0\) and \(b_0 = 2 \kappa^{- 1} L^d Ds \leqslant e_0\). The local splitting gives a coefficient change at most \(p_0\), a regular remainder of mass at most \(p_0\), a normalized forced remainder of mass at most \(b_0 + p_0\), and a response of reserved mass at most \(p_0\). To bound the coefficient change by \(p_0\), use \(R_d \leqslant F_d\), which follows from \(w \geqslant 0\) and the definition of \(F_d\).

It follows directly that

\(\displaystyle C_{\text{orb}} \mu (q) \leqslant C_{\text{orb}} p_0 \leqslant H_d e_0, \qquad C_{\text{turn}} p_0 + (b_0 + p_0) \leqslant H_d e_0 .\)

The response cost is also at most \(H_d e_0\). Therefore any \(Q_e\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle Q_e \geqslant (\eta_0 + |2L^d V / g|) H_d\) (7.84)

gives a coefficient field with coefficient and remainder bounds \(Q_e m\) and exact density \(L^d \mathcal{T}f = L^d \mathcal{T} \text{dens} (M)\). This is the numerical projection bound in (15.1).

The marked transition is retained as well. The original real insertion occupies its fixed ordinary or flagged slot with cost \(m\); the real/imaginary convention just used gives the available bound \(2 m\). Its raw output forced profile is the transferred singleton profile. Splitting adds exactly the hook of the response \(\psi\) constructed in Subsection 7.9, of cost at most \(Q_e m\). Thus the transition identity has fine cost \(2 m\), coarse response cost \(Q_e m\), and multiplier \(L^d\), precisely as required in Subsection 15.1. This last assertion uses the marked profile hypothesis stated alongside (7.79); the scalar density identity alone would not suffice.

For the selected transition, Subsection 9.1 constructs the localized expansion and gives the singleton, tail and flag estimates. Subsection 9.2 checks the address and background conditions and bounds the small terms in (7.83); Subsection 19.4 uses them on the selected history. The finite projection, support correction, principal reserve conversion, and passage to the elementary allowance have now been proved from those inputs.
