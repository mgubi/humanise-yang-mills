6.1Complex heat time and real-time translations

The complex heat extension used in the boundary argument can be constructed from the real Euclidean semigroup alone. This also fixes the sign of the real-time translation group. Let \((E_t)_{t \geqslant 0}\) be a strongly continuous semigroup of self-adjoint contractions on a complex Hilbert space, with \(E_0 = I\). Each \(E_t\) is positive because \(E_t = E_{t / 2}^2\).

We use continuous functional calculus for a bounded self-adjoint operator: continuous scalar functions on its spectrum act as a unital isometric star homomorphism, with the usual composition rule. This is the self-adjoint instance of the continuous calculus in [Wil24, Theorem 2.19]. We use the continuous calculus for positive roots and the bounded Borel calculus for the imaginary powers.

Proposition 6.1. There is a holomorphic operator-valued function \(E (z)\) on \(\text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} z > 0\) such that

\(\displaystyle \|E (z)\| \leqslant 1, \quad E (z + w) = E (z) E (w), \quad E (z)^{\ast} = E (\bar{z}), \quad E (t) = E_t \quad (t > 0) .\)

Each \(E_t\), \(t > 0\), is injective and has dense range. There is a strongly continuous unitary group \(V (t)\), \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), characterized by

\(\displaystyle V (t) E (z) = E (z) V (t) = E (z - it) \qquad (\text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} z > 0) .\) (6.1)

Every bounded operator commuting with all \(E_t\) commutes with \(E (z)\) and \(V (t)\). If \(E_t \Omega = \Omega\) for all \(t\), then \(E (z) \Omega = V (t) \Omega = \Omega\).

Proof. If \(E_t x = 0\) for some \(t > 0\), self-adjointness and the semigroup law give \(E_{t / 2} x = 0\). Iterate and use strong continuity at zero to get \(x = 0\). Since \(E_t\) is self-adjoint, its range is dense.

Write \(A = E_1\). By the bounded Borel functional calculus [RS80, Theorem VII.2] the spectral measure of \(A\) is supported on \([0, 1]\) and has zero projection at \(0\) by injectivity. Define \(E (z) = A^z\) and \(V (t) = A^{- it}\) using the real logarithm on \((0, 1]\). The values of the multipliers at zero are immaterial. The product, adjoint, norm and edge identities follow from the scalar multipliers. Dominated convergence for the vector spectral measures makes \(V (t)\) strongly continuous. On each compact subset of \(\text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} z > 0\), the bound on \(\lambda^{\delta} | \log \lambda |^2\) for any \(\delta > 0\) gives the uniform Taylor remainder needed for norm holomorphy.

For positive integers \(n\), \(E_{1 / n}\) is the positive \(n\)th root of \(A\) by the semigroup law and the continuous calculus. Thus \(E (p / n) = E_{p / n}\) for positive rational \(p / n\). Norm continuity of \(E (z)\) and strong continuity of the semigroup identify them on all positive real times. A bounded operator commuting with the semigroup commutes with \(A\) and hence its Borel calculus. If \(A \Omega = \Omega\), the scalar multipliers at \(1\) give both vacuum identities. Finally, the edge identity on the dense range of \(A\) determines \(V (t)\).\(\Box\)

Coordinate convention in Sections 6.1–6.11.
Section 1 writes points of \(\mathbb{R}^4\) as \(x = (\boldsymbol{x}, t)\), with time last. In Sections 6.1–6.11 we write instead \(a = (a^0, \mathbf{a})\) and \(X_i = (t_i, x_i)\) with time first, so that index \(0\) is the time coordinate, and matrices such as \(\eta = \text{diag} (1, - 1, - 1, - 1)\) are written in this order. The two descriptions are related by the linear change of coordinates \(C (t, \mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{x}, t)\) from time-first to time-last coordinates, with \(| \det C| = 1\) and \(C^{- 1} = C^{\textsf{T}}\). This is a change of realization, not a symmetry of either one. The Minkowski matrices satisfy \(\eta_{\text{last}} = C \eta_{\text{first}} C^{\textsf{T}}\); a Lorentz matrix \(\Lambda\) in time-first coordinates corresponds to \(C \Lambda C^{- 1}\); the time-last translation by \(a\) corresponds to the time-first translation by \(C^{- 1} a\); and the forward cone \(\{p^0 \geqslant |\mathbf{p}|\}\) corresponds to \(\{(\mathbf{p}, E) : |\mathbf{p}| \leqslant E\}\). Accordingly, the statements of these sections transfer to the time-last convention of Section 1 under the following substitutions: test functions are pulled back, \(f \mapsto f \circ C\); the Poincaré action is conjugated by \(C\); and supports, cones and spectral measures are pushed forward by \(C\). The pullback is a topological linear isomorphism of \(\mathcal{S} (\mathbb{R}^4)\) commuting with complex conjugation. It preserves spacelike separation of supports for the corresponding forms, and integrals acquire no Jacobian factor.

Suppose now that spatial translations \(U (x)\) form a strongly continuous unitary representation commuting with the heat semigroup and fixing \(\Omega_E\). The proposition defines a strongly continuous unitary representation of all real translations,

\(\displaystyle T_E (a^0, \mathbf{a}) = V (a^0) U (\mathbf{a}) .\)

The group law follows from commutation. Joint strong continuity follows by adding and subtracting \(V (a^0) U (\mathbf{b}) \xi\) and using the unitary norms. Moreover \(E (\varepsilon - it) \xi = V (t) E_{\varepsilon} \xi \to V (t) \xi\) as \(\varepsilon \downarrow 0\), which justifies the boundary notation \(E (- it)\) used in the heat-vector chapter. No operator-norm continuity at the boundary is required.

Lemma 6.2. The vectors fixed by every \(V (t)\) are exactly the vectors fixed by every \(E_s\). Consequently, if the selected heat ground space is \(\mathbb{C} \Omega_E\), the same is true of the subspace fixed by all real translations.

Proof. A heat-fixed vector satisfies \(A \xi = \xi\), so functional calculus and the definition of \(V\) give \(V (t) \xi = \xi\). Conversely suppose \(V (t) \xi = \xi\). The holomorphic vector function \(g (z) = E (z) \xi\) satisfies \(g (z - it) = E (z) V (t) \xi = g (z)\). Its derivative in the imaginary direction is zero; complex differentiability therefore makes its full derivative zero. It is constant on the connected right half-plane. Since \(E_s \xi \to \xi\) as \(s \downarrow 0\), this constant is \(\xi\). A vector invariant under all translations is, in particular, invariant under \(V (t)\), and hence belongs to the heat ground space. The vacuum itself is translation-invariant, which proves the last assertion.\(\Box\)

Combining this lemma with the isometric translation intertwiner in Proposition 6.24 proves uniqueness of the invariant vacuum in the reconstructed field Hilbert space: an invariant \(\xi\) maps to \(c \Omega_E =\mathcal{V} (c \Omega)\), and injectivity of the intertwiner \(\mathcal{V}\) gives \(\xi = c \Omega\).

We next construct the tempered boundary distribution used in the field reconstruction argument. The crucial bound is a power of the smallest imaginary-time gap. No bound uniform in the number of insertions is needed in this section.

6.2.1Centered insertions and continuation

Work in the selected Euclidean Hilbert space with heat contractions \(E_t\), spatial translations \(U (x)\), and vacuum \(\Omega_E\). In this section we use the complex heat extension \(E (z)\) on \(\text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} z > 0\): it is holomorphic in operator norm, satisfies the semigroup law and \(\|E (z)\| \leqslant 1\), agrees with \(E_t\) for positive real \(z\), and commutes with \(U (x)\). These properties were established in Proposition 6.1.

For a finite-level label \(v\), define \(B_p (v)\), \(p > 0\), to be the slab operator of width \(2 p\) with its single insertion at spatial position zero and time \(p\). All exterior matrix elements are precisely the corresponding Euclidean correlations. Multilinearity of those correlations, tested against the dense span of Euclidean words on both sides, proves linearity in \(v\). The same argument, using compatibility of the finite-level inclusions in every correlation, proves that these maps agree under inclusion. They therefore define one linear map \(B_p : E \to \mathcal{B} (\mathcal{H}_E)\) on the union of the field spaces. This use of all exterior words is what makes the union map well-defined.

The single-insertion case of the all-copy slab bound gives, for each fixed \(v \in E\), constants \(C_v > 0\) and an integer \(a_v \geqslant 0\) such that

\(\displaystyle \|B_p (v)\| \leqslant C_v p^{- a_v}, \qquad 0 < p < 1.\) (6.2)

Indeed, choose a finite level representing \(v\) and a bound for the norm of its chosen raw representative. The centered slab has both boundary clearances \(p\), so the estimate with one insertion applies with buffer \(p\). The padding identities for slab operators give

\(\displaystyle B_{p + u} (v) = E_u B_p (v) E_u \qquad (p > 0, u \geqslant 0) .\) (6.3)

Both statements retain the constants of the all-copy estimate; no uniform bound over all labels in the union is asserted.

For times \(z_i = t_i + iy_i\) with \(y_1 < \cdots < y_n\), choose \(0 < p < 1\) with \(4 p < y_j - y_i\) whenever \(i < j\). Write

\(\displaystyle R_p (z, x) = E (- iz - 2 p) U (x) .\)

Define

\(\displaystyle F_n (z, x ; v) = \langle \Omega_E, B_p (v_1) R_p (z_2 - z_1, x_2 - x_1) B_p (v_2) \cdots R_p (z_n - z_{n - 1}, x_n - x_{n - 1}) B_p (v_n) \Omega_E \rangle .\) (6.4)

For \(n = 1\) the intervening factors are absent; for \(n = 0\) the value is one. All arguments of \(E\) have positive real part.

Lemma 6.3. The value (6.4) is independent of the admissible width \(p\). It is multilinear in the labels, jointly continuous in \((z, x)\), and holomorphic in the complex time variables on the strictly ordered tube. At purely imaginary times it equals the Euclidean correlation of the insertions at \((x_i, y_i)\). For each fixed label tuple there are \(C \geqslant 0\) and \(m \in \mathbb{N}\) such that, for \(0 < k \leqslant 1\),

\(\displaystyle y_j - y_i \geqslant k (i < j) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad |F_n (t + iy, x ; v) | \leqslant Ck^{- m}\) (6.5)

uniformly in all real times \(t\) and spatial positions \(x\).

Proof. Compare widths \(p\) and \(q = p + u\). For \(u > 0\), the semigroup law and spatial commutation imply

\(\displaystyle R_p (z, x) = E_u R_q (z, x) E_u .\)

Substituting (6.3) for every \(B_q\) in the product at width \(q\) merges the two adjacent \(E_u\) factors around each \(R_q\) into \(R_p\). The exterior \(E_u\) factors disappear against the invariant vacuum. This proves equality; \(u = 0\) is immediate, and any two widths can be ordered. The admissibility inequalities are strict, so the same fixed width works on a neighborhood of every point of the tube. There the product is holomorphic in all time variables. It is jointly continuous in the spatial variables as a vector matrix element: strong continuity of \(U (x)\), local norm continuity of the other factors, and their local uniform bounds prove this successively from the rightmost vector. No operator-norm continuity of spatial translations is required. Multilinearity follows from that of each \(B_p\).

At imaginary times each intervening factor is the ordinary positive heat translation through the unused gap. Starting with the rightmost insertion, the slab insertion rule and the time and space translation rules show that the suffix kernel history after a preceding reference \((X, Y)\) is the Euclidean word vector with suffix positions \((x_i - X, y_i - Y - p)\). For the first suffix point its preceding heat duration is \(y_i - Y - 2 p > 0\): the centered slab places that insertion at height \(p\), and translation through this duration places it at \(y_i - Y - p\). The remaining suffix points follow the same translation, proving the induction invariant. Pairing the resulting kernel history with the initial centered insertion gives the complete word shifted by \((- x_1, - y_1 + p)\). Pairing with the vacuum and using translation invariance removes this last shift and gives the asserted Euclidean correlation.

All intervening factors have norm at most one. Thus the absolute value is at most \(\prod_i \|B_p (v_i)\|\), since \(\| \Omega_E \| = 1\). When the gaps are at least \(k\), choose \(p = k / 8\). Equation (6.2) gives

\(\displaystyle m = \sum_i a_{v_i}, \qquad C = \prod_i (C_{v_i} 8^{a_{v_i}}),\)

and proves (6.5). The empty product and sum also cover \(n = 0\).\(\Box\)

6.2.2Differentiating tested functions

Fix the arity and labels, and write \(X = (t_1, x_1, \ldots, t_n, x_n)\) for real positions. For strictly ordered heights \(y\) and a joint Schwartz test \(f\), put

\(\displaystyle \psi_y (f) = \int F_n (t + iy, x ; v) f (X) \hspace{0.17em} dX.\)

For a real height vector \(a\), let \(\partial_a\) denote differentiation of the real time coordinates in direction \(a\), and put \(D_a = - i \partial_a\) on tests.

Lemma 6.4. The integral defining \(\psi_y\) is absolutely convergent, depends continuously on \(y\) in the open ordered cone, and satisfies

\(\displaystyle \frac{d}{ds} \psi_{y + sa} (f) = \psi_{y + sa} (D_a f)\)

whenever the heights remain strictly ordered.

Proof. Near any strictly ordered \(y\), all gaps have a common positive lower bound. Equation (6.5) therefore supplies a bound uniform in \(X\) and in the nearby heights. Multiplication by the integrable function \(|f|\) proves integrability and, by dominated convergence, height continuity. For the derivative, restrict the holomorphic function to the complex line which changes all complex time coordinates by \(\zeta a\). A fixed disc about zero stays in the tube for every real configuration \(X\) and every height in a sufficiently small neighborhood. The same gap bound holds throughout that disc. Cauchy's formula therefore bounds the first complex derivative by a constant independent of \(X\). The Cauchy–Riemann equations give the pointwise height derivative \(i \partial_a F\). This uniform bound times \(|f|\) justifies differentiation under the integral.

To move the derivative onto the test, use smooth compactly supported cutoffs \(\chi_R (X) = \chi (X / R)\) equal to one on the radius-\(R\) ball and supported on the radius-\(2 R\) ball. Integration by parts for the real time direction gives

\(\displaystyle \int i (\partial_a F) f \chi_R = - \int iF (\partial_a f) \chi_R - \int iFf \partial_a \chi_R .\)

The last integral has magnitude at most a fixed constant times \(R^{- 1} \|f\|_{L^1}\), hence tends to zero. The other two integrals converge by the uniform bounds for \(F\) and \(\partial_a F\) and the integrability of \(f, \partial_a f\). This proves the identity, including its sign.\(\Box\)

6.2.3An exact Taylor formula at the edge

Let \(r = (0, 1, \ldots, n - 1)\) and \(D = D_r\). If \(y\) is only weakly ordered, then \(y + sr\) has all distinct-index gaps at least \(s\) for \(s > 0\); for arities zero and one the conditions on pairs are empty. Choose \(m, C\) from (6.5) for the fixed labels. Define

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \tilde{\psi}_y (f) & = \sum_{j = 0}^m \frac{(- 1)^j}{j!} \psi_{y + r} (D^j f) - \int_0^1 \frac{(- s)^m}{m!} \psi_{y + sr} (D^{m + 1} f) \hspace{0.17em} ds. \hspace{3cm} \text{(6.6)} \end{array}\)

This formula uses values strictly inside the tube even when some heights of \(y\) coincide.

Theorem 6.5. (Joint tempered boundary) Formula (6.6) defines a continuous linear functional of \(f\) on the joint Schwartz space for every weakly ordered \(y\); for each fixed \(f\) the scalar \(\tilde{\psi}_y (f)\) is continuous in the weakly ordered \(y\) (that is, \(y \mapsto \tilde{\psi}_y\) is weak-\(\ast\) continuous). It agrees with \(\psi_y (f)\) when \(y\) is strictly ordered. In particular

\(\displaystyle W_n (v) (f) := \tilde{\psi}_0 (f)\)

is a tempered distribution and

\(\displaystyle \psi_y (f) \longrightarrow W_n (v) (f) \quad \text{as } y \to 0 \text{ through all strictly ordered heights} .\)

The limit is multilinear in the labels. No restriction on the ratios of height gaps is imposed.

Proof. First take strictly ordered \(y\) and set

\(\displaystyle P (s) = \sum_{j = 0}^m \frac{(- s)^j}{j!} \psi_{y + sr} (D^j f) .\)

By Lemma 6.4, differentiating the finite sum cancels successive terms, leaving

\(\displaystyle P' (s) = \frac{(- s)^m}{m!} \psi_{y + sr} (D^{m + 1} f) .\)

Integrating from zero to one and using \(P (0) = \psi_y (f)\) proves the asserted identity with (6.6).

For weakly ordered \(y\) and \(0 < s \leqslant 1\), the gap estimate gives

\(\displaystyle \left| \frac{(- s)^m}{m!} \psi_{y + sr} (D^{m + 1} f) \right| \leqslant \frac{C}{m!} \|D^{m + 1} f\|_{L^1} .\)

Thus the remainder is absolutely integrable uniformly in \(y\). For each \(s > 0\) its integrand is continuous in \(y\) on the closed ordered cone, by Lemma 6.4. Dominated convergence proves continuity of the remainder, and the finite sum in (6.6) is continuous for the same reason. The formula also gives the uniform estimate

\(\displaystyle | \tilde{\psi}_y (f) | \leqslant C \sum_{j = 0}^m \frac{\|D^j f\|_{L^1}}{j!} + \frac{C}{m!} \|D^{m + 1} f\|_{L^1} .\)

These are continuous seminorms of \(f\) in the Schwartz topology. Explicitly, in real dimension \(d = 4 n\), each \(L^1\) norm is bounded by \(\int (1 + |X|)^{- d - 1} dX\) times the corresponding weighted supremum norm; \(D^j\) is a finite sum of ordinary derivatives. This proves continuity as a test functional.

The cone of strictly ordered heights accumulates at every weakly ordered height, by the path \(y + sr\). Hence its limits determine the functional uniquely. Linearity in \(f\) and multilinearity in every label follow by passing those identities for the interior integrals to the common boundary limit. This also shows that the answer is independent of the chosen power bound \(m, C\), although those bounds may depend on the label tuple. Finally, continuity of (6.6) at zero and its agreement with the interior integrals give the full asserted limit.\(\Box\)

The theorem constructs boundary values for joint tests on all real positions, not just factorized tests and not just pointwise limits. To obtain the heat vectors needed for positivity, one must also identify their Gram matrix with these same continued functions at mixed positive and negative heights. That identification is the next step.

6.3The first spectral support restriction

The joint boundary theorem also controls the Fourier support of the continued distributions. We use the radian convention

\(\displaystyle \hat{f} (X) = \int e^{- iX \cdot p} f (p) \hspace{0.17em} dp, \qquad \hat{W} (f) = W (\hat{f}) .\)

Thus the inverse test transform has factor \((2 \pi)^{- 4 n}\). The dot product here is the Euclidean pairing of the absolute coordinates, including each real time coordinate; no relative-coordinate reduction has been made. For a height tuple \(y\), write \(d (y) = (y_1 e_0, \ldots, y_n e_0)\).

Lemma 6.6. (Exponential testing identity) For strictly ordered \(y\) and every smooth compactly supported frequency test \(f\),

\(\displaystyle \hat{W}_n (v) (f) = \psi_y \left( \widehat{\hspace{0.17em} e^{d (y) \cdot p} f (p) \hspace{0.17em}} \right) .\) (6.7)

Proof. Set \(f_s (p) = e^{sd (y) \cdot p} f (p)\) and \(g_s = \hat{f}_s\). All \(f_s\) have the same compact support. Differentiating their explicit formula, including all spatial derivatives, shows that \(s \mapsto f_s\) is differentiable in the Schwartz topology with derivative \((d (y) \cdot p) f_s\). To see the topology directly, every weighted derivative is supported in that fixed compact set; Taylor's formula in \(s\), uniformly on it for each derivative, proves convergence in each Schwartz seminorm. Continuity of the Fourier transform then gives

\(\displaystyle g_s' = \widehat{(d (y) \cdot p) f_s} = - D_y g_s .\)

The sign follows by differentiating the kernel \(e^{- iX \cdot p}\) and using \(D_y = - i \partial_y\).

For \(s > 0\), differentiate \(\psi_{sy} (g_s)\). The varying-test step is legitimate with the uniform seminorm bound proved in Theorem 6.5: write a difference quotient as the sum of the change in the test at the new height and the change in height with the old test fixed. The first part converges to \(\psi_{sy} (g_s')\), because the test quotient converges in Schwartz space and the family of functionals has a common continuous seminorm bound. Lemma 6.4 handles the second part. Their sum is \(\psi_{sy} (- D_y g_s) + \psi_{sy} (D_y g_s) = 0\). The function is therefore constant on \((0, \infty)\). As \(s \downarrow 0\), \(g_s \to \hat{f}\) in Schwartz space, and the same uniform bound plus the joint boundary limit gives \(\psi_{sy} (g_s) \to W_n (v) (\hat{f})\). Comparing this with its value at \(s = 1\) proves the formula.\(\Box\)

Proposition 6.7. For every label tuple, the distribution \(\hat{W}_n (v)\) is supported in

\(\displaystyle H_n = \left\{ p : \sum_{i = 1}^n y_i p_i^0 \geqslant 0 \text{ for every } y_1 \leqslant \cdots \leqslant y_n \right\} .\)

Equivalently,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i = 1}^n p_i^0 = 0, \qquad \sum_{i = j}^n p_i^0 \geqslant 0 \quad (2 \leqslant j \leqslant n) .\)

Proof. Fix a weakly ordered \(y\) and a smooth compactly supported \(f\) whose support lies in the open set \(d (y) \cdot p < - 1\). Put \(y (t) = ty + r\), where \(r = (0, 1, \ldots, n - 1)\) and \(t \geqslant 0\). These heights are strictly ordered, with all gaps at least one. The functions

\(\displaystyle h_t (p) = e^{d (y (t)) \cdot p} f (p)\)

tend to zero in Schwartz space. Indeed, on the fixed compact support every derivative is a finite sum of a fixed smooth coefficient times a power of \(t\) times \(e^{td (y) \cdot p}\), and the latter is bounded by \(e^{- t}\). All polynomially weighted derivative suprema therefore tend to zero. Equation (6.7) and the gap bound at \(k = 1\) imply

\(\displaystyle | \hat{W}_n (v) (f) | = | \psi_{y (t)} (\hat{h}_t) | \leqslant C \| \hat{h}_t \|_{L^1} \longrightarrow 0.\)

Thus the distribution vanishes on that open set. If a point has \(d (y) \cdot p < 0\), rescale \(y\) by a positive constant so that its value is less than \(- 1\). A neighborhood of the point then has the same strict inequality. This proves support in \(H_n\).

To identify \(H_n\), the constant height tuples \(1\) and \(- 1\) give zero total time momentum. The weakly increasing step tuple equal to zero before \(j\) and one from \(j\) onward gives nonnegativity of each suffix sum. Conversely, for any weakly ordered \(y\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_i y_i p_i^0 = y_1 \sum_i p_i^0 + \sum_{j = 2}^n (y_j - y_{j - 1}) \sum_{i = j}^n p_i^0 \geqslant 0.\)

This proves the equivalence, with the empty and single-slot conventions included.\(\Box\)

Corollary 6.8. (Conditional Lorentz improvement) If the boundary distributions are covariant under the label representation \(\rho\) of the proper orthochronous Lorentz group of Subsection 6.5 (which preserves every finite-dimensional field level and is continuous there; only the covariance identity is used below), then the support of every \(\hat{W}_n (v)\) lies in

\(\displaystyle \left\{ p : \sum_i p_i = 0, \quad \sum_{i = j}^n p_i \in \bar{V}_+ (2 \leqslant j \leqslant n) \right\}, \qquad \bar{V}_+ = \{p : p^0 \geqslant |\mathbf{p}|\} .\)

Proof. Fourier transformation of the covariance identity changes momenta by the dual Lorentz action: this follows by the linear change of variables in the test transform, whose Jacobian has absolute value one. The transformed labels are again an allowed tuple, so Proposition 6.7 applies after every such dual Lorentz transformation. The inverse-transpose action ranges over the same group: for the Minkowski metric matrix \(\eta\), \(\Lambda^{- T} = \eta \Lambda \eta\), which preserves the future cone, and this operation is an involution. In particular every transformed suffix momentum has nonnegative time coordinate.

A real vector \(q\) has this property exactly when \(q \in \bar{V}_+\). One implication follows from preservation of the future cone. For the other, if \(q^0 < 0\) the identity transformation suffices. If \(0 \leqslant q^0 < |\mathbf{q}|\), choose a boost in the direction of \(\mathbf{q}\) with speed \(\beta\) strictly between \(q^0 / |\mathbf{q}|\) and one; the transformed time coordinate \((q^0 - \beta |\mathbf{q}|) / \sqrt{1 - \beta^2}\) is negative, a contradiction. This proves the suffix assertion. The total momentum has zero time coordinate in every frame. The identity frame makes its time coordinate zero, and the same boost argument forces its spatial part to vanish.\(\Box\)

The Lorentz covariance hypothesis in this last corollary is still to be proved from the infinitesimal Euclidean rotation identities. The conclusion here is support of the distributions in momentum variables; constructing the projection-valued momentum measure of the Hilbert-space representation is a separate step.

6.4Compatibility of the Lorentz action across field levels

The continuation argument uses one Lorentz action on the union of the field spaces. Constructing an action separately at every finite level would not be enough: the changes of basis used to compare with the free theory need not commute with the inclusions. The compatibility is instead proved from the Euclidean action and the infinitesimal boost relation.

Write \(E_d\) for the interacting finite-dimensional complex field space and \(\iota_{de} : E_d \to E_e\), \(d \leqslant e\), for its injective inclusions. Let \(E_d^0\) be the corresponding reference spaces. The following input isolates the finite-level comparison produced by the calibration argument.

Input 6.9. (Calibrated finite-level actions) For each \(d\) there is a complex-linear isomorphism \(Q_d : E_d^0 \to E_d\). The reference spaces carry continuous representations \(R_d^0\) of \(O (4)\) and \(T_d^0\) of the proper orthochronous Lorentz group. For each spatial coordinate \(a\), their generators satisfy

\(\displaystyle \left. \frac{d}{dt} T_d^0 (B_a (t)) \right|_{t = 0} = - iL_{d, a}^0,\)

where \(L_{d, a}^0\) is the generator of the Euclidean time–\(a\) plane rotation, with the conventions of Subsection 6.5. On spatial rotations the two reference actions agree. Define \(R_d (g) = Q_d R_d^0 (g) Q_d^{- 1}\) and let \(L_{d, a}\) be its indicated Euclidean generator. These actions satisfy

\(\displaystyle R_e (g) \iota_{de} = \iota_{de} R_d (g), \qquad L_{d, a} Q_d = Q_d L_{d, a}^0 .\)

There are antilinear involutions \(J_d, J_d^0\), compatible with inclusions, such that \(J_d Q_d = Q_d J_d^0\) and \(J_d^0\) commutes with \(T_d^0\). The inclusions form a directed system and the field union is its algebraic direct limit.

The comparison maps \(Q_d\) are the same maps that define \(R_d\); a fresh choice at the Lorentz stage would not justify the displayed generator identity. Production of these maps and the reference actions is separate from the compatibility proof below.

Proposition 6.10. Under Input 6.9, the finite-level representations

\(\displaystyle T_d (\Lambda) = Q_d T_d^0 (\Lambda) Q_d^{- 1}\)

commute with all inclusions. They therefore induce one representation \(T\) on the field union, continuous on each finite level. Its spatial action is the Euclidean one, its boost generator on \(E_d\) is \(- iL_{d, a}\), and it commutes with the induced involution \(J\).

Proof. Conjugation immediately gives the representation laws and continuity on each level. Differentiation at zero, using the fixed linear map \(Q_d\), gives the boost generator \(K_{d, a} = - iL_{d, a}\). Differentiating the Euclidean inclusion law along its plane rotation gives

\(\displaystyle L_{e, a} \iota_{de} = \iota_{de} L_{d, a}, \qquad K_{e, a} \iota_{de} = \iota_{de} K_{d, a} .\)

For \(v \in E_d\), the two curves

\(\displaystyle f (t) = T_e (B_a (t)) \iota_{de} v, \qquad g (t) = \iota_{de} T_d (B_a (t)) v\)

have the same initial value and solve \(h' = K_{e, a} h\). The derivative at arbitrary \(t\) follows from the group law by translating the parameter in the derivative at zero; for \(g\) use the generator intertwining just proved. Uniqueness of this constant-coefficient equation can be seen directly: the derivative of \(e^{- tK_{e, a}} (f (t) - g (t))\) is zero, and its value at zero is zero. Hence the inclusion commutes with every coordinate boost.

It commutes with each spatial rotation because the transported spatial action is \(R_d\) and the Euclidean inclusion law already holds. For fixed \(d, e\), the transformations commuting with \(\iota_{de}\) form a subgroup. Products follow by two substitutions. For inverses, if \(T_e (\Lambda) \iota_{de} = \iota_{de} T_d (\Lambda)\), multiply on the left and right by the respective inverse transformations. Spatial rotations and coordinate boosts generate the full proper orthochronous group, by the explicit reduction of \(\Lambda e_0\) in the proof of Theorem 6.13. Thus every transformation commutes with the inclusions. Notice that this argument has not asserted any compatibility of the maps \(Q_d\) themselves.

Define the action on a union element represented by \(v \in E_d\) to be represented by \(T_d (\Lambda) v\). If another representative lies in \(E_e\), pass to a common higher level; the inclusion compatibility proves the same resulting class. This proves well-definedness. Linearity and the group laws hold after putting finitely many representatives in a common level, and the inverse action is that of \(\Lambda^{- 1}\). The asserted spatial and derivative identities follow in the same way. Finally

\(\displaystyle J_d T_d (\Lambda) Q_d v = Q_d J_d^0 T_d^0 (\Lambda) v = Q_d T_d^0 (\Lambda) J_d^0 v = T_d (\Lambda) J_d Q_d v.\)

Surjectivity of \(Q_d\) and compatibility of the involutions prove commutation with \(J\) on the union.\(\Box\)

6.5From Euclidean rotations to Lorentz covariance

This section proves covariance of the boundary distributions from the Euclidean rotation identities, retaining the same field labels. The finite-dimensional Lorentz action on those labels must first be available. We state its required compatibility explicitly rather than identify it with an arbitrary representation having the same dimension.

On each finite level let \(R\) be the Euclidean rotation representation, and write \(L_a\) for its infinitesimal generator in the plane consisting of time and spatial coordinate \(a\). Our convention for the coordinate generator is

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R}_a = \sum_i (x_i^a \partial_{y_i} - y_i \partial_{x_i^a}) .\)

Assume \(R\) acts compatibly under the inclusions of field levels, and the smooth Euclidean correlation \(S (v ; X)\) obeys simultaneous rotation covariance. Differentiating it gives

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R}_a S (v ; X) = - \sum_i S (v_1, \ldots, L_a v_i, \ldots, v_n ; X) .\) (6.8)

Indeed, covariance says \(S (v ; R_t X) = S (R_{- t} v ; X)\); the ordinary chain rule on positions and the finite multilinear expansion on labels give the displayed formula. This is applied only at distinct Euclidean positions.

Suppose also that the proper orthochronous Lorentz group has a representation \(\rho\) on the field union preserving every finite level, continuous there, compatible with the inclusions, and agreeing with \(R\) on spatial rotations. For the coordinate boost \(B_a (s)\), with coordinate infinitesimal generator \(x^a \partial_t + t \partial_{x^a}\), require

\(\displaystyle \left. \frac{d}{ds} \rho (B_a (s)) v \right|_{s = 0} = - iL_a v.\) (6.9)

Proposition 6.10 supplies this action from the calibrated finite-level actions. Their construction remains an explicit earlier input. Set \(K_a = - iL_a\).

Proposition 6.11. (The tested boost identity) For strictly ordered \(y\) and any joint Schwartz test \(f\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_i \psi_y (v_1, \ldots, K_a v_i, \ldots, v_n ; f) = \psi_y \left( v ; \sum_i [x_i^a \partial_{t_i} + (t_i + iy_i) \partial_{x_i^a}] f \right) .\) (6.10)

Consequently the boundary distributions satisfy

\(\displaystyle \sum_i W_n (v_1, \ldots, K_a v_i, \ldots, v_n) (f) = W_n (v) (\mathcal{B}_a f), \qquad \mathcal{B}_a = \sum_i (x_i^a \partial_{t_i} + t_i \partial_{x_i^a}) .\) (6.11)

Proof. First suppose \(f\) has compact support \(C\). For a complex parameter \(c\) put \(z_i (c) = iy_i + ct_i\) and

\(\displaystyle F_c (v ; X) = F_n (z (c), x ; v), \qquad T_c f = \sum_i [x_i^a \partial_{t_i} + cz_i (c) \partial_{x_i^a}] f.\)

Let \(D\) consist of parameters for which \(z (c)\) has strictly ordered imaginary parts for every \(X \in C\). This is an open convex set containing the real axis: its constraints are affine strict inequalities in \(\text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} c\), and compactness of \(C\) makes their margins uniform near each admissible parameter. Consider the integral

\(\displaystyle H (c) = \int_C \left[ F_c (v ; X) T_c f (X) - c \sum_i F_c (v_1, \ldots, K_a v_i, \ldots, v_n ; X) f (X) \right] dX.\)

It is holomorphic on \(D\). In fact the integrand is jointly continuous on \(D \times C\) and holomorphic in \(c\) for every \(X\); on each compact subdisc, Cauchy's formula gives uniformly bounded derivatives, so differentiation under this finite-volume integral is justified.

At \(c = i \sigma\), put \(Y_i = y_i + \sigma t_i\). All these heights are strictly ordered on a neighborhood of \(C\), and \(F_{i \sigma} (v ; X) = S (v ; (x, Y))\). The real vector field

\(\displaystyle A_{\sigma} = \sum_i [x_i^a \partial_{t_i} - \sigma (y_i + \sigma t_i) \partial_{x_i^a}]\)

is divergence free. Under the map \((t, x) \mapsto (Y, x)\) it becomes \(\sigma \mathcal{R}_a\). Thus (6.8) gives

\(\displaystyle A_{\sigma} F_{i \sigma} (v ; X) = - \sigma \sum_i S (v_1, \ldots, L_a v_i, \ldots, v_n ; (x, Y)) = - i \sigma \sum_i F_{i \sigma} (v_1, \ldots, K_a v_i, \ldots, v_n ; X) .\)

Also \(T_{i \sigma} f = A_{\sigma} f\). The integrand of \(H (i \sigma)\) is therefore \(A_{\sigma} (F_{i \sigma} (v ; X) f (X))\), whose integral is zero. The product is smooth near \(C\) and zero off \(C\), so its extension by zero is a smooth compactly supported function; no regularity of the continued function at positions outside that neighborhood is being assumed. The identity theorem on the connected set \(D\) now gives \(H (1) = 0\), which is (6.10) for compactly supported tests.

To pass to Schwartz tests, choose a smooth cutoff \(\chi (X / R)\) equal to one near zero. Then \(\chi (X / R) f \to f\) in every Schwartz seminorm: on the outside region use one extra polynomial decay weight, and for each positive-order derivative of the cutoff use its factor \(R^{- 1}\) or a higher inverse power and the same tail estimate. At fixed strictly ordered \(y\), every \(\psi_y\) is continuous on Schwartz space, and all coordinate multiplications and derivatives in (6.10) are continuous there. The compact-test identity consequently extends to \(f\).

Finally take the full ordered-height limit \(y \to 0\). The test on the right is \(\mathcal{B}_a f + \sum_i iy_i \partial_{x_i^a} f\). Each fixed-test pairing \(\psi_y (v ; \partial_{x_i^a} f)\) has a finite boundary limit, so multiplication by \(y_i\) makes the correction tend to zero. All other terms have their specified joint boundary limits, proving (6.11).\(\Box\)

Lemma 6.12. (Affine pullback of tests) The action \(P_g f (X) = f (g^{- 1} X)\) of the Poincaré group, acting on all positions at once, is continuous in the Schwartz topology. More generally, for a fixed Schwartz test \(f\) on the joint position space, the map \(A \mapsto f \circ A^{- 1}\) is continuous from the group of invertible affine maps of that space (an open subset of a finite-dimensional real vector space) into the Schwartz space; hence so is \(g \mapsto f \circ A_g^{- 1}\) for every continuous map \(g \mapsto A_g\) into that group. For coordinate boosts,

\(\displaystyle \left. \frac{d}{ds} P_{B_a (s)} f \right|_{s = 0} = -\mathcal{B}_a f\)

in that topology.

Proof. We prove continuity in the general form; the Poincaré action is the case \(A_g = g\) acting diagonally on all positions. On a compact neighborhood of a fixed invertible affine map, its linear part and inverse have uniformly bounded norms and its translation is bounded. Hence \(1 + |X|\) and \(1 + |g^{- 1} X|\) are comparable uniformly there. The chain rule expresses every derivative of \(f \circ g^{- 1}\) as a finite sum of derivatives of \(f\) at \(g^{- 1} X\) times products of matrix entries. Each weighted supremum seminorm is therefore bounded by finitely many Schwartz seminorms of \(f\), uniformly on that neighborhood.

For continuity, split the supremum into a fixed ball and its complement. On the ball, uniform continuity of each derivative and continuous variation of the matrix coefficients give convergence. On the complement, the comparison of weights and one extra decay seminorm make the tail uniformly small. This proves continuity in every seminorm. For the boost derivative, the pointwise chain rule at zero gives \(-\mathcal{B}_a f\). To justify the same derivative in Schwartz space, use Taylor's formula along the smooth curve of invertible matrices \(B_a (- s)\). Its second derivative acts on \(f\) as a finite sum of first and second derivatives composed with \(B_a (- s)\), multiplied by coefficients polynomial in \(X\) of degree at most two. The preceding weight comparison bounds every seminorm of this second derivative uniformly for \(s\) in a small compact interval. The integral Taylor remainder is therefore \(O (s^2)\) in each seminorm, proving the stated derivative.\(\Box\)

Theorem 6.13. Under the Euclidean covariance and compatible label-action hypotheses above, the boundary distributions satisfy

\(\displaystyle W_n (\rho (\Lambda) v_1, \ldots, \rho (\Lambda) v_n) (P_{\Lambda} f) = W_n (v) (f)\)

for every proper orthochronous Lorentz transformation \(\Lambda\). They are also invariant under common spacetime translations.

Proof. For a fixed coordinate boost set

\(\displaystyle A (s) = W_n (\rho (B_a (s)) v_1, \ldots, \rho (B_a (s)) v_n) (P_{B_a (s)} f) .\)

Put the finitely many labels in one common finite level. Expanding them in a finite basis expresses \(A (s)\) as a finite sum of smooth scalar coefficients times fixed-label distributions applied to the moving test. The label coefficients are smooth along the boost: its group law and the derivative at zero give the linear differential equation \(\rho (B_a (s))' = \rho (B_a (s)) K_a\) on each finite level. Thus Lemma 6.12 and (6.9) justify the ordinary product rule at zero. Its label terms are the left side of (6.11), and its test term is the negative of the right side. Hence \(A' (0) = 0\). The representation laws for labels and pullbacks give the same calculation at any \(s\), using the labels and test already moved by \(B_a (s)\). Therefore \(A' (s) = 0\) everywhere and \(A (s) = A (0)\).

Spatial rotations and common spatial translations are inherited by the strict-time continuation from their Euclidean identities. To check this without an assumption of holomorphy in spatial coordinates, fix all real positions, compare the two scalar continued expressions, and replace each time by \(iy_i + ct_i\). Both sides are holomorphic on an open strip about the real axis and agree for \(c = i \sigma\) near zero by Euclidean covariance. The identity theorem gives equality at \(c = 1\). For a common real-time translation \(b\), compare times \(iy_i + c (t_i + b)\) on one side with \(iy_i + ct_i\) on the other. Their gaps agree, and at \(c = i \sigma\) they differ by the common Euclidean time translation \(\sigma b\). The same proof applies. Changing variables in the joint test integrals, with unit absolute Jacobian, and taking boundary limits gives spatial-rotation covariance and translation invariance of \(W\).

Finally these spatial rotations and coordinate boosts generate the proper orthochronous Lorentz group. Here is a direct verification. Given \(\Lambda\), its image \(q = \Lambda e_0\) is future timelike with Minkowski norm squared one. A boost in coordinate \(a\) with rapidity \(- \text{artanh} (q^a / q^0)\) makes that spatial component zero; \(|q^a | < q^0\) ensures the parameter exists. It leaves the other spatial components unchanged. Three such boosts produce a vector with zero spatial part and positive time component, necessarily \(e_0\). Their product \(B\) therefore makes \(B \Lambda\) fix \(e_0\). Preservation of the Minkowski form then shows that \(B \Lambda\) preserves its spatial orthogonal complement and is orthogonal on it; determinant one makes its spatial part a rotation. Thus \(\Lambda\) is a product of coordinate boosts and a spatial rotation. Covariance is closed under products and inverses by the representation laws, proving the assertion for every \(\Lambda\).\(\Box\)

Together with Corollary 6.8, this gives the full forward suffix-cone support of the boundary distributions, conditional only on the specified compatible Lorentz action and the previously stated Euclidean construction inputs. It does not yet prove spacelike commutativity.

6.6Continuation to the full spacetime tube

Hall and Wightman [HW57, parts I–III] use tube analyticity and Lorentz invariance to continue scalar vacuum correlations. Our starting point is the continuation in the time variables of the Euclidean tables. The Fourier–Laplace construction below supplies the full tube for the present field multiplets and controls its tested boundary limits.

The ordered complex-time functions constructed earlier are holomorphic in the time variables. Locality needs a larger domain: the spatial variables must also become complex. We construct this continuation from the spectral support of the boundary distributions. A smooth cutoff near that support makes the Fourier–Laplace expression an actual Schwartz test. Keeping the cutoff also permits limits in which some imaginary gaps lie on the boundary of the forward cone.

Fix \(n\) and set \(D = 4 n\). All coordinates in this section are absolute coordinates in \(\mathbb{R}^D\), rather than relative coordinates. Write

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} K_n & = \left\{ p : \sum_i p_i = 0, \quad \sum_{i = j}^n p_i \in \bar{V}_+ (2 \leqslant j \leqslant n) \right\},\\ C_n & = \{y : y_{i + 1} - y_i \in \bar{V}_+ (1 \leqslant i < n)\},\\ C_n^{\circ} & = \{y : y_{i + 1} - y_i \in V_+ (1 \leqslant i < n)\}, \qquad V_+ = \{q : q^0 > |\mathbf{q}|\} . \end{array}\)

The superscript on \(C_n^{\circ}\) denotes the strict-gap set, which is open in the full space. For \(n = 0\) or \(1\) the gap conditions are vacuous. The tube is \(\mathcal{T}_n =\mathbb{R}^D + iC_n^{\circ}\). By Corollary 6.8 and Theorem 6.13, the distribution \(\hat{W}_n (v)\) has support in \(K_n\), under the hypotheses stated there.

Lemma 6.14. (Dual positivity and a strict margin) If \(p \in K_n\) and \(y \in C_n\), then \(p \cdot y \geqslant 0\). For each \(y \in C_n^{\circ}\) there are \(c > 0\) and a neighborhood \(N\) of \(y\) such that

\(\displaystyle p \cdot u \geqslant c |p| \qquad (p \in K_n, u \in N) .\)

Proof. Put \(q_j = \sum_{i = j}^n p_i\). The zero total momentum and telescoping give

\(\displaystyle p \cdot y = \sum_{j = 2}^n q_j \cdot (y_j - y_{j - 1}) .\)

The Euclidean pairing of two forward-cone vectors is nonnegative: \(a \cdot b = a^0 b^0 +\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} \geqslant a^0 b^0 - |\mathbf{a}| |\mathbf{b}| \geqslant 0\). This proves the first assertion. Choose \(r > 0\) with \(B (y, r) \subseteq C_n^{\circ}\), and put \(c = r / 2\). For \(u \in B (y, r / 2)\) and \(p \ne 0\), the point \(u - cp / |p|\) belongs to \(B (y, r)\) and hence to \(C_n\). Applying the first assertion to this point gives \(p \cdot u - c |p| \geqslant 0\). The case \(p = 0\) is immediate. This argument also covers the vacuous gap cases, where \(K_n = \{0\}\).\(\Box\)

6.6.1A smooth cutoff and closed imaginary shifts

Let \(K_n^{(a)} = \{p : \text{dist} (p, K_n) < a\}\). Choose a nonnegative smooth bump \(\rho\), supported in \(B (0, 1 / 2)\), with integral one, and define

\(\displaystyle \chi = \rho \ast \boldsymbol{1}_{K_n^{(2)}} .\)

Then \(0 \leqslant \chi \leqslant 1\), \(\chi = 1\) on \(K_n^{(1)}\), and \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \chi \subseteq K_n^{(3)}\), with support understood as closed support. Indeed, for \(p \in K_n^{(1)}\) every point \(p - q\) with \(q \in \text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \rho\) has distance less than \(3 / 2\) from \(K_n\); the convolution therefore integrates the constant one. Nonzero values require distance less than \(5 / 2\) from \(K_n\), and their closure still has distance at most \(5 / 2 < 3\). Every derivative is globally bounded, since

\(\displaystyle \partial^{\alpha} \chi = (\partial^{\alpha} \rho) \ast \boldsymbol{1}_{K_n^{(2)}}, \qquad \| \partial^{\alpha} \chi \|_{\infty} \leqslant \| \partial^{\alpha} \rho \|_1 .\)

Differentiating the compactly supported convolution proves smoothness: difference quotients of the smooth factor converge in \(L^1\), so their convolutions with the bounded indicator converge uniformly. In dimension zero simply take \(\chi = 1\).

For \(y \in C_n\) set \(a_y (p) = e^{- y \cdot p} \chi (p)\). This is a smooth multiplier of Schwartz space. More precisely, for every \(R\) and multi-index \(\alpha\) there is a finite constant \(A_{R, \alpha}\) such that

\(\displaystyle \sup_p | \partial^{\alpha} a_y (p) | \leqslant A_{R, \alpha} \qquad (y \in C_n, |y| \leqslant R) .\) (6.12)

On the closed support of \(\chi\) choose \(q \in K_n\) with \(|p - q| < 3\). Dual positivity gives \(- y \cdot p \leqslant 3 |y|\). Each derivative of the exponential adds a product of components of \(y\), while derivatives of \(\chi\) are bounded. Leibniz's rule proves (6.12); outside that support all derivatives of \(a_y\) vanish.

For each \(f \in \mathscr{S}\), the map \(y \mapsto a_y f\) is continuous from \(C_n\) to \(\mathscr{S}\). On a compact frequency ball this follows uniformly for every derivative from the explicit smooth formula. Outside a large ball, the uniform derivative bounds and Leibniz's rule bound a seminorm of weight \(k\) by a constant times \((1 + |p|)^{- 1}\) times finitely many seminorms of \(f\) of weight \(k + 1\). The tail is therefore uniformly small. Combining the compact and tail estimates proves convergence in every Schwartz seminorm, including at closed-cone boundary shifts.

For a fixed label tuple abbreviate \(w = \hat{W}_n (v)\) and define a tempered distribution \(W_y\) by

\(\displaystyle W_y (f) = w (a_y \check{f}), \qquad \check{f} (p) = (2 \pi)^{- D} \int e^{ip \cdot x} f (x) \hspace{0.17em} dx.\) (6.13)

The preceding continuity shows that \(W_y (f)\) is continuous in \(y \in C_n\). Moreover \(W_0 = W_n (v)\). To see this, multiplication by \(\chi\) leaves \(w\) unchanged because \(\chi = 1\) on a neighborhood of its support. The support assertion applies to Schwartz tests as well as compact tests: multiply a test vanishing near the support by compact cutoffs, apply distributional support, and pass to the Schwartz limit. Fourier inversion now gives \(w (\check{f}) = W_n (v) (f)\).

6.6.2The holomorphic Fourier–Laplace expression

For \(z = x + iy \in \mathcal{T}_n\) define

\(\displaystyle L_z (p) = e^{ip \cdot z} \chi (p), \qquad H_n (v ; z) = (2 \pi)^{- D} w (L_z) .\)

Here \(p \cdot z\) is the complex bilinear extension of the Euclidean pairing, with no conjugation. The test \(L_z\) is in \(\mathscr{S}\). In fact, the margin lemma at \(y\) and the distance-three support property give, on \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \chi\),

\(\displaystyle - y \cdot p \leqslant 3 (|y| + c) - c |p| .\)

To obtain this, choose \(q \in K_n\) with \(|p - q| < 3\), use \(y \cdot q \geqslant c |q|\), and \(|q| \geqslant |p| - 3\). Frequency derivatives of \(L_z\) only introduce bounded derivatives of \(\chi\) and powers of the fixed vector \(z\). They consequently have exponential decay. The same estimates hold in a sufficiently small neighborhood of \(z\), with a common positive decay rate.

Proposition 6.15. The map \(z \mapsto L_z\) is complex differentiable into Schwartz space, with derivative \(h \mapsto i (p \cdot h) L_z (p)\). Consequently \(H_n (v ; z)\) is holomorphic jointly in all \(D\) complex coordinates. For each fixed \(y \in C_n^{\circ}\) it grows at most polynomially in \(x\), and for every \(f \in \mathscr{S}\),

\(\displaystyle W_y (f) = \int H_n (v ; x + iy) f (x) \hspace{0.17em} dx,\) (6.14)

with an absolutely convergent integral.

Proof. The difference from the proposed derivative is

\(\displaystyle L_{z + h} - L_z - i (p \cdot h) L_z = L_z (e^{ip \cdot h} - 1 - ip \cdot h) .\)

For \(|h| \leqslant 1\), every fixed-order frequency derivative of the factor in parentheses has absolute value at most \(C_{\alpha} |h|^2 (1 + |p|)^2 e^{|h| |p|}\). At derivative order zero use \(|e^u - 1 - u| \leqslant |u|^2 e^{|u|}\); at order one use \(|e^u - 1| \leqslant |u| e^{|u|}\); at higher orders differentiate the exponential, which contributes at least two factors of \(h\). These inequalities follow directly from its power series. Choose \(|h| < c / 2\). Leibniz's rule and the exponential decay of \(L_z\) show that the remainder is \(O (|h|^2)\) in each Schwartz seminorm. The derivative map is continuous from the finite-dimensional parameter space into \(\mathscr{S}\), since multiplication by each frequency coordinate is continuous. Composition with the continuous linear functional \(w\) proves complex differentiability of \(H_n\); in finite complex dimension this gives holomorphy.

For fixed \(y\), put \(g (p) = a_y (p) = L_{iy} (p)\). Modulation becomes translation under Fourier transformation:

\(\displaystyle \widehat{L_{x + iy}} (u) = \hat{g} (u - x) .\)

For \(s_{k, j} (h) = \sup_u |u|^k \|D^j h (u)\|\), the triangle inequality gives the explicit translation bound

\(\displaystyle s_{k, j} (h (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - x)) \leqslant 2^k (s_{k, j} (h) + |x|^k s_{0, j} (h)) .\)

A continuous linear functional on Schwartz space is bounded by finitely many such seminorms. Fourier inversion and the displayed bound therefore bound \(|w (L_{x + iy}) |\) by a polynomial in \(|x|\). Translation is continuous in Schwartz space: its derivatives converge uniformly on compact sets, and the same bound with one extra decay weight controls the tails. Thus the integrand in (6.14) is continuous and absolutely integrable for every Schwartz \(f\).

First let \(f\) be compactly supported. Fourier inversion, pointwise in \(p\), gives

\(\displaystyle a_y (p) \check{f} (p) = (2 \pi)^{- D} \int f (x) L_{x + iy} (p) \hspace{0.17em} dx.\)

This integral can be passed through \(w\) in the Schwartz topology. For an explicit justification take Riemann sums in a box containing the support of \(f\). The integrand is continuous into Schwartz space and has compact parameter support. For each seminorm the error is bounded by the box volume times its uniform modulus of continuity in that seminorm. The sums therefore converge in every seminorm to the displayed function; continuity of \(w\) gives (6.14). For general \(f\), use \(f_R = \kappa (x / R) f\) with \(0 \leqslant \kappa \leqslant 1\) and \(\kappa = 1\) near zero. Then \(f_R \to f\) in Schwartz space, while polynomial growth gives an integrable majorant for \(|H_n (v ; x + iy) f_R (x) |\) independent of \(R\). Continuity on the left and dominated convergence on the right finish the proof.\(\Box\)

Proposition 6.16. (Identification with the ordered-time function) For strictly increasing scalar heights \(y_1 < \cdots < y_n\),

\(\displaystyle H_n (v ; X + id (y)) = F_n (t + iy, x ; v), \qquad X = ((t_1, x_1), \ldots, (t_n, x_n)) .\)

For any \(Y \in C_n\), the integrals of \(H_n (v ; X + iY') f (X)\) converge to \(W_Y (f)\) as \(Y' \to Y\) through \(C_n^{\circ}\).

Proof. At fixed strict scalar heights, \(\psi_y\) is a tempered distribution by the uniform real-position bound on \(F_n\). Let \(h\) be a compactly supported smooth frequency test. Since \(\chi = 1\) near \(K_n\) and \(w\) is supported there, the exponential testing identity gives

\(\displaystyle w (a_{d (y)} h) = w (e^{- d (y) \cdot p} h) = \psi_y (\hat{h}) .\)

The middle expression is used only for compactly supported \(h\), since multiplication by \(e^{- d (y) \cdot p}\) does not preserve \(\mathscr{S}\). The two outer expressions, \(h \mapsto w (a_{d (y)} h)\) and \(h \mapsto \psi_y (\hat{h})\), are continuous linear functionals on Schwartz space. Compact-test density therefore extends the equality of the outer expressions to every \(h \in \mathscr{S}\). Apply inverse Fourier transformation: \(W_{d (y)} = \psi_y\). Equation (6.14) now identifies the integrals of the two proposed continuous functions against every Schwartz test. They agree pointwise: a nonzero difference would have, after a constant phase change, strictly positive real part on a ball and would be detected by a nonnegative smooth bump there. The final boundary statement follows from (6.14) and continuity of \(W_{Y'} (f)\) on \(C_n\).\(\Box\)

Proposition 6.17. (Lorentz covariance in the tube) For every proper orthochronous Lorentz matrix \(\Lambda\),

\(\displaystyle H_n (v ; z) = H_n (\rho (\Lambda) v ; \Lambda z) \qquad (z \in \mathcal{T}_n) .\)

The action is simultaneous on all slots.

Proof. The matrix \(\Lambda\) preserves strict forward gaps, so \(\Lambda z\) is in the tube. Its dual \(A = \Lambda^{- T}\) preserves \(K_n\), because it is again proper orthochronous and preserves total momentum and suffix cones. The Fourier form of Theorem 6.13, obtained by the change of variables of determinant one, is

\(\displaystyle \hat{W}_n (\rho (\Lambda) v) (h \circ A^{- 1}) = \hat{W}_n (v) (h) .\)

The two Schwartz functions \(L_z\) and \(L_{\Lambda z} \circ A\) agree near \(K_n\). Indeed their exponential factors agree because \((Ap) \cdot (\Lambda z) = p \cdot z\). At each \(p \in K_n\), both cutoffs are one on the neighborhood \(B (p, 1) \cap A^{- 1} B (Ap, 1)\). Their difference therefore vanishes near the support of \(w\). Apply the Fourier covariance identity to \(h = L_{\Lambda z} \circ A\), discard this vanishing difference, and multiply by \((2 \pi)^{- D}\). This is exactly the claimed equality.\(\Box\)

6.7The heat-vector bridge

We now supply the independent-height Gram limit in Input 6.20 from the joint boundary theorem. Throughout this section the complex heat and slab properties used in Subsection 6.2 are retained. The involution \(J\) is the one induced on the field union by Euclidean time reflection and conjugation. This identification is essential in the Gram formula below.

For \(0 < y_1 < \cdots < y_n\), real positions \(X_i = (t_i, x_i)\), and an admissible width \(p\) satisfying \(4 p < y_1\) and \(4 p < y_j - y_i\) for \(i < j\), define

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{l} Q_{p, y} (X ; v) = E (- i (t_1 + iy_1) - p) U (x_1) B_p (v_1)\\ \cdot R_p (t_2 - t_1 + i (y_2 - y_1), x_2 - x_1) B_p (v_2) \cdots R_p (t_n - t_{n - 1} + i (y_n - y_{n - 1}), x_n - x_{n - 1}) B_p (v_n) \Omega_E . \hspace{3cm} \text{(6.15)} \end{array}\)

The empty tuple gives \(\Omega_E\). The subtraction in the first complex heat time is \(p\), whereas that between two insertions is \(2 p\). Each complex heat factor is a contraction, so

\(\displaystyle \|Q_{p, y} (X ; v)\| \leqslant \prod_i \|B_p (v_i)\| .\) (6.16)

For fixed \(p, y, v\) this vector is continuous in \(X\), by the same successive strong-continuity argument used for (6.4). Consequently \(f (X) Q_{p, y} (X ; v)\) is Bochner integrable for every joint Schwartz test \(f\). To justify strong measurability without a global separability assumption on the Hilbert space, its continuous image of the separable position space is separable. The bound (6.16) then gives the integrable majorant \(|f (X) | \prod_i \|B_p (v_i)\|\).

Lemma 6.18. (Mixed Gram identity) For positive strictly ordered height tuples \(y, z\), separately admissible widths \(p, q\), positions \(X, Y\) and labels \(v, u\), put

\(\displaystyle \hat{y} = (- y_n, \ldots, - y_1, z_1, \ldots, z_m), \quad \hat{X} = (X_n, \ldots, X_1, Y_1, \ldots, Y_m), \quad \hat{v} = (Jv_n, \ldots, Jv_1, u_1, \ldots, u_m) .\)

Then

\(\displaystyle \langle Q_{p, y} (X ; v), Q_{q, z} (Y ; u) \rangle = F_{n + m} (\hat{t} + i \hat{y}, \hat{x} ; \hat{v}) .\) (6.17)

Here \(\hat{t}, \hat{x}\) are the real time and spatial parts of \(\hat{X}\). The real positions in the first tuple are reversed in order; their real time coordinates are not negated.

Proof. When all real time coordinates are zero, the vector in (6.15) is the Euclidean word vector with insertions \((x_i, y_i)\) and labels \(v_i\). This follows directly by induction from the right: a centered insertion adds its point at height \(p\), the preceding positive heat interval supplies the missing distance, and the spatial translation changes the common reference point. More explicitly, with a preceding reference \((X_0, Y_0)\) and preceding half-width \(q_0\), the partial vector has points \((x_i - X_0, y_i - Y_0 - q_0)\). The recursion uses first heat time \(y_1 - Y_0 - q_0 - p\); after insertion it produces the same formula with the first point included. Taking \(q_0 = 0\) and \((X_0, Y_0) = 0\) gives the stated word. Pairing the two Euclidean words reflects the first tuple in time and applies \(J\) to its labels. Permutation symmetry allows its reversed order, and the imaginary-time identity of Lemma 6.3 proves (6.17) in this case. This argument does not require equal widths \(p, q\).

For arbitrary fixed real positions, introduce one complex parameter \(c\). In the first vector replace its times by \(iy_i + ct_i\), and in the second by \(iz_j + cs_j\). Call the resulting holomorphic vector functions \(A (c)\) and \(B (c)\). Since all clearance inequalities are strict and there are only finitely many of them, there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that these vectors are well-defined on \(| \text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} c| < \delta\). The same strip works for \(A (\bar{c})\), after decreasing \(\delta\) if necessary. The scalar function

\(\displaystyle c \longmapsto \langle A (\bar{c}), B (c) \rangle\)

is holomorphic: expand the difference quotient and use antilinearity in the first argument, which cancels the conjugation of its increment. Its proposed right side is \(F\) at times \(i \hat{y} + c \hat{t}\), with the fixed combined spatial tuple. This too is holomorphic on a sufficiently small horizontal strip; its heights are the concatenation of the reversed negatives of \(y_i - (\text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} c) t_i\) and the tuple \(z_j + (\text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} c) s_j\), and remain strictly ordered there.

At \(c = i \tau\) near zero, both vectors have purely imaginary times, with these modified positive height tuples. The already proved Euclidean identity therefore equates the two scalar functions. The identity theorem on the connected strip extends equality to \(c = 1\). This is the desired identity with arbitrary real positions. Empty tuples cause no difficulty: their vector is the fixed vacuum and the same argument applies.\(\Box\)

For a word \(b = ((v_1, f_1), \ldots, (v_n, f_n))\), choose

\(\displaystyle y (t) = (t, 2 t, \ldots, nt), \qquad p (t) = \tfrac{1}{8} \min (t, 1), \quad t > 0,\)

and define

\(\displaystyle h_t (b) = \int \left( \prod_i f_i (X_i) \right) Q_{p (t), y (t)} (X ; v) \hspace{0.17em} dX.\)

The clearance conditions hold for every \(t > 0\), and the fixed-height bound above proves integrability. No uniform pointwise bound as \(t \downarrow 0\) is asserted or needed.

Proposition 6.19. These vectors satisfy (6.18) for the boundary functional of Theorem 6.5, and \(h_t (1) = \Omega_E\). Moreover, let \(T_E (a) = E (- ia^0) U (\mathbf{a})\) be the joint real-time translation representation on \(\mathcal{H}_E\), and for \(a \in \mathbb{R}^4\) let \(\tau_a\) act on words letterwise by replacing each test \(f\) by \(f (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - a)\). Then for every \(a \in \mathbb{R}^4\), every word \(b\) and every \(t > 0\),

\(\displaystyle h_t (\tau_a b) = T_E (a) h_t (b) .\)

Proof. For words \(b, c\) of lengths \(n, m\), use Lemma 6.18 inside the scalar product of their Bochner integrals. The integrand is absolutely integrable on the product space: its absolute value is bounded by the product of the two integrable scalar test magnitudes times the two fixed-height bounds (6.16). Fubini and the coordinate permutation \((X, Y) \mapsto (X_n, \ldots, X_1, Y_1, \ldots, Y_m)\) therefore give

\(\displaystyle \langle h_s (b), h_t (c) \rangle = \psi_{(- ns, \ldots, - s, t, \ldots, mt)} (f_{b^{\ast} c}),\)

with labels \((Jv_n, \ldots, Jv_1, u_1, \ldots, u_m)\) and joint product test \(f_{b^{\ast} c}\), whose first factors are the reversed conjugates of the tests in \(b\). The displayed heights are strictly ordered for every \(s, t > 0\) and approach zero as the two parameters independently approach zero. Theorem 6.5 gives the limit \(w (b^{\ast} c) = K (b, c)\). The zero-dimensional integral for the empty word is the vacuum itself.

For translations write \(T_E (a) = E (- ia^0) U (\mathbf{a})\), where the imaginary-axis values of \(E\) denote its strongly continuous unitary boundary values. These boundary values and their law with the interior extension are part of the complex heat extension used here. Translating all real positions by \(a\) leaves all differences in (6.15) unchanged; its first factor changes by the commuting factor \(T_E (a)\). Hence

\(\displaystyle Q_{p, y} (X + a ; v) = T_E (a) Q_{p, y} (X ; v) .\)

For the empty word the same assertion follows from translation invariance of the vacuum. Substitute \(X \mapsto X + a\) in the integral with translated tests \(f_i (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - a)\) and move the bounded operator \(T_E (a)\) outside the Bochner integral. This proves the translation identity for every \(t > 0\).\(\Box\)

Thus the heat-vector limit required for positivity and for the isometric embedding of Proposition 6.24 follows from the slab bounds and complex heat extension. Lorentz covariance, spacelike locality, and retention of nonzero labels at the boundary are distinct remaining steps; they have not been inferred from the Gram identity.

6.8From boundary values to fields on a common domain

The construction by quotienting the positive form on field words goes back to Wightman [Wig56, §6]. We use joint tests and the common graded field labels. The joint boundary theorem supplies the positive form, and the arguments below identify the field operators and motions on its common domain.

The Euclidean Hilbert space and the relativistic field Hilbert space have related, but different, definitions. The bridge between them is a limit of vectors with small positive imaginary times. This section proves the bridge and the ensuing operator construction. The preceding two sections construct the analytic boundary values and verify their heat-vector bridge from the stated slab hypotheses. Positivity of the boundary values will now follow from that bridge.

Write \(E\) for the algebraic union of the finite-dimensional field spaces and \(\mathscr{S} = \mathscr{S} (\mathbb{R}^4 ; \mathbb{C})\) for the Schwartz space. A letter is a pair \(a = (v, f) \in E \times \mathscr{S}\), and a word is a finite ordered list of letters. Let \(J : E \to E\) be an antilinear involution, put \(a^{\ast} = (Jv, \bar{f})\), and define the star of a word by reversing its order and starring each letter. The empty word is denoted by \(1\). Concatenation is written without an intervening symbol.

Suppose \(W_n (v_1, \ldots, v_n)\) is a tempered distribution on \((\mathbb{R}^4)^n\), multilinear in the labels. Its value on a product test function defines \(w (a_1 \cdots a_n)\). Set

\(\displaystyle K (b, c) = w (b^{\ast} c) .\)

The joint distribution, rather than merely this restricted family of tests, will be needed in constructing the boundary values. For the operator argument below the following properties specify exactly what is used.

Input 6.20. (Boundary data) The functional \(w\) is multilinear separately in every label and every test function, and \(w (1) = 1\). For fixed words \(b, c\) and labels \(v, u\), the function

\(\displaystyle (f, g) \longmapsto K ((v, f) b, (u, g) c)\)

is jointly continuous in the Schwartz topology. There are vectors \(h_t (b)\), \(t > 0\), in the selected Euclidean Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}_E\), with unit vacuum \(\Omega_E\), such that

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \lim_{s, t \downarrow 0} \langle h_s (b), h_t (c) \rangle & = K (b, c), \hspace{3cm} \text{(6.18)} \\ \lim_{t \downarrow 0} h_t (1) & = \Omega_E . \end{array}\)

The two positive parameters in the first limit vary independently.

Here and below the inner product is antilinear in its first argument. In the application \(h_t (b)\) is the integral of a product of bounded slab insertions and intervening complex-time translations, at strictly increasing heights \((t, 2 t, \ldots, nt)\). Thus the integrability of this vector-valued integral and the joint limit (6.18) require proof. A scalar boundary limit along one fixed ray does not by itself give this input.

Lemma 6.21. (Removing the imaginary heights) Under Input 6.20, each \(h_t (b)\) has a norm limit \(q (b) \in \mathcal{H}_E\), and

\(\displaystyle \langle q (b), q (c) \rangle = K (b, c), \qquad q (1) = \Omega_E .\)

In particular \(K\) is a positive semidefinite kernel.

Proof. Fix \(b\). Expanding a squared norm gives

\(\displaystyle \|h_s (b) - h_t (b)\|^2 = \langle h_s (b), h_s (b) \rangle + \langle h_t (b), h_t (b) \rangle - \langle h_s (b), h_t (b) \rangle - \langle h_t (b), h_s (b) \rangle .\)

All four terms tend to \(K (b, b)\), including the diagonal terms, by the joint limit. The expression therefore tends to zero as \(s, t \downarrow 0\). Completeness gives the norm limit. Continuity of the inner product gives the asserted Gram identities and the specified empty-word limit gives the vacuum. For any finitely many coefficients \(c_i\) and words \(b_i\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i, j} \overline{c_i} K (b_i, b_j) c_j = | \sum_i c_i q (b_i) |^2 \geqslant 0.\)

\(\Box\)

Let \(\mathcal{P}\) be the vector space of formal finite complex linear combinations of words. Extend \(K\) sesquilinearly to \(\mathcal{P}\) and let \(\mathcal{N}= \{p : K (p, p) = 0\}\). Cauchy–Schwarz for a positive semidefinite form follows by applying positivity to \(p + zq\) and minimizing over \(z\) (the case \(K (q, q) = 0\) follows by allowing \(|z|\) to grow). Consequently \(\mathcal{N}\) is the radical of the form. The quotient \(\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{P}/\mathcal{N}\) is an inner-product space; let \(\mathcal{H}\) be its completion and \([b] \in \mathcal{D}\) the class of a word.

Proposition 6.22. (The common invariant domain) There is a bilinear map

\(\displaystyle (v, f) \longmapsto \Phi (v, f) : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{D}, \qquad \Phi (v, f) [b] = [(v, f) b] .\)

The vacuum \(\Omega = [1]\) has norm one, the domain \(\mathcal{D}\) is dense and spanned by its word vectors, and

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \langle \Phi (v, f) \xi, \eta \rangle & = \langle \xi, \Phi (Jv, \bar{f}) \eta \rangle, \hspace{3cm} \text{(6.19)} \\ \langle \Omega, \Phi (a_1) \cdots \Phi (a_n) \Omega \rangle & = w (a_1 \cdots a_n) . \end{array}\)

For each \(v\) and \(\xi \in \mathcal{D}\), the map \(f \mapsto \Phi (v, f) \xi\) is continuous from \(\mathscr{S}\) to \(\mathcal{H}\). As densely defined operators on \(\mathcal{H}\), \(\Phi (Jv, \bar{f}) \subseteq \Phi (v, f)^{\ast}\); in particular every field operator is closable.

Proof. Let \(L_a\) denote left concatenation on formal sums. Word reversal gives the exact identity

\(\displaystyle K (L_a p, q) = K (p, L_{a^{\ast}} q) .\)

If \(p \in \mathcal{N}\), the right side vanishes for every \(q\). Taking \(q = L_a p\) proves \(K (L_a p, L_a p) = 0\). Thus \(L_a\) preserves \(\mathcal{N}\) and descends to \(\mathcal{D}\). This is the necessary null-space argument; positivity without the concatenation identity would not suffice. The same identity proves (6.19). Multilinearity of \(w\) shows that every matrix element against a word of the defect in a proposed linearity identity is zero. Since words span \(\mathcal{D}\), each such defect is zero. This proves bilinearity of \(\Phi\) even though no multilinearity relations were imposed on the formal word space initially.

The vacuum norm, cyclicity and moment identity follow directly from the construction. To prove continuity first take \(\xi = [b]\) and write \(\eta_f = [(v, f) b]\). At any \(f_0\),

\(\displaystyle \| \eta_f - \eta_{f_0} \|^2 = K ((v, f) b, (v, f) b) + K ((v, f_0) b, (v, f_0) b) - 2 \text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} K ((v, f_0) b, (v, f) b),\)

which tends to zero by the assumed joint continuity. Finite linear combinations give the result for every \(\xi \in \mathcal{D}\). Finally (6.19), with \(\mathcal{D}\) dense, is precisely the criterion for the asserted adjoint inclusion. The adjoint domain contains the dense space \(\mathcal{D}\); hence the original operator is closable. Equivalently, if \(\xi_n \to 0\) and \(\Phi (v, f) \xi_n \to \eta\), pairing with each vector of \(\mathcal{D}\) gives \(\eta = 0\), which directly proves closability.\(\Box\)

Proposition 6.23. (Locality and retention of labels) Suppose, whenever \(f, g\) have mutually spacelike supports, that

\(\displaystyle w (b (v, f) (u, g) c) = w (b (u, g) (v, f) c)\)

for all exterior words \(b, c\). Then \([\Phi (v, f), \Phi (u, g)] = 0\) on \(\mathcal{D}\). Suppose also that a label \(v\) whose insertion has zero expectation against all exterior words and all tests must be zero. Then for every \(v \ne 0\) there is an \(f\) with \(\Phi (v, f) \ne 0\).

Proof. Pair the commutator applied to \([c]\) with \([b]\). Its value is the difference of the two assumed expectations, with left exterior word \(b^{\ast}\), and so is zero. Density and linearity prove the operator identity on \(\mathcal{D}\). If all \(\Phi (v, f)\) vanish, then for any \(b, c, f\),

\(\displaystyle w (b (v, f) c) = \langle [b^{\ast}], \Phi (v, f) [c] \rangle = 0,\)

because \((b^{\ast})^{\ast} = b\). The detection assumption now gives \(v = 0\).\(\Box\)

6.8.1The group action and its Euclidean embedding

Let \(G\) be the Poincaré group with proper orthochronous Lorentz part. Suppose its action on labels and pullback action on test functions induce an action \(b \mapsto gb\) on words, preserving the empty word. Assume

\(\displaystyle K (gb, gc) = K (b, c), \qquad g \longmapsto K (b, gc) \text{is continuous}\) (6.20)

for all words. These are properties of the continued distributions and the label action on each finite level. For the boundary distributions of Theorem 6.5 they are verified in Corollary 6.27, under the hypotheses stated there.

Proposition 6.24. There is a strongly continuous unitary representation \(U\) of \(G\) on \(\mathcal{H}\) with \(U (g) [b] = [gb]\). It fixes \(\Omega\), preserves \(\mathcal{D}\), and satisfies field covariance there. There is also a linear isometry \(\mathcal{V}: \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}_E\) with \(\mathcal{V} [b] = q (b)\) and \(\mathcal{V} \Omega = \Omega_E\). Let \(T_E\) be a unitary representation of the translation group on \(\mathcal{H}_E\), and write \(\tau_a b\) for the action of the translation by \(a\) on a word \(b\). Suppose that for every translation \(a\) and every word \(b\) there is \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon (a, b) > 0\) such that the heat vectors obey

\(\displaystyle h_t (\tau_a b) = T_E (a) h_t (b) \qquad (0 < t < \varepsilon) .\)

Then \(\mathcal{V}U (a) = T_E (a) \mathcal{V}\).

Proof. Extend the action on words linearly. Gram invariance makes it an isometry of the quotient; the inverse action makes its extension to the completion unitary. Multiplication and identity laws hold on words, hence on the dense span, hence on the completion. For a word \(b\), Gram invariance and (6.20) give

\(\displaystyle \|[gb] - [g_0 b]\|^2 = 2 K (b, b) - 2 \text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} K (g_0 b, gb) \longrightarrow 0.\)

Thus orbit maps are continuous first on finite word sums and then on every vector: approximate that vector by a finite sum, and use the isometry at both \(g\) and \(g_0\) to bound the approximation errors uniformly. The vacuum and domain statements follow on words. For a letter \(a\) and a word \(b\) the equality \(g (ab) = (ga) (gb)\) gives covariance on their span.

The map \(\sum c_i [b_i] \mapsto \sum c_i q (b_i)\) preserves squared norms by Lemma 6.21, so is well-defined and extends isometrically. Its vacuum identity is already known. Taking the norm limit in the stated translation identity for heat vectors proves intertwining on every word; boundedness then extends it to \(\mathcal{H}\).\(\Box\)

These arguments construct all fields on one common invariant domain. They still require the continued distributions, their locality and label-detection properties, the continuous group action, and the independent-height limit. The forward spectral condition and uniqueness of the invariant vacuum require additional facts about the Euclidean translation representation and are not consequences of the formal word construction alone.

6.9Joint tests and survival of field labels

Two details complete the common-domain reconstruction, apart from locality and the momentum projection measure. First, the joint tempered distributions supply the test-function continuity used in the word construction. Second, a label detectable by Euclidean correlations remains detectable after continuation to real time. The latter uses the full-tube construction and its identification with the ordered-time function; no pointwise boundary values of the fields are required.

Lemma 6.25. The finite linear span of functions \(\prod_{i = 1}^n f_i (X_i)\), \(f_i \in \mathscr{S} (\mathbb{R}^4)\), is dense in \(\mathscr{S} ((\mathbb{R}^4)^n)\). The product map from the \(n\) test spaces into the joint test space is continuous multilinear.

Proof. Every joint derivative of a product separates into the corresponding single-slot derivatives. Also \(1 + |X| \leqslant \prod_i (1 + |X_i |)\) up to a fixed constant depending on \(n\). Thus each weighted derivative supremum of the product is bounded by a constant times a product of single-slot Schwartz seminorms. This proves continuous multilinearity.

For density, the canonical map \(\mathscr{S} (\mathbb{R}^a) \hat{\otimes} \mathscr{S} (\mathbb{R}^b) \to \mathscr{S} (\mathbb{R}^{a + b})\) is an isomorphism [Trè67, Theorem 51.6, Eq. (51.3)]. The algebraic tensor product is dense in the completed product. Iterate with \(a = 4 (n - 1)\) and \(b = 4\), using continuity of the product map to approximate each \((n - 1)\)-slot factor. The case \(n = 1\) is immediate. For \(n = 0\) the empty product spans the scalar space.\(\Box\)

Corollary 6.26. For the jointly tempered distributions constructed in Theorem 6.5, the word functional is separately linear in every label and test. Its mixed Gram expressions

\(\displaystyle (f, g) \longmapsto K ((v, f) b, (u, g) c)\)

are jointly continuous. Thus these parts of Input 6.20 follow from the construction.

Proof. Multilinearity follows by composing the label-multilinear distribution with the multilinear product test. In the Gram expression the first variable occurs as \(\bar{f}\) in a fixed slot and the second as \(g\) in another fixed slot; all other factors and labels are fixed. Conjugation is continuous on Schwartz space. Lemma 6.25 and continuity of the fixed-label distribution therefore give the asserted joint continuity. The empty-word value is one because the zero-insertion continuation is constantly one.\(\Box\)

The remaining hypothesis (6.20) of Proposition 6.24 concerns the Poincaré group. Let \(g = (a, \Lambda)\) act on spacetime by \(gX = \Lambda X + a\), with \(\Lambda\) proper orthochronous, and on tests by \(P_g f = f \circ g^{- 1}\), as in Lemma 6.12. Let \(\rho\) be the label action of Proposition 6.10, on which translations act trivially. Define

\(\displaystyle g (v, f) = (\rho (\Lambda) v, P_g f)\)

on letters, extend it letter by letter to words, and let it fix the empty word. The word construction uses the label involution \(J\) of Subsection 5.1, the reflected conjugate. The following corollary assumes that this is the involution with which Proposition 6.10 proves commutation, that is, the involution of Input 6.9.

Corollary 6.27. (Poincaré invariance of the word kernel) Assume the hypotheses of Theorem 6.13 for the action \(\rho\) just described, and assume the identification of involutions just stated. Then \(b \mapsto gb\) is an action of the Poincaré group on words, \((gb)^{\ast} = g (b^{\ast})\), and

\(\displaystyle K (gb, gc) = K (b, c), \qquad g \longmapsto K (b, gc) \text{is continuous},\)

for all words \(b, c\). Thus (6.20) holds. The translation hypothesis of Proposition 6.24 is supplied, for every \(t > 0\), by Proposition 6.19.

Proof. For \(g, h\) in the Poincaré group, \(P_g (P_h f) (X) = f (h^{- 1} g^{- 1} X) = P_{gh} f (X)\), the Lorentz part of \(gh\) is the product of the Lorentz parts, and \(\rho\) is a representation. Hence the letterwise rule is a group action on words, and \(P_e\) and \(\rho (I)\) are identities.

Proposition 6.10 gives \(J \rho (\Lambda) = \rho (\Lambda) J\). Since \(P_g\) commutes with complex conjugation of tests,

\(\displaystyle (g (v, f))^{\ast} = (J \rho (\Lambda) v, \overline{P_g f}) = (\rho (\Lambda) Jv, P_g \bar{f}) = g ((v, f)^{\ast}) .\)

The star of a word reverses the order of its letters and stars each letter. Reversal commutes with a letterwise action, so \((gb)^{\ast} = g (b^{\ast})\) and \((gb)^{\ast} (gc) = g (b^{\ast} c)\).

Now let \(d = (v_1, f_1) \cdots (v_n, f_n)\) be any word and \(F = \prod_i f_i (X_i)\) its product test. The product of the moved tests is \(\prod_i (P_g f_i) (X_i) = F (g^{- 1} X_1, \ldots, g^{- 1} X_n)\), the simultaneous pullback of \(F\) by \(g\). Write \(g = (a, I) (0, \Lambda)\). The translation invariance and Lorentz covariance in Theorem 6.13 give

\(\displaystyle w (gd) = W_n (\rho (\Lambda) v) (P_{(a, I)} P_{(0, \Lambda)} F) = W_n (\rho (\Lambda) v) (P_{(0, \Lambda)} F) = W_n (v) (F) = w (d) .\)

Applying this to \(d = b^{\ast} c\) gives \(K (gb, gc) = w (g (b^{\ast} c)) = w (b^{\ast} c) = K (b, c)\).

For continuity fix words \(b\) of length \(n\) and \(c = (u_1, \varphi_1) \cdots (u_m, \varphi_m)\). All labels lie in one finite level \(E_d\). Choose a basis \(e_1, \ldots, e_N\) of \(E_d\) and write \(\rho (\Lambda) u_j = \sum_k r_{kj} (\Lambda) e_k\). The coefficients \(r_{kj}\) are continuous, because \(\rho\) is continuous on each finite level. Let \(F_g\) be the joint test of \(b^{\ast} (gc)\). It equals \(F_e \circ \tilde{g}^{- 1}\), where \(\tilde{g}\) is the invertible affine map of the joint position space which acts by the identity on the first \(n\) positions and by \(g\) on each of the last \(m\) positions. Multilinearity of \(W_{n + m}\) in the labels gives

\(\displaystyle K (b, gc) = \sum_{k_1, \ldots, k_m} \left( \prod_{j = 1}^m r_{k_j j} (\Lambda) \right) W_{n + m} (Jv_n, \ldots, Jv_1, e_{k_1}, \ldots, e_{k_m}) (F_g),\)

where \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) are the labels of \(b\). Each fixed-label distribution is continuous on the joint Schwartz space. The map \(g \mapsto \tilde{g}\) is continuous into the group of invertible affine maps of the joint position space, so the general form of Lemma 6.12 shows that \(g \mapsto F_g\) is continuous into the joint Schwartz space. The finite sum of products of continuous functions is continuous in \(g\).

The last statement is Proposition 6.19: for \(g = (a, I)\) the word action translates every test, and the heat vectors satisfy \(h_t (\tau_a b) = T_E (a) h_t (b)\) for every \(t > 0\).\(\Box\)

The identification of involutions is a same-data requirement, not a convention. Let \(\theta\) be the label action of the time reflection and \(\kappa\) the real structure, so that the reflected conjugate is \(J = \theta \kappa\). The time reflection conjugates the rotation in the time–\(a\) plane to its inverse, so \(\theta L_a \theta = - L_a\). If the Euclidean generators are real, \(\kappa L_a \kappa = L_a\), antilinearity of \(J\) gives

\(\displaystyle J (- iL_a) J^{- 1} = i \hspace{0.17em} \theta L_a \theta = - iL_a,\)

so \(J\) commutes with the boost generators \(K_a = - iL_a\), while \(\kappa\) alone would anticommute with them. Both \(\theta\) and \(\kappa\) commute with spatial rotations.

Lemma 6.28. (A zero boundary has zero strict continuation) Fix an ordered label tuple \(v\). If \(W_n (v) = 0\) as a joint tempered distribution, then \(F_n (t + iy, x ; v) = 0\) for every strictly ordered height tuple \(y\) and every real position tuple \((t, x)\).

Proof. The full-tube function is defined by \(H_n (v ; z) = (2 \pi)^{- 4 n} \hat{W}_n (v) (L_z)\). It is therefore identically zero if \(W_n (v) = 0\). Proposition 6.16 identifies its restriction at \(z = X + id (y)\) with the ordered-time function for every strictly ordered \(y\). This proves the assertion.\(\Box\)

Let \(T\) denote the selected Euclidean hierarchy. At each finite raw label level, its null space consists of labels whose insertion vanishes in every distinct-point correlation with arbitrary exterior labels. Assume the selected and original null spaces agree at every level, as established by the detector argument when its moment and rank hypotheses are supplied. Assume also the finite-level quotient embeddings into the union are injective and every union label has a finite-level representative.

Proposition 6.29. (Boundary detection) If a union label \(v\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle w (b (v, f) c) = 0\)

for every pair of exterior words \(b, c\) and every single-slot Schwartz test \(f\), then \(v = 0\). Consequently every nonzero label defines a nonzero smeared field operator in Proposition 6.22.

Proof. Take any ordered tuple of labels containing \(v\), at any slot. The assumption says that its joint distribution vanishes on every product test: group the factors before the distinguished slot into \(b\), and those after it into \(c\). Lemma 6.25 and tempered continuity therefore make that entire joint distribution zero. By Lemma 6.28, its strict-time continuation is zero. At real times zero, the pure-imaginary identity gives zero for the corresponding selected Euclidean correlation whenever the Euclidean times are strictly increasing.

For arbitrary positions with pairwise distinct time coordinates, sort them by time. The label tuple is permuted with the positions and still contains \(v\), so the preceding zero-distribution argument applies to the sorted tuple. Euclidean permutation symmetry transfers its zero value back to the original ordering. Such position tuples are dense in the full configuration space of distinct points: perturb the finitely many time coordinates by distinct arbitrarily small amounts, avoiding the finitely many equality hyperplanes. Openness of the distinct-point configuration space keeps all points distinct during a sufficiently small perturbation. Continuity of the Euclidean correlations extends the zero value to every configuration, including those with time ties.

Represent \(v\) by a finite-level raw label. The preceding conclusion, with arbitrary raw exterior labels projected into the union, is exactly the all-exterior null criterion for that label in \(T\). Equality of selected and original null spaces makes its original quotient class zero. Its union image is \(v\), so \(v = 0\). This proves the detection premise of Proposition 6.23; that proposition then supplies the nonzero smeared operator for every nonzero label.\(\Box\)

6.10Positive energy and the joint momentum measure

The Fourier support of the vacuum distributions and the spectrum of the translation operators are different assertions. We now prove the latter. The essential analytic fact is elementary: a positive finite measure whose Fourier transform extends boundedly to the upper half-plane cannot charge negative frequencies. Positivity is what makes a Gaussian probe detect such a charge without cancellation.

We use the spectral theorem for strongly continuous unitary representations of locally compact abelian groups, in the form of the SNAG theorem [BdlHV07, Theorem D.3.1, p.397]. Applied to a strongly continuous unitary representation \(T\) of \(\mathbb{R}^4\), it supplies a unique regular projection-valued measure \(P\) on \(\mathbb{R}^4\) with

\(\displaystyle T (a) = \int e^{ip \cdot a} \hspace{0.17em} dP (p), \qquad P (\mathbb{R}^4) = I.\) (6.21)

Here we identify the character indexed by \(p\) with \(a \mapsto e^{ip \cdot a}\); the pairing is Euclidean, as in Subsection 6.3. The hypotheses hold for the translation representation constructed in Proposition 6.24, and for its Euclidean counterpart in Subsection 6.1. In particular, continuity is strong operator continuity, not operator-norm continuity. For each vector \(v\), \(\mu_v (B) = \langle v, P (B) v \rangle\) is a positive regular finite measure, of mass \(\|v\|^2\), with characteristic function \(\langle v, T (a) v \rangle\).

Lemma 6.32. (A bounded upper-half-plane extension) Let \(\mu\) be a positive finite Borel measure on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), and let \(\ell : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\) be continuous. Suppose a function \(F\) is continuous on \(\text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} z \geqslant 0\), holomorphic on \(\text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} z > 0\), bounded there by \(C\), and satisfies

\(\displaystyle F (t) = \int e^{it \ell (p)} \hspace{0.17em} d \mu (p) \qquad (t \in \mathbb{R}) .\)

Then \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \mu \subseteq \{p : \ell (p) \geqslant 0\}\).

Proof. Fix \(\varepsilon > 0\), \(b \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(s \geqslant 0\). Multiply \(F (z)\) by \(\exp (- ibz - \varepsilon z^2)\) and integrate around the rectangle with corners \(- R, R, R + is, - R + is\). Cauchy's theorem applies: the function is continuous on the rectangle and holomorphic in its interior. One can also apply the theorem above height \(\delta > 0\) and let \(\delta \downarrow 0\); continuity on each compact rectangle justifies that limit. On either vertical edge the magnitude is at most \(C \exp (- \varepsilon R^2 + |b|s + \varepsilon s^2)\). The vertical integrals tend to zero. The horizontal integrals converge absolutely, since at height \(u\) the common majorant is \(C \exp (- \varepsilon t^2 + bu + \varepsilon u^2)\). Consequently the integrals along heights zero and \(s\) agree, and

\(\displaystyle \left| \int_{\mathbb{R}} F (t) e^{- ibt - \varepsilon t^2} \hspace{0.17em} dt \right| \leqslant C \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}} \hspace{0.17em} e^{bs + \varepsilon s^2} .\) (6.22)

Fubini applies to the boundary integral because \(\mu\) is finite and \(e^{- \varepsilon t^2}\) is integrable. The Gaussian transform gives

\(\displaystyle \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{it (\ell (p) - b) - \varepsilon t^2} \hspace{0.17em} dt = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}} \hspace{0.17em} e^{- (\ell (p) - b)^2 / (4 \varepsilon)} .\)

For completeness, if \(G (a) = \int e^{iat - \varepsilon t^2} \hspace{0.17em} dt\), differentiation under the integral and integration by parts give \(G' (a) = - aG (a) / (2 \varepsilon)\). The value \(G (0) = \sqrt{\pi / \varepsilon}\) follows by squaring the Gaussian integral and using polar coordinates. Solving this differential equation proves the formula with its stated normalization.

Suppose \(p_0 \in \text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \mu\) and \(b = \ell (p_0) < 0\). In (6.22) choose \(s = - b / (2 \varepsilon)\) and cancel the positive factor \(\sqrt{\pi / \varepsilon}\). Positivity of the resulting integral yields

\(\displaystyle \int e^{- (\ell (p) - b)^2 / (4 \varepsilon)} \hspace{0.17em} d \mu (p) \leqslant Ce^{- b^2 / (4 \varepsilon)} .\)

The open neighborhood \(A = \{p : (\ell (p) - b)^2 < b^2 / 4\}\) of \(p_0\) has mass \(m > 0\). Its contribution to the left side is at least \(me^{- b^2 / (16 \varepsilon)}\). Hence \(m \leqslant Ce^{- 3 b^2 / (16 \varepsilon)}\) for every \(\varepsilon > 0\). Letting \(\varepsilon \downarrow 0\) is a contradiction.\(\Box\)

Apply the lemma first on the Euclidean Hilbert space. Write \(T_E (t, \mathbf{x}) = V (t) U_E (\mathbf{x})\) for its joint translations, where \(V\) is the unitary time group of Proposition 6.1. For \(v \in \mathcal{H}_E\) define

\(\displaystyle F_v (t + is) = \langle v, V (t) E_s v \rangle \qquad (s \geqslant 0) .\)

This function is continuous on the closed upper half-plane by strong continuity and the uniform operator bounds. On its interior it equals \(\langle v, E (- iz) v \rangle\), so is holomorphic and bounded by \(\|v\|^2\). Its boundary is the characteristic function of the time coordinate of the joint measure \(\mu^E_v\). The lemma gives

\(\displaystyle \text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \mu^E_v \subseteq \{p : p^0 \geqslant 0\} .\) (6.23)

Spatial covariance is not used in this step.

Theorem 6.33. (Forward spectrum and the unique vacuum) Suppose the word construction has a strongly continuous Poincaré representation \(U\) with proper orthochronous Lorentz part, and an isometric translation intertwiner \(\mathcal{V}: \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}_E\) as in Proposition 6.24. Then its joint momentum measure satisfies

\(\displaystyle P (\mathbb{R}^4 \setminus \bar{V}_+) = 0, \qquad \bar{V}_+ = \{p : p^0 \geqslant |\mathbf{p}|\} .\)

If the heat-fixed subspace of \(\mathcal{H}_E\) is \(\mathbb{C} \Omega_E\), then the vectors fixed by all translations on \(\mathcal{H}\) are exactly \(\mathbb{C} \Omega\).

Proof. The intertwining identity and isometry give

\(\displaystyle \langle v, U (a) v \rangle = \langle \mathcal{V}v, T_E (a)\mathcal{V}v \rangle .\)

Thus \(\mu_v\) and \(\mu^E_{\mathcal{V}v}\) have equal characteristic functions and are equal measures. Here is the measure-uniqueness step explicitly. For any Schwartz function \(f\), Fourier inversion and Fubini express its integral against a finite measure in terms of that characteristic function; Fubini follows from integrability of \(\hat{f}\). The two measures therefore agree on all smooth compactly supported tests. Continuous compactly supported tests are uniformly approximated, in a fixed compact neighborhood of their supports, by smooth mollifications. They too have equal integrals. Two finite positive Borel measures on \(\mathbb{R}^4\) with equal integrals on \(C_c (\mathbb{R}^4)\) are equal. Indeed, for compact \(K\) the functions \(\phi_k (p) = \max (0, 1 - k \text{dist} (p, K))\) lie in \(C_c (\mathbb{R}^4)\) and decrease pointwise to the indicator of \(K\), so dominated convergence gives equal masses on every compact set; compact sets form a \(\pi\)-system generating the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra, and the total masses agree by monotone convergence along balls, so Dynkin's \(\pi\)–\(\lambda\) theorem gives equality of the measures. In particular every \(\mu_v\) obeys (6.23).

For a Lorentz matrix \(\Lambda\), covariance of translations implies

\(\displaystyle \mu_{U (\Lambda) v} = (\Lambda^{- T})_{\ast} \mu_v .\)

Indeed the characteristic function of the left side at \(a\) equals \(\langle v, U (\Lambda^{- 1} a) v \rangle\), which is that of the right side. The preceding uniqueness argument applies again. A homeomorphism carries the support of a measure to the support of its pushforward: every neighborhood of the image has a preimage neighborhood of positive measure, and the converse follows by its inverse. It follows that, for any \(p \in \text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \mu_v\),

\(\displaystyle (\Lambda^{- T} p)^0 \geqslant 0 \quad \text{for every proper orthochronous } \Lambda .\)

The dual matrices \(\Lambda^{- T} = \eta \Lambda \eta\), with \(\eta = \text{diag} (1, - 1, - 1, - 1)\), range over the same group. The identity gives \(p^0 \geqslant 0\). If \(|\mathbf{p}| > p^0\), take a boost in the direction \(\mathbf{p}/ |\mathbf{p}|\) with positive rapidity \(r\) such that \(\tanh r > p^0 / |\mathbf{p}|\). Its time coordinate is \(p^0 \cosh r - |\mathbf{p}| \sinh r < 0\), a contradiction. Thus every \(\mu_v\) is supported in \(\bar{V}_+\). The complement of the support of a finite Borel measure on \(\mathbb{R}^4\) has measure zero: it is a union of a countable collection of zero-measure basis neighborhoods. Hence \(\mu_v (\bar{V}_+^c) = 0\) for every \(v\). Since \(P (B)\) is an orthogonal projection, \(\|P (B) v\|^2 = \mu_v (B)\), which proves the asserted operator identity.

Finally let \(v\) be translation invariant. Its image \(\mathcal{V}v\) is fixed by all Euclidean real-time translations. The fixed-vector equivalence proved in Subsection 6.1 makes it heat fixed. Therefore \(\mathcal{V}v = c \Omega_E =\mathcal{V} (c \Omega)\); injectivity of the isometry gives \(v = c \Omega\). The converse follows from vacuum invariance. This proves uniqueness under the stated heat hypothesis, and makes no spectral-gap assertion.\(\Box\)

6.11Spacelike exchange and locality

We prove that exchanging two adjacent spacelike-separated insertions does not change the boundary distribution. The argument keeps all the other insertions present. It first makes the two tube functions analytic near a partial boundary with one tied imaginary-time gap. Euclidean symmetry identifies their germs when the real times vanish. Analytic continuation along a path then identifies them at arbitrary spacelike configurations. Finally their partial boundary distributions agree, and the remaining imaginary heights can be removed.

Fix adjacent slots \(i, j = i + 1\) and let \(\sigma\) exchange them. It acts on tuples of labels, real points and complex points by the same permutation. Put

\(\displaystyle O_{ij} = \{X : X_j - X_i \text{ is spacelike} \} = \{X : |X_j^0 - X_i^0 | < |\mathbf{X}_j -\mathbf{X}_i |\} .\)

Choose scalar heights \(y\) with \(y_i = y_j\) and with every other ordered pair strictly increasing. Then \(d (y) \in C_n\) and \(\sigma d (y) = d (y)\). The point \(X + id (y)\) lies in the closures of both \(\mathcal{T}_n\) and \(\sigma^{- 1} \mathcal{T}_n\): strictly separate the tied heights in the appropriate order by arbitrarily small amounts. The functions to be compared are

\(\displaystyle z \longmapsto H_n (v ; z), \qquad z \longmapsto H_n (v \circ \sigma ; \sigma z) .\)

6.11.1Small imaginary boosts enter both tubes

For a spatial unit vector \(e\), write, in time-first coordinates,

\(\displaystyle C_e (t, \mathbf{x}) = (e \cdot \mathbf{x}, te), \qquad P_e (t, \mathbf{x}) = (t, (e \cdot \mathbf{x}) e) .\)

The boost and its entire complex extension are \(B_e (z) = I + \sinh z \hspace{0.17em} C_e + (\cosh z - 1) P_e\). For a spacelike vector \(a\), the choice \(e =\mathbf{a}/ |\mathbf{a}|\) gives \(C_e a = (|\mathbf{a}|, a^0 e) \in V_+\).

Lemma 6.34. For \(X \in O_{ij}\) and \(q = X + id (y)\) there are \(e\) and \(\tau > 0\) such that \(B_e (i \tau) q \in \mathcal{T}_n\) and \(\sigma B_e (- i \tau) q \in \mathcal{T}_n\). Moreover, for all \(z\) sufficiently close to \(q\) in \(\mathcal{T}_n\), \(B_e (ib) z \in \mathcal{T}_n\) for every \(0 \leqslant b \leqslant \tau\). For all \(z\) sufficiently close to \(q\) in \(\sigma^{- 1} \mathcal{T}_n\), \(\sigma B_e (- ib) z \in \mathcal{T}_n\) throughout the same interval.

Proof. Take \(e\) from the spacelike difference \(X_j - X_i\) as above. For any adjacent difference \(z_{k + 1} - z_k\), write its real and imaginary parts as \(x\) and \(Y\). The imaginary part after the positive imaginary boost is

\(\displaystyle Y + \sin b \hspace{0.17em} C_e x + (\cos b - 1) P_e Y.\) (6.24)

At \(q\), every gap other than \((i, j)\) has \(Y \in V_+\). By openness and continuity those finitely many transformed gaps remain in \(V_+\) for \((b, z)\) in a product neighborhood of \((0, q)\). At the distinguished gap \(Y = 0\) and \(C_e x \in V_+\). For small \(b\) rewrite (6.24) as

\(\displaystyle Y + \sin b \left( C_e x - \frac{\sin b}{1 + \cos b} P_e Y \right) .\)

The bracket is continuous near \((0, q)\) and lies in \(V_+\) there, after shrinking the neighborhood. For \(0 < b < \pi\), the sine is positive. If \(Y \in \bar{V}_+\), the sum is strictly in \(V_+\): the spatial triangle inequality shows that a closed forward-cone vector plus a positive multiple of a strict one is strict.

For the negative boost followed by the exchange, the original tied gap is reversed and the sine changes sign. Its inward vector is again \(C_e (X_j - X_i)\). All other imaginary gaps at \(q\) remain strictly forward, since the tied heights agree and are the only tie. The same neighborhood argument applies. Take a common product neighborhood for both orientations and all gaps, and choose \(\tau > 0\) inside its positive parameter interval. At \(q\) the closed-gap condition gives the two strict endpoints. At nearby interior points it gives strictness for \(b > 0\), while \(b = 0\) follows from membership in the relevant original tube. This proves the asserted uniform path property, including its endpoint at zero.\(\Box\)

6.11.2Holomorphic germs across the tied gap

The labels in any finite tuple lie in one common finite-dimensional level. On that level the continuous representation \(r \mapsto \rho (B_e (r))\) has an entire extension. Here is the elementary argument, which uses operator-norm continuity only in this finite-dimensional space. For a continuous one-parameter group \(R (r)\) in its matrix algebra, put \(A (t) = \int_0^t R (s) \hspace{0.17em} ds\). Then \(A (t) / t \to I\) as \(t \to 0\), so \(A (a)\) is invertible for a sufficiently small nonzero \(a\). The group law gives

\(\displaystyle R (t) A (a) = A (t + a) - A (t) .\)

The right side is continuously differentiable. Thus \(R\) is differentiable; differentiating the group law gives \(R' (t) = R (t) K\), \(K = R' (0)\). The derivative of \(R (t) e^{- tK}\) is zero and its value at zero is \(I\). Therefore \(R (t) = e^{tK}\), and \(R (z) = e^{zK}\) is the required entire extension. This construction is compatible with the finite-level inclusions, since the real representations are compatible and the identity theorem extends their equality to complex parameters.

For a point \(q\) and parameters supplied by Lemma 6.34, define near \(q\)

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} A_q (z) & = H_n (R (i \tau) v ; B_e (i \tau) z),\\ B_q (z) & = H_n (R (- i \tau) (v \circ \sigma) ; \sigma B_e (- i \tau) z) . \end{array}\)

They are holomorphic in a neighborhood of \(q\), because their arguments at \(q\) lie strictly in the tube. They extend the respective original tube functions. To prove this for \(A_q\), fix a nearby \(z \in \mathcal{T}_n\) for which the entire path stays in the tube. The function of one complex parameter

\(\displaystyle \zeta \longmapsto H_n (R (\zeta) v ; B_e (\zeta) z)\)

is holomorphic on an open neighborhood of the segment \(i [0, \tau]\). Moving labels cause no difficulty: expansion in a basis of the common finite level makes the expression a finite sum of holomorphic coefficients times the fixed-label holomorphic tube functions. For real \(\zeta\) near zero the expression equals \(H_n (v ; z)\) by tube covariance. The identity theorem on the connected component containing the segment makes it constant along that segment. Its value at \(i \tau\) proves the assertion. The same proof with the negative segment and the exchange gives the assertion for \(B_q\).

Such a germ is uniquely determined by its original tube function. Indeed, two candidate germs are holomorphic on a common ball about \(q\) and agree on the intersection of that ball with the relevant open tube. This intersection contains a nonempty open set because \(q\) is in the tube's closure. The identity theorem on the ball makes the germs equal.

Lemma 6.35. (Equality on the Euclidean slice) If every real time coordinate of \(X\) is zero, then the germs \(A_q\) and \(B_q\) coincide at \(q = X + id (y)\).

Proof. Let \(M\) be the real-linear Wick map that sends Euclidean time to imaginary time and leaves spatial coordinates real. Put \(p = ((y_k, \mathbf{X}_k))_{k = 1}^n\), so \(Mp = q\). This is a distinct-point Euclidean configuration. All pairs other than \((i, j)\) have different time coordinates, and the distinguished pair has different spatial coordinates because its real separation is spacelike. The Euclidean correlation \(S_v\) is consequently smooth near \(p\).

On the open side where the Euclidean times are strictly ordered, \(A_q \circ M = S_v\) near \(p\), by the tube restriction and pure-imaginary identification. On the open side where their exchanged tuple is strictly ordered, \(B_q \circ M = S_v\), using Euclidean permutation symmetry as well. The point \(p\) is in the closure of each side. Differentiate the respective identities on their open sides. For each order, continuity of the derivatives up to \(p\) shows that the two real derivatives at \(p\) both equal the corresponding derivative of \(S_v\).

The image of \(M\) spans the full complex coordinate space over \(\mathbb{C}\): each spatial basis vector is already in the image, and each time basis vector is \(- i\) times an image vector. By the chain rule the real derivatives just compared are the restrictions of the complex multilinear derivatives of \(A_q\) and \(B_q\) to this image. Complex multilinearity and spanning make all their full complex derivatives equal. Their convergent local power series therefore agree. This proves equality of germs, rather than merely equality of values on the Euclidean slice.\(\Box\)

The equality propagates to every \(X \in O_{ij}\). Keep its spatial coordinates fixed and multiply all its real time coordinates by \(s \in [0, 1]\). The distinguished separation remains spacelike throughout. Along \(q (s) = ((sX_k^0, \mathbf{X}_k))_k + id (y)\) both germs exist, and both underlying tubes have \(q (s)\) in their closures. Germ equality is locally constant in \(s\). To verify this, choose a common ball where the two germs at \(q (s_0)\) are holomorphic and extend their original tube functions. For \(s\) near \(s_0\), uniqueness of germs identifies the germs at \(q (s)\) with these same functions. The identity theorem on the ball says that equality near either point is equivalent to equality near the other. Thus the set of parameters with equal germs and its complement are both open in \([0, 1]\). Connectedness and Lemma 6.35 at \(s = 0\) give equality at \(s = 1\).

6.11.3From germs to boundary distributions

Let \(W_{d (y), v}\) denote the partial boundary distribution (6.13). Near a given \(X \in O_{ij}\) it is represented by the germ \(A_q\) at the partial height:

\(\displaystyle W_{d (y), v} (f) = \int A_q (Z + id (y)) f (Z) \hspace{0.17em} dZ\) (6.25)

for compactly supported smooth \(f\) in a sufficiently small ball about \(X\). Indeed, choose a smaller product neighborhood of \((X, d (y))\) on which the germ is bounded and agrees with the tube function at interior heights. For \(Y \to d (y)\) through \(C_n^{\circ}\), its integrals against \(f\) converge to the displayed integral by dominated convergence, using a constant times \(|f|\) as majorant. The tube boundary theorem identifies the same limit with \(W_{d (y), v} (f)\). Applying this argument to the exchanged tube, and changing variables by the coordinate permutation of absolute Jacobian one, gives the analogous formula for \(W_{\sigma d (y), v \circ \sigma} (f \circ \sigma)\) with germ \(B_q\). Since these germs agree, the two distributions agree locally on \(O_{ij}\).

They agree on every Schwartz test whose closed support is contained in \(O_{ij}\). For compact support, cover it by finitely many of the local balls and apply a smooth partition of unity. For an arbitrary Schwartz test \(f\) with closed support contained in \(O_{ij}\), put \(f_R = \chi (X / R) f\) with \(\chi\) as in the proof of Proposition 6.11: \(\chi \in C_c^{\infty}\) and \(\chi = 1\) near the origin. Each \(f_R\) is smooth with compact support contained in \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} f \subset O_{ij}\), so the compact-support case applies to it, and \(f_R \to f\) in the Schwartz topology as \(R \to \infty\), by the estimate given in that proof. Both sides of the equality are continuous on Schwartz space, so the equality passes to the limit. (The cutoffs \(\chi (\cdot / R)\) themselves do not converge in Schwartz space; only the products \(f_R\) do.)

Theorem 6.36. (Adjacent spacelike exchange) For every joint Schwartz test \(f\) supported in \(O_{ij}\),

\(\displaystyle W_n (v) (f) = W_n (v \circ \sigma) (f \circ \sigma) .\)

Consequently the reconstructed field operators commute on their common domain whenever their test functions have mutually spacelike supports.

Proof. There is a sequence of tied profiles of the kind used above tending to zero. For example, with slots numbered \(1, \ldots, n\), take \(y_k^{(m)} = a_k / (m + 1)\) where \(a_k = k\) except \(a_j = i\). All other ordered pairs have positive gaps, and the exchanged profile is unchanged. The preceding partial-distribution equality holds for each \(m\). Continuity on the closed shift set, and \(W_0 = W_n (v)\), give the asserted equality by letting \(m \to \infty\).

For the operator conclusion take any two exterior words and place the two test functions \(f_i, f_j\) between them. The closed support of their joint product test is contained in the inverse image of \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} f_i \times \text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} f_j\) under the two coordinate projections: wherever either factor vanishes on a neighborhood, so does the product. Mutually spacelike supports therefore put that joint support in \(O_{ij}\). The distributional identity exchanges exactly these two letters and retains all spectators. Proposition 6.23 now proves that the commutator vanishes on \(\mathcal{D}\).\(\Box\)
