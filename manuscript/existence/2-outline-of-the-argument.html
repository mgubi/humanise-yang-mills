2Outline of the argument

The construction starts with finite-dimensional Wilson integrals and keeps one choice of lattice observables through renormalization, extraction and reconstruction. This section explains the route and the role of the main estimates. It is a guide to the proofs and their hypotheses, not a replacement for them.

2.1Rescaling and one-step estimates

For a coefficient vector \(v\) in a frame of maximal grade \(d\), the diagonal grade dilation at block size \(L\) is

\(\displaystyle (\textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, L} v)_{\alpha} = L^{d - \deg (\alpha)} v_{\alpha},\)

where \(\deg (\alpha)\) is the grade of the coordinate \(\alpha\). For a step with block side \(\ell_m\) the dilation is \(\textsf{D}^{\text{gr}}_{d, \ell_m}\). The dilation is the diagonal part of the linear coefficient transfer; the other factor mixes grades. See Subsection 10.9 for the transfer matrix and its bounds.

In a nonlinear RG step the leading coefficients are compared with this linear transfer and the remainder is estimated in a weighted activity norm. The one-step transfer record also specifies the coarse coefficient field, its exact density identity, and a response decomposition with input and output bounds. See Subsubsection 13.10.14 for the full definition and the estimates for the three elementary insertions. Where a bound is obtained by summing absolute coefficients against weights, the sum itself is a weighted coefficient norm.

2.2The construction and its scope

The main statement is Theorem 1.2. Its assembly in Theorem 19.7 retains one lattice family, one refinement, one field quotient and one normalized stress tensor. The weaker public target in Section 1 is a consequence, but its abstract formulation alone does not identify the lattice representatives with curvature polynomials. That connection is supplied by the local sections and their reference-normalized transport. The final assembly identifies the actual suppliers of the construction premises, rather than inferring them from the existence of separate local lemmas.

The result has no mass-gap or uniqueness-of-the-infinite-volume-limit assertion. Its trace law excludes a specified class of finite mass-shell profiles; it does not exclude every generalized free-field description. The scope paragraph following the headline compares these conclusions with the Yang–Mills existence and mass-gap problem.

2.3Prerequisites

We assume finite-dimensional Lie groups, probability Haar measure on compact groups, and measure and integration, including Fubini–Tonelli, the \(\pi\)–\(\lambda\) theorem and Bochner integration. We also use Cauchy's theorem and formula, Hahn–Banach, elementary Hilbert-space theory, the Fourier transform on Schwartz space, and elementary Gaussian inequalities and Wick's rule. The specific uses of commutative \(C^{\ast}\)-algebras, Riesz representation, the spectral theorem for unitary representations, and the local analytic structure of matrix groups are cited where they enter. In the spectral arguments the dual of \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is identified with \(\mathbb{R}^d\) by \(p \mapsto (x \mapsto e^{ip \cdot x})\); Fourier normalizations are stated with the formulas that use them.

2.4Context

The finite-volume measures are Wilson's lattice gauge theory [Wil74]. Reflection positivity of these measures and the resulting positive transfer matrix were established by Osterwalder and Seiler and by Lüscher [OS78, Lüs77], and lattice gauge theories were analysed from the constructive point of view by Brydges, Fröhlich and Seiler and by Seiler [BFS79, BFS80, BFS81, Sei82]. The perturbative discovery of asymptotic freedom [GW73, Pol73] motivates the logarithmic running law. The relation between the bare lattice coupling and continuum renormalization schemes was computed at one loop in [HH80, DG81] and at two loops in [LW95]. None of these results supplies the uniform lattice estimate proved here.

The construction proceeds by block-spin renormalization for lattice gauge fields, an approach developed in Bałaban's programme. Its ingredients include regularity and decay of lattice Green's functions [Bał83]; propagators for the renormalization transformations, first for the free gauge field and then in a background field [Bał84a, Bał84b, Bał85b]; averaging operations, spaces of regular configurations and gauge fixing [Bał85a, Bał85c]; the variational problem for background fields [Bał85d]; ultraviolet stability in three dimensions [Bał85e]; effective actions, cluster expansions and coupling-constant renormalization in four dimensions [Bał87, Bał88b, Bał88a]; and the large-field renormalization operation [Bał89a, Bał89b]. Dimock's expository series [Dim13a, Dim13b, Dim13c] presents the method in detail for a scalar model. Other constructive approaches to non-abelian gauge fields are Federbush's phase-cell programme [Fed86, Fed87a, Fed88, Fed90, Fed87b] and the construction of Magnen, Rivasseau and Sénéor with an infrared cutoff [MRS93]. Continuum limits are known for abelian lattice gauge theories in three and four dimensions [Gro83, Dri87] and for the abelian Higgs model [Kin86], and the two-dimensional Yang–Mills measure has been constructed in the continuum [Dri89, Sen97, Lév03]. These papers provide historical context; their construction theorems are not used as substitutes for the estimates below.

For probabilistic background, see [Cha19, Che22]. Subsequent work constructs the Langevin dynamics for the two-dimensional Yang–Mills measure [CCHS22]. In three dimensions, work constructs state spaces for distributional gauge orbits [CC24] and a local Markov process on gauge orbits for the stochastic Yang–Mills–Higgs flow [CCHS24]. The existence and mass-gap problem is formulated in [JW06].

The passage to a relativistic theory follows the Osterwalder–Schrader strategy [OS73, OS75]. The 1975 sequel corrects the original sufficiency argument and gives revised hypotheses. Glaser [Gla74] proves analytic continuation and a corresponding positivity property on the causal domain. Zinoviev [Zin95] proves an equivalence theorem with a further weak spectral condition. The Wightman framework and the reconstruction of a relativistic theory from Euclidean correlations are treated in [SW64, GJ87]. Here the continuation, boundary, locality and spectral arguments are carried out directly for the constructed correlations. The comparison with the corrected abstract theorem in Subsection 5.1 is not a premise of that proof.

2.5The order of the limits

The lattice spacings \(a_N = D_N^{- 1}\), physical periods \(P_p\), bare coefficients and graded insertions are chosen before the requested extraction order. For each pair of increasing index maps \((u, p)\) one then chooses a refinement \((u \circ v, p \circ q)\) and one continuum hierarchy. The Wilson limit uses the full mesh sequence at every suitable fixed period, followed by the selected period sequence \(p \circ q\). The refined mesh is used for auxiliary comparisons, not as a replacement for that full mesh limit. One refinement serves all arities, labels and tests. Lemma 19.4 identifies a subsequential row limit with an already established full row limit; it does not prove full mesh convergence from compactness.

2.6The plan in ten steps

  1. Finite lattice and quadratic transformation. Normalize the Wilson measure, establish gauge and reflection positivity, and calculate the free block transformation. Laurent-symbol estimates give exponentially decaying rows on every torus. The flat one-step estimate and its scalar recurrence compare different terminal cutoffs. Curvature factorization supplies the positive rows used by the quadratic horizon; its bounds retain the powers of the block side in the residue sums. See Sections 3.1–3.16.

  2. One nonlinear block step. Defect geometry allocates action to large-field regions. On each sector, a positive precision and localized covariance control the Gaussian integral. Exact reassembly, a convergent cluster expansion and local jet extraction give the nonlinear step and its marked versions. These are identities and estimates for the same density, not separately chosen approximations. The principal constructions begin in Sections 7.8, 8.1 and 8.29.

  3. Stationary analysis and the one-loop coefficient. A uniform stationary chart and determinant comparison isolate the quadratic logarithmic term. The annulus estimate gives \(\lambda_G = 11 C_G / (24 \pi^2)\) in the color inner product fixed by the Wilson representation. It is the same constant as \(2 b_0 (G)\) and the shooting slope \(s_G\); see (12.86) and Subsection 12.12.

  4. One selected history and schedule. The reset and shooting construction keeps the couplings within a sublinear error of a line in \(\log a_m^{- 1}\). Specialization to the finest coefficient gives the period-uniform running law. Numerical profiles and floors are chosen in the required order before the block lengths; costs evaluated after those choices are absorbed on the same schedule. The public family retains the resulting actual coefficients, levels, paths and insertion transport. See Sections 12.13, 19.2 and 19.5.

  5. Centered estimates and ordered limits. Reflection positivity turns finite-cell root bounds into estimates for separated correlations. A fixed intermediate scale controls the discarded higher marked terms; convergence of the retained data there yields the full mesh Cauchy estimate. Difference estimates give smoothness away from coincidences, and compactness gives one volume refinement for all tests. See Sections 4.8, 10.13, 4.10 and 4.14. Their common-history premises are part of the final assembly check.

  6. The Euclidean Hilbert space and phases. Reflection positivity gives a contraction semigroup. Bounded slab insertions compressed to its ground space generate a commutative algebra. Its character space carries the vacuum's full-support probability measure. Evaluating whole words gives component correlations and the barycenter identity. Uniform slab bounds, derivatives and all-spectator null relations survive this construction. See Sections 5.1–5.8.

  7. Fields, stress and calibrated actions. Finite probes and interacting/free comparisons identify field ranks and compatible grade inclusions. The resulting physical field space carries one conserved stress tensor with fixed Ward normalization, and one compatible rotation and derivative calculus. Its shell identities pass to every character before continuation. See Sections 16.12, 17.3, 17.10 and 17.11.

  8. Relativistic reconstruction in every character. Complex heat time gives ordered holomorphic correlations; common growth bounds give joint tempered boundary distributions. Lorentz covariance, locality, the word domain and the forward spectrum are obtained before vacuum uniqueness. The latter first holds almost everywhere, then for every character: locality and cone support give a uniform low-energy bound, hence a uniform decaying heat remainder; continuity in the character and full support remove the exceptional set. Theorems 6.5, 6.33 and 5.19 give these steps. Proposition 19.3 retains the mixture identity on arbitrary joint Schwartz tests.

  9. The operator-product expansion. One finite-dimensional prescription is matched with the local grouping of inserted fields. Uniform character estimates give both coefficient bounds and the remainder after truncation at any fixed degree. The coefficients are independent of the state and spectators, whereas the small-radius threshold may depend on the fields, geometry and truncation. See Theorem 15.4 and the matching arguments in Subsection 15.4.

  10. The centered physical trace. The same Ward-normalized tensor is centered at the selected character. Rounded lattice comparisons, the common chart estimates and the free contraction determine its short-distance amplitude. Positivity and monotonicity remove the rounding; the finite-profile argument then gives the stated exclusion. Theorem 18.53 attaches this conclusion to the selected hierarchy without a new extraction or a new stress normalization.

2.7Reading order and final interfaces

The exposition presents the analytic consequences before many of their construction estimates. Forward references to a later producer retain that producer's hypotheses; they do not identify two independently chosen families. The index in Section 20 records the principal symbols and normalization changes.

The final assembly in Subsection 19.6 follows the order: selected finite family, full mesh and volume limits, common field quotients, all-character reconstruction and OPE, then the selected physical trace. The actual background estimates are supplied by the one-step and retained path construction of Subsection 11.21. Proposition 4.31 supplies the uniform all-period bounds for the calibrated insertions themselves. The final assembly verifies these applications on the same data; the reference map here is a guide to those proofs, rather than a substitute for them.
