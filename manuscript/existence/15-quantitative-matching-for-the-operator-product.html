15Quantitative matching for the operator product expansion

15.1The coefficient field at the last grouping scale

We connect the local-field representation to the degree-bounded coefficient field in the cutoff estimate. This step uses a projection estimate for one elementary insertion. We state its content explicitly and prove the extension to the whole grouped observable, including the normalization that determines its final bound.

Let \(m + 1\) and \(m\) be adjacent fine and coarse scales, with mesh sizes \(a_{m + 1} = a_m / L\) and \(L \geqslant 1\). Fix a degree \(d \geqslant 0\). For every fine elementary insertion \(M\) anchored at the origin, assume there is a coarse coefficient field \(Q_M\) of degree at most \(d\) with the prescribed locality and covariance format, satisfying

\(\displaystyle \text{dens} (Q_M) = L^d \mathcal{T} \text{dens} (M), \qquad |Q_M |_{\text{coeff}} \leqslant x \text{cost} (M), \qquad \text{rem} (Q_M) \leqslant x \text{cost} (M),\) (15.1)

where \(x \geqslant 0\). Here \(\text{rem} (Q) \leqslant B\) means that \(Q\) has a regular remainder with summable majorants of total mass \(R\) and a forced remainder with uniformly summable majorants of total mass \(F\) such that \(R + \kappa_m^{- 1} F \leqslant B\), where \(\kappa_m > 0\) is the forced-remainder allowance of this scale; it is the normalization of the forced part used in Subsection 7.12. The same \(\kappa_m\) is used for every field at this scale, in particular in the finite sums below. Assume, in addition, the singleton transition expansion of Subsection 7.8: the fine expansion cost for this insertion is at most \(2 \text{cost} (M)\), and its coarse response cost is at most \(x \text{cost} (M)\). The relation for the quoted profile has multiplier \(L^d\), consistently with the first identity in (15.1). These statements concern the actual background transition and its actual coefficient fields; they are not an abstract polynomial approximation assumption. Subsection 7.12 derives both statements from a localized expansion of one real insertion under the block integral: any \(Q_e\) satisfying (7.84) serves as \(x\), with \(\text{cost} (M)\) the fine mass \(m\) there, fine expansion cost \(2 m\) and coarse response cost \(Q_e m\). That derivation is conditional on the localized expansion and the tail, flag and smallness inputs stated in Subsection 7.12. Subsection 19.4 places the projection on the selected history with allowance \(X_m = \exp (\sqrt{\hat{\beta}_{m + 1}})\), for \(m \geqslant t_d\), where \(t_d\) is the reference level with \(d + 3 \leqslant J (t_d)\), for every cutoff \(N_0 > m\) and every period. The local expansion and its tail and flag estimates are given in Subsection 9.1; the principal and smallness bounds are given in Subsection 9.2.

15.1.1From one insertion to a grouped field

Suppose a grouped fine field has zero separately recorded constant and an elementary representation

\(\displaystyle (g, q) = \sum_j z_j (\text{ord} (M_j), \text{flag} (M_j)), \qquad \sum_j |z_j | \text{cost} (M_j) \leqslant A,\)

where the list is finite and every insertion is anchored at the origin. Choose \(Q_j\) by (15.1), and let \(Q = \sum_j z_j Q_j\). The sum means addition of coefficient vectors and of forced arrays, and concatenation of the regular remainder lists. Thus all locality and covariance conditions are preserved. The triangle inequality gives

\(\displaystyle |Q|_{\text{coeff}} \leqslant xA, \qquad \text{rem} (Q) \leqslant xA.\) (15.2)

For the regular remainder, multiply each majorant by \(|z_j |\) and sum its weighted costs. For the forced remainder, do the same for its uniformly summable majorants. These constructions prove the asserted bound without any cancellation of signed terms. Finite linearity of integration gives

\(\displaystyle \text{dens} (Q) = L^d \mathcal{T} \text{coin}_R (g, q) .\)

Indeed the linear density of \((g, q)\) is the sum of the elementary densities with coefficients \(z_j\), by the singleton formula (7.45). Each elementary density is required to be integrable under the fine Haar prior and in each transition slice, as in Subsection 7.8; the lawful representation and transition-kernel properties supply this in the application. Thus finite summation commutes with the transition integral. The empty list gives \(Q = 0\) and the same conclusion.

The singleton transition expansions concatenate in precisely the same way. Their fine cost is at most \(2 A\) and their coarse response cost at most \(xA\). Dividing the output field and its profile by \(L^d\) changes the latter cost to \(L^{- d} xA\), while the fine cost stays \(2 A\). The multiplier in the quoted-profile identity is now one. This is the normalization needed by the one-scale transport lemma.

Require also that the whole marked input density is integrable under the fine prior and the transition slices; this is the hypothesis needed below for taking polynomial coefficients and regrouping through integration. Suppose the other grouped fields are translated, coordinate transformed or conjugated copies of \((g, q)\), with origins that are embeddings of their coarse origins. The transition commutes with these motions, and the coefficient-field format is stable under them. Apply the same motion to \(L^{- d} Q\) in each cell. The quoted-profile expansions transform by relabelling the finite set of slots; their cost bounds remain \(2 A\) and \(L^{- d} xA\). The singleton coefficient of the integrated marked density is the linear density of the corresponding transformed copy of \(L^{- d} Q\): taking a coefficient commutes with integration, and the preceding density identity commutes with the motion. Thus the singleton-identification hypothesis of Subsection 7.8 holds for every copied cell, with one and the same coefficient field \(Q\).

If the incoming mark weights \(I_v\) satisfy

\(\displaystyle 2 I_v (2 A) \leqslant 1, \qquad I_v L^{- d} xA \leqslant 1,\) (15.3)

and the support, occupancy, numerical and new-weight hypotheses of that transport lemma hold, it follows that the integrated marked density has the required local representation with these coefficient fields and higher-array bound one. This conclusion retains the entire marked density, not only its singleton coefficients.

15.1.2The terminal bound and physical normalization

At the terminal grouping stage, let \(z > 0\) be any lower bound for the common grouped weight supplied by the grouping induction. Subsection 7.7 gives \(A = 4 / z\). Take \(x \geqslant 1\) and reduce the incoming weights to

\(\displaystyle I_v = \frac{z}{16 x} .\)

They do not exceed the available grouped weight. Reducing positive weights decreases every higher coefficient bound. Moreover

\(\displaystyle 2 I_v (2 A) = \frac{1}{x} \leqslant 1, \qquad I_v L^{- d} xA = \frac{L^{- d}}{4} \leqslant 1.\)

This verifies (15.3). If the outgoing weights obey \(0 < J_v \leqslant \delta_m I_v\), where \(\delta_m\) is the first reduction factor in the transport lemma, its final reweighting condition follows term by term: the product of the \(J_v\) is at most the product of \(\delta_m I_v\), and the allowed support exponential is at least one.

We have obtained a coarse field \(Q\) with both bounds at most \(4 x / z\), appearing in the density with prefactor \(L^{- d}\). Define the physically normalized coefficient field

\(\displaystyle Q' = a_{m + 1}^d Q.\)

Scaling multiplies its coefficient and remainder costs by the positive number \(a_{m + 1}^d\). Since

\(\displaystyle a_m^{- d} a_{m + 1}^d = L^{- d},\)

this changes neither the ordinary singleton nor its flagged profile in the density. It only rewrites them with the standard physical prefactor \(a_m^{- d}\). We obtain

\(\displaystyle |Q' |_{\text{coeff}}, \hspace{0.27em} \text{rem} (Q') \leqslant a_{m + 1}^d \frac{4 x}{z} .\) (15.4)

This is the terminal projection bound used in the cutoff accounting: take \(z\) equal to its available grouping budget and \(x\) equal to the elementary projection allowance.

Finally, regrouping and backwards integration are compatible. The regrouping map reads the coefficient of the complete old group at each new mark; it is a linear map between finite-dimensional polynomial spaces. It therefore commutes with every integrable transition. At the finest scale, reading a complete group in the marked birth density gives the product of its constituent observables. An induction over the intervening transitions shows that the terminal reaped density is the backwards-integrated density of that product. Consequently the coefficient field in (15.4) represents the intended cluster product through the same sequence of scales. This identifies the observable to which the cutoff bound applies; the elementary estimate (15.1) and the nonlinear transition estimate still require their own proofs.

15.2Decay of the matched reference error

The reference comparison uses a second cone argument. Here the coefficient vector is small compared with the remainder cost. The initial condition is imposed at the coarse comparison scale; the resulting cone propagates towards finer scales and bounds the error in the opposite direction.

Let \(\mu\) be the coefficient \(\ell^1\) norm, including the unit coordinate. At scale \(i\) write \(v_i\) for the coefficient vector of a signed comparison and \(r_i \geqslant 0\) for its remainder cost. These are the data of the actual difference, not the sum of the costs of its two summands. On an interval \(l \leqslant i \leqslant k\), assume \(v_l = 0\) and a one-step comparison of the following form. Put

\(\displaystyle q_i = L_i^{1 / 8}, \qquad \varepsilon_i = q_i^{- 3}, \qquad Y_i = \mu (D_{L_i} v_{i + 1}),\)

where \(D_L\) multiplies the coordinate of degree \(j \leqslant d\) by \(L^{d - j}\). An invertible coefficient map \(B_i\) and its inverse have norm at most \(K_i \geqslant 1\), and the transition satisfies

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \mu (v_i - B_i D_{L_i} v_{i + 1}) & \leqslant \varepsilon_i \{\mu (\text{core} \hspace{0.17em} v_{i + 1}) + r_{i + 1} \}, \hspace{3cm} \text{(15.5)} \\ r_i & \leqslant K_i \mu (\text{core} \hspace{0.17em} D_{L_i} v_{i + 1}) + \varepsilon_i r_{i + 1} . \end{array}\)

Here \(\text{core}\) deletes the unit coordinate. Let \(C_i \geqslant 1\) be the column allowances. The schedule is required to satisfy

\(\displaystyle 64 K_i^2 \leqslant C_i, \qquad q_i \geqslant 100000 M_i^6 (1 + C_{i + 1})^2, \qquad M_i \geqslant K_i \geqslant 1.\) (15.6)

Finally suppose the finest-scale cost obeys

\(\displaystyle r_k \leqslant w + C_k \{w + \mu (v_k)\}, \qquad w \geqslant 0.\) (15.7)

We prove

\(\displaystyle e^{(s_k - s_i) / 8} \{r_i + \mu (v_i)\} \leqslant 4 (1 + C_k) w \qquad (l \leqslant i \leqslant k),\) (15.8)

where \(s_{i + 1} - s_i = \log L_i\).

First, deleting the unit coordinate cannot increase \(\mu\), and \(L_i \geqslant 1\) gives \(\mu (v_{i + 1}) \leqslant Y_i\). Apply \(B_i^{- 1}\) to (15.5) and use the triangle inequality:

\(\displaystyle Y_i \leqslant K_i \{\mu (v_i) + \varepsilon_i (Y_i + r_{i + 1})\}, \qquad r_i \leqslant K_i Y_i + \varepsilon_i r_{i + 1} .\) (15.9)

Set \(A_i = 32 (1 + C_i)^2\). The schedule implies

\(\displaystyle A_i \geqslant 4 K_i^2, \quad 4 K_i \varepsilon_i \leqslant 1, \quad 4 K_i A_{i + 1} \varepsilon_i \leqslant 1, \quad (4 K_i^2 + 1) q_i \varepsilon_i \leqslant 1.\) (15.10)

Indeed (15.6) gives \(q_i \geqslant 128 K_i^2 (1 + C_{i + 1})^2 \geqslant 1\). Since \(\varepsilon_i \leqslant q_i^{- 1}\) and \(q_i \varepsilon_i \leqslant q_i^{- 1}\), the last three claims follow from \(4 K_i \leqslant q_i\), \(128 K_i (1 + C_{i + 1})^2 \leqslant q_i\), and \(4 K_i^2 + 1 \leqslant q_i\), respectively. The first follows from \(C_i \geqslant 64 K_i^2\) and \(C_i \geqslant 1\).

Suppose the cone inequality \(A_i \mu (v_i) \leqslant r_i\) holds. For this one step abbreviate \(K = K_i\), \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_i\), \(u = \mu (v_i)\), \(Y = Y_i\) and \(t = r_{i + 1}\). The second inequality in (15.9) yields

\(\displaystyle 4 Ku \leqslant Y + \varepsilon t,\)

because \(4 K^2 u \leqslant r_i \leqslant KY + \varepsilon t\) and \(K \geqslant 1\). Substitute this bound for \(u\) in the first inequality:

\(\displaystyle Y \leqslant \tfrac{1}{4} (Y + \varepsilon t) + K \varepsilon Y + K \varepsilon t \leqslant \tfrac{1}{2} Y + (K + \tfrac{1}{4}) \varepsilon t.\)

Thus \(Y \leqslant (2 K + 1 / 2) \varepsilon t \leqslant 4 K \varepsilon t\). Using (15.10), we obtain

\(\displaystyle A_{i + 1} \mu (v_{i + 1}) \leqslant A_{i + 1} Y \leqslant t, \qquad q_i r_i \leqslant q_i (4 K^2 + 1) \varepsilon t \leqslant t.\)

The first statement propagates the cone. The second gives the gain in remainder cost. At \(i = l\) the cone holds because \(v_l = 0\); induction therefore proves both inequalities at every step. Multiplying the latter gives

\(\displaystyle e^{(s_k - s_i) / 8} r_i \leqslant r_k .\)

At the finest scale the cone also implies \(2 C_k \mu (v_k) \leqslant r_k\). Insert this in (15.7):

\(\displaystyle r_k \leqslant (1 + C_k) w + \tfrac{1}{2} r_k, \qquad r_k \leqslant 2 (1 + C_k) w.\)

Since \(\mu (v_i) \leqslant r_i\) everywhere in the cone, this proves (15.8).

15.2.1Keeping the error affordable along the interval

The gain estimate is combined with a sequence of mark weights. Let \(\eta_i > 0\), put \(b_l = b > 0\) and \(b_{i + 1} = \eta_i b_i\). Let \(\rho_i > 0\) satisfy

\(\displaystyle \eta_i L_i^{d + 1} \rho_i \leqslant \rho_{i + 1} .\)

If \(E \geqslant 0\) and

\(\displaystyle b_l a_l^{- d} \frac{E}{e^{(s_k - s_l) / 8}} \leqslant \rho_l,\)

then the same estimate holds at every \(i \geqslant l\) up to \(k\):

\(\displaystyle b_i a_i^{- d} \frac{E}{e^{(s_k - s_i) / 8}} \leqslant \rho_i .\) (15.11)

In fact, the expression on the left is multiplied in one step by \(\eta_i L_i^d q_i \leqslant \eta_i L_i^{d + 1}\). This proves the assertion by induction. Applied with \(E = 4 (1 + C_k) w\), it bounds the weighted coefficient-plus-remainder cost of the matched difference at all intermediate scales. The construction of that signed difference and the proof of its one-step comparison (15.5) remain separate inputs: this numerical argument does not obtain them by subtracting two unrelated error estimates.

15.3Constructing the signed reference difference

The cone in Subsection 15.2 applies only to a comparison produced on a common background history. We describe that construction. Its principal feature is to remove the coefficient vector after each backward step, retaining the removed part in a reference label. This provides a family of exact comparisons, from which a single label is chosen at the coarsest scale.

Fix the degree \(d\) and scales \(l \leqslant k\). Assume the bank and column inequalities (15.6), with \(K_i, C_i \geqslant 1\), as in Subsection 15.2. At each scale \(i\), let \(\mathcal{P}_i (b)\) be the calibrated reference coefficient field with bare label \(b\) in the fixed coefficient space. Its coefficient vector is \(I_i b\), where \(I_i\) is a linear isomorphism. Assume its density, ordinary field and flagged profile depend linearly on \(b\), and its remainder has a nonnegative subadditive bound \(h_i (b)\) with

\(\displaystyle h_i (b) \leqslant C_i \mu (\text{core} \hspace{0.17em} I_i b) \leqslant C_i \mu (I_i b) .\) (15.12)

The reference densities satisfy the exact degree-\(d\) transition identity. Their coefficient and remainder data satisfy (15.5), with a common comparison map for each reference label, as supplied by the calibrated construction.

We also require a small transition estimate for a coefficient field \(S\) whose coefficient vector is zero. If its remainder is bounded by \(q \geqslant 0\), its backward transition has a representation \(U\) such that

\(\displaystyle \text{dens} (U) = L_i^d \mathcal{T}_i \text{dens} (S), \quad \mu (U.x) \leqslant \eta_i q, \quad \text{rem} (U) \leqslant \eta_i q, \quad (1 + 2 C_i) \eta_i \leqslant \varepsilon_i .\) (15.13)

Here \(\eta_i \geqslant 0\) and \(\varepsilon_i = L_i^{- 3 / 8}\). The fields and their sums retain the coefficient-field format and the integrability needed for the displayed transition identities. These are analytic inputs for the same history, including its calibrated reference fields.

15.3.1Removing the coefficient after each step

Start with a coefficient field \(Q\) at scale \(k\), with remainder bound \(t \geqslant 0\) and \(\mu (Q.x) + t \leqslant w\). Put

\(\displaystyle c_k = I_k^{- 1} Q.x, \qquad S_k = Q -\mathcal{P}_k (c_k), \qquad q_k = t + h_k (- c_k) .\)

The minus sign denotes a sum with the field \(\mathcal{P}_k (- c_k)\); its regular remainders are concatenated with those of \(Q\). Thus \(S_k .x = 0\) and its remainder is at most \(q_k\). For every bare label \(v\), define

\(\displaystyle D_k (v) = S_k +\mathcal{P}_k (c_k + v), \qquad r_k (v) = q_k + h_k (c_k + v) .\)

Linearity of the reference density and vector gives

\(\displaystyle D_k (v) .x = Q.x + I_k v, \qquad \text{dens} (D_k (v)) = \text{dens} (Q) + \text{dens} (\mathcal{P}_k (v)) .\) (15.14)

The cost, despite possible cancellation in this identity, is kept as the sum of the displayed nonnegative remainder bounds. Since \(t \leqslant w\) and \(\mu (Q.x) \leqslant w\),

\(\displaystyle r_k (v) \leqslant w + C_k \{w + \mu (D_k (v) .x)\} .\)

This is exactly the finest-scale condition (15.7).

Suppose \(c_{i + 1}, S_{i + 1}, q_{i + 1}\) have been constructed, with \(S_{i + 1} .x = 0\). Apply (15.13) to obtain \(U_i\), put \(e_i = \eta_i q_{i + 1}\), and set

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} b_i & = I_i^{- 1} U_i .x, & c_i & = c_{i + 1} + b_i,\\ S_i & = U_i -\mathcal{P}_i (b_i), & q_i & = e_i + h_i (- b_i) . \end{array}\)

Then \(S_i .x = 0\), and \(q_i\) bounds its remainder. Define \(D_i (v) = S_i +\mathcal{P}_i (c_i + v)\) and \(r_i (v) = q_i + h_i (c_i + v)\). Their densities satisfy

\(\displaystyle \text{dens} (D_i (v)) = \text{dens} (U_i) + \text{dens} (\mathcal{P}_i (c_{i + 1} + v)) = L_i^d \mathcal{T}_i \text{dens} (D_{i + 1} (v)) .\)

The first equality cancels the two reference terms with labels \(- b_i\) and \(b_i\) by linearity. The second uses both transition identities and linearity of the integrable transition. The same cancellation holds separately for ordinary fields and flagged profiles; it is not merely an equality after taking expectation.

We verify the signed one-step estimates. Fix \(v\) and write \(y = c_{i + 1} + v\). Then

\(\displaystyle D_{i + 1} (v) .x = I_{i + 1} y, \qquad D_i (v) .x = I_i y + U_i .x.\)

Use the comparison map belonging to the primary label \(y\). Its coefficient error is at most

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon_i \{\mu (\text{core} \hspace{0.17em} I_{i + 1} y) + h_{i + 1} (y)\} .\)

The added vector \(U_i .x\) costs at most \(\eta_i q_{i + 1} \leqslant \varepsilon_i q_{i + 1}\). Thus the coefficient inequality in (15.5) holds for the actual signed vectors, with cost \(r_{i + 1} (v)\).

For the remainder, (15.12) gives \(h_i (\pm b_i) \leqslant C_i e_i\). Subadditivity yields

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} r_i (v) & = e_i + h_i (- b_i) + h_i (y + b_i)\\ & \leqslant h_i (y) + (1 + 2 C_i) e_i\\ & \leqslant K_i \mu (\text{core} \hspace{0.17em} D_{L_i} I_{i + 1} y) + \varepsilon_i \{h_{i + 1} (y) + q_{i + 1} \} . \end{array}\)

This is the other inequality of (15.5). In particular it is a bound for the very same presentation that supplies the exact density identity. No subtraction of unsigned remainder bounds is used.

Now choose the single label \(v = - c_l\) and write \(b = c_l\). The coefficient vector of \(D_l (- b)\) vanishes. The construction therefore meets every hypothesis of Subsection 15.2, including its terminal condition. Writing \(Z_i = D_i (- b)\) and \(r_i = r_i (- b)\) gives

\(\displaystyle \text{dens} (Z_k) = \text{dens} (Q) - \text{dens} (\mathcal{P}_k (b)), \qquad r_i + \mu (Z_i .x) \leqslant \frac{4 (1 + C_k) w}{e^{(s_k - s_i) / 8}} .\) (15.15)

If \(E \geqslant 4 (1 + C_k) w\), then \(w \leqslant E\) and at \(i = k\)

\(\displaystyle \mu (I_k b) = \mu (Q.x - Z_k .x) \leqslant w + E \leqslant 2 E.\)

This is the quota bound for the matched reference label.

15.3.2The singleton transition expansion is retained

To use this difference inside a marked density, more than its coefficient and remainder estimates is needed. Suppose the zero-vector transition in (15.13) comes with the singleton expansion of Subsection 7.8, of fine cost \(2 q_{i + 1}\) and coarse response cost \(e_i\). Suppose the primary transition for label \(y\) has fine cost at most

\(\displaystyle \text{orb} (d) \mu (I_{i + 1} y) (\psi_i + 2 C_{i + 1}),\)

and coarse response cost \(h_i (y)\). Both expansions must use the same quoted-profile map; their finite sets of slots can then be united. Their sum has the ordinary and flagged profiles of \(D_{i + 1} (v)\) and the coarse profile of \(D_i (v)\), by the cancellations just proved. Its fine cost is at most

\(\displaystyle \text{orb} (d) \mu (D_{i + 1} (v) .x) (\psi_i + 2 C_{i + 1}) + 2 r_{i + 1} (v) .\)

Its response cost \(e_i + h_i (y)\) is at most \(r_i (v)\), because

\(\displaystyle h_i (y) = h_i (- b_i + (y + b_i)) \leqslant h_i (- b_i) + h_i (y + b_i) .\)

Thus the same signed difference carries the singleton transition expansions needed for the marked transport argument. Combining (15.15) with (15.11) makes these expansions affordable once the prescribed one-step allowances are imposed. The production of the small zero-vector transition and the calibrated primary transition bounds remains an analytic dependency of this construction.

15.4From a coefficient field to a finite-cutoff reference observable

The signed comparison must control the original finite-cutoff observable, not only its coefficient vector. This requires a simultaneous marked argument. We explain the finite-dimensional matching and the exact replicated difference used in that argument.

Fix a degree \(d\) and three depths \(J \leqslant m \leqslant k < N\) with \(\text{ref} (d) \leqslant m\) and \(B_m^2 \leqslant a_J / a_m\), where \(B_m\) is the purchased covering radius. We call \(m\) an opened depth for \(J\) when \(J \leqslant m\) and this last inequality hold. All paths below use the same finest scale \(N\) and the same history. The depth \(J\) is the centered depth: the replicated cells are the \(M\) cells of the centered depth-\(J\) tiling, and the root test \(\mathcal{R}\) below is taken over them. The depth \(m \geqslant J\) is the opening depth, at which the common weight evolution of the marked transport starts; \(k\) is the matching depth. The opening condition is distinct from the room condition imposed below. Let \(Q\) represent a continuous finite-cutoff observable \(f\) at scale \(k\) through the full marked local representation. Suppose its coefficient-plus-remainder bound is at most \(c > 0\), and put

\(\displaystyle E \geqslant 4 (1 + C_k) c, \qquad G = e^{(s_k - s_m) / 8}, \qquad w > 0.\)

Assume the signed transition hypotheses of Subsection 15.3, the one-scale marked transport hypotheses of Subsection 7.8, and the necessary cell separation and chamber locality at each scale. The common affordability condition is

\(\displaystyle 32 (1 + C_k) wa_m^{- d} E / G \leqslant \theta_m / 2.\) (15.16)

Here \(\theta_i\) are the single-source allowances; their growth condition is the one used in (15.11). The coefficient paths must also satisfy the corresponding one-step singleton cost conditions there and in the marked transport lemma. No availability of these analytic hypotheses is inferred from (15.16) alone.

15.4.1An approximate coordinate basis

Apply the signed reference construction first to \(Q\). It gives a label \(b\) with \(\mu (I_k b) \leqslant 2 E\) and a path representing \(Q -\mathcal{P}_k (b)\). Its opening bound is \(2 w\); the required initial affordability follows from (15.16) because \(2 \leqslant 32 (1 + C_k)\).

For each coefficient coordinate \(j\), apply the same construction to the primary field with vector \(e_j\) and zero remainder. Use opening bound \(8 Ew\) and coefficient allowance \(4 (1 + C_k)\). Its affordability expression is exactly the left side of (15.16). Obtain labels \(b_j\). The cone estimate at \(k\), now with terminal bound \(1\), gives

\(\displaystyle 32 (1 + C_k)^2 \mu (e_j - I_k b_j) \leqslant 2 (1 + C_k), \qquad \mu (e_j - I_k b_j) \leqslant \tfrac{1}{4} .\)

Define \(Az = \sum_j z_j I_k b_j\). Since \(\mu\) is the coefficient \(\ell^1\) norm,

\(\displaystyle \mu (z - Az) \leqslant \sum_j |z_j | \mu (e_j - I_k b_j) \leqslant \tfrac{1}{4} \mu (z) .\)

It follows that \(\mu (z) \leqslant 2 \mu (Az)\). Thus \(A\) is injective and, in this finite-dimensional space, surjective. Solve \(As = I_k b\). Injectivity of \(I_k\) gives

\(\displaystyle b = \sum_j s_j b_j, \qquad \sum_j |s_j | \leqslant 2 \mu (I_k b) \leqslant 4 E.\) (15.17)

No uniform condition number of an arbitrarily chosen reference basis is being assumed; the bound comes from the explicitly constructed approximate coordinate vectors.

Let \(\ell_j\) be the physically normalized primary field for \(e_j\) at scale \(k\), with its local mask, and set

\(\displaystyle \ell = - \sum_j s_j \ell_j, r =\mathcal{B}_{k, N} \ell,\)

where \(\mathcal{B}_{k, N}\) is the backward observable transfer to the finest lattice. All \(\ell_j\) lie in the same fixed stencil window, of some fixed radius \(R\). Hence \(\ell\) does too. We assume the room condition of Subsection 13.11 for the centered depth \(J\), the matching depth \(k\) and this radius, namely \(2 (a_k R + T_k) < a_J\) with \(T_k = 30 a_k (h_k + 5)\); it places the propagated window, and so \(r\), inside the centered \(J\)-chamber at every depth between \(k\) and \(N\). This common window is essential for the replicated transfer identity below.

15.4.2The full replicated difference

We spell out that identity, keeping every mixed term. Write \(\mathcal{T}_{N, k}\) for the forward density transfer, adjoint to \(\mathcal{B}_{k, N}\) under the Haar priors. For copied fine observables \(O_i\) and copied coarse observables \(F_i\), the coefficient of a mark set \(S\) after introducing the coarse sources is

\(\displaystyle \sum_{A \subseteq S} \mathcal{T}_{N, k} \left( \rho_N \prod_{i \in S \setminus A} (- O_i) \right) \prod_{i \in A} F_i .\) (15.18)

Here \(\rho_N\) is the unnormalized Wilson density. The same forward transfer is used for the whole fine product in each summand. It has not been replaced by a product of transferred factors.

For coarse functions in the common local window, the room condition ensures that backward transfer respects their copied product: \(\mathcal{B}_{k, N} (\prod_{i \in A} F_i)\) is the product of the corresponding copies of \(\mathcal{B}_{k, N} F\). This is (13.35), proved under exactly that condition by disjointness of the kernel read sets of different chambers at every intervening transition; it is not a consequence of adjointness or of normalization alone. Using adjointness in each summand of (15.18), its integral is therefore

\(\displaystyle \int \rho_N \prod_{i \in S} (\mathcal{B}_{k, N} F_i - O_i) \hspace{0.17em} dU.\)

This follows by the finite subset expansion of the product. The interchange is justified by bounded local observables, integrable fine densities and the transition-slice bounds. Further forward transfers leave this integral unchanged. Thus the coefficient of the complete mark set is the Wilson partition function times the replicated difference moment.

The singleton bounds used here follow from the priced coefficient basis and the remainder bounds. Explicitly, require the masked basis evaluation bound with column constant \(C_m \geqslant 1\) and remainder divisor at most one. If a coefficient field has vector \(q\) and remainder cost \(t\), the triangle inequality gives \(|g| \leqslant (1 + C_m) \mu (q) + t\) before physical normalization. The sum of the norms of its flagged profiles is at most \(t\): sum the absolutely summable forced majorants and use that every support weight is at least one. The same argument bounds the ordinary remainder by \(t\); the primary term is bounded by \((1 + C_m) \mu (q)\) using the priced basis. If \(va_m^{- d} (t + \mu (q)) \leqslant \theta_m\) and \(C_m \theta_m \leqslant 1\), these bounds give normalized singleton allowances \(2\) and \(1\). Translations and conjugation preserve them. This is the sharper bound for the matched coefficient paths; it is separate from the general centered-field estimate.

Suppose the marked transport has reached the opening depth \(m\) with common mark weight \(v > 0\), higher-array bound one, and singleton bounds

\(\displaystyle v |g_i (U) | \leqslant 2, \qquad v \sum_X |q_i (X, U) | \leqslant 1.\)

The integrated marked coefficient estimate (4.10), applied at the opening depth with the \(M\) centered \(J\)-cells as marked copies and with singleton allowances \(2\) and \(1\) (so \(x = 2\), \(y = 1\), \(f = 1\) and \(x + y + 2 f = 5\)), then bounds the complete moment by

\(\displaystyle v^M | \text{moment} | \leqslant \exp (| \Lambda_m |\mathcal{V}_m + 5 M) .\)

Here \(| \Lambda_m |\) is the number of depth-\(m\) sites and \(\mathcal{V}_m\) is the per-site treasury bound \(t_m\) of the opening depth, which bounds the extensive bracket of (4.10) by the finite-volume treasury identity, as in Subsection 4.2. Since each centered \(J\)-cell contains \((a_J / a_m)^4\) depth-\(m\) sites, \(| \Lambda_m | = M (a_J / a_m)^4\). Write

\(\displaystyle S_0 = \exp (| \Lambda_m |\mathcal{V}_m / M + 5) = \exp ((a_J / a_m)^4 t_m + 5),\)

the cell bound. Taking the \(M\)th root gives the centered root bound \(S_0 / v\). This is an estimate for the complete replicated difference; no estimate on its individual mixed summands is substituted.

15.4.3Combining the comparison paths

The one-scale transport is stable under affordable finite linear combinations of its single-source paths. Explicitly, if path \(j\) has positive opening bound \(v_j\) and coefficient \(a_j\), the condition

\(\displaystyle \sum_j w |a_j | / v_j \leqslant 1\) (15.19)

preserves every common normalized cost bound. All paths use the same multiplicative weight evolution: at scale \(i\) the opening bounds \(v_j\) and \(w\) are multiplied by the same positive factor. Thus the fractions in (15.19) are unchanged at each intermediate scale. To verify the cost bound, if \(v_j \sum_X |F_{j, X} | \leqslant C\), then

\(\displaystyle w \sum_X \left| \sum_j a_j F_{j, X} \right| \leqslant \sum_j \frac{w |a_j |}{v_j} v_j \sum_X |F_{j, X} | \leqslant C.\)

The same calculation applies to singleton values, elementary transition costs and response majorants. Sum the dominated indexed arrays using their summed majorants. The transition identities are linear, so remain exact. Applying the marked transport successively proves the claim for the whole interval.

Use the base difference path with coefficient \(1\) and bound \(2 w\), and the coordinate paths with coefficients \(- s_j\) and bound \(8 Ew\). Equation (15.17) gives

\(\displaystyle \frac{w}{2 w} + \sum_j \frac{w |s_j |}{8 Ew} \leqslant 1.\)

At scale \(k\), the reference terms cancel separately in the ordinary and flagged profiles, because \(- b + \sum_j (- s_j) (- b_j) = 0\). For a conjugating copy, use the conjugate of every coefficient \(a_j\) in the linear combination; for an ordinary copy use \(a_j\) itself. Their absolute values coincide, so the cost proof is unchanged. The cancellation in a conjugating copy is the conjugate of the displayed identity. Thus cancellation holds for every copied profile. The resulting ordinary field is the one for \(Q\) plus \(\ell\); the flagged profile is exactly the one for \(Q\). Introducing \(\ell\) into the marked representation of \(f\) is multiplication by its squarefree source exponential. The identity (15.18), with \(O = - f\) and \(F = \ell\), therefore identifies the complete coefficient with the replicated moment of \(f + r\). The transport and complete-moment estimate give

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R} (f + r) \leqslant S_0 / w,\)

where \(\mathcal{R}\) is the centered root seminorm.

For each coordinate path the same argument, starting from the terminal reference field and inserting \(\ell_j\) at scale \(k\), gives

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R} (\mathcal{B}_{k, N} \ell_j -\mathcal{O}(b_j)) \leqslant S_0 / (8 Ew) .\)

Here \(\mathcal{O} (b_j)\) denotes the fine observable attached to that bare label by the calibrated terminal identification. This application requires the terminal reference representation and its singleton profile identity on the same history. They remain among the analytic inputs to the matched construction. The chamber seminorm inequality, backward-transfer linearity and (15.17) imply

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R} (- r -\mathcal{O}(b)) \leqslant 4 E \frac{S_0}{8 Ew} = \frac{S_0}{2 w} .\)

Finally \(f -\mathcal{O} (b) = (f + r) + (- r -\mathcal{O}(b))\) lies in the common chamber class, so its centered root is at most \(2 S_0 / w\). The matched reference observable has quota at most \(2 E\), as proved above. These are exactly the two bounds used in the cutoff matching argument.

15.5Capturing a cluster and paying for a cutoff match

The local match used in Subsection 15.6 is constructed before any continuum limit. One follows a product of local observables down through the scales, merging groups whenever their parent sites cease to be separated. The bookkeeping must be independent of the number of replicated cells. Otherwise it would be unusable in the centered root estimate. We give the geometric selection and the scale accounting explicitly, and state where the remaining local RG estimates enter.

15.5.1Regrouping without a growing arity cost

Let a cluster have \(h \geqslant 1\) marked sites and distinguish one of its marks. At a given scale, call sites separated if their periodic maximum-coordinate distance is at least \(B^2\), where \(B \geqslant 1\) is the purchased support radius. Among the subsets containing the distinguished mark choose one of maximal cardinality with separated sites. Every omitted mark lies at distance less than \(B^2\) from a chosen mark: otherwise it could be added. Assign each omitted mark to one such representative, and assign each chosen mark to itself. This defines a partition with a chosen representative in every part. If the original sites were not separated, the number of parts strictly decreases.

Copies in different centered cells have the same relative geometry, up to translations and signed coordinate transformations, which preserve distance. Use the same partition in each copy. Cross-cell separation and separation of the chosen representatives in the home cell then give separation of all representatives. In particular the number of regroupings is at most \(h - 1\), regardless of how many cells are copied.

The cost of connecting a mark to its representative can be charged one scale before their parents meet. To check the geometry, let the block ratio be \(L\), let \(K\) be the parent's support radius and \(B\) the child's, and let \(x, y\) be two child sites whose parents have distance less than \(K^2\). Each child is at distance less than \(L / 2\) from the embedded parent. The triangle inequality gives

\(\displaystyle \text{dist} (x, y) < L (K^2 + 1) \leqslant L (K + B + 1)^2 = : c.\)

A pair at distance at most \(cB\) can be covered by at most \((2 c + 1)^4\) \(B\)-blocks: both points lie in the radius-\(cB\) halo of one point, which is tiled by at most \(2 c + 1\) blocks per coordinate. Since \(B \geqslant 1\), the preceding pair therefore has covering number at most

\(\displaystyle (2 c + 1)^4 \leqslant 16 (1 + c)^4 .\) (15.20)

This is the halo charged to the individual grouping operation. It does not assert that the entire cluster lies in a single small halo at every intervening scale.

Here are the two local RG estimates used in the induction. A group has a positive weight \(W\) measuring the allowed size of its marked representation. If its parents stay separated, descent through a block of ratio \(L\) changes its weight to \(W / L^4\). If \(p\) groups merge into \(q < p\) groups at a scale with connecting-cover bound \(\ell\), their new weights can be taken to be

\(\displaystyle \tilde{W}_A = \frac{1}{8} \prod_{r \in A} (\lambda W_r), \qquad \lambda = \frac{\delta^2}{h} e^{- (\nu + 1 / 2) \ell} .\) (15.21)

Here \(\nu\) is the purchased support exponent and \(\delta > 0\) the local smallness parameter. The connecting-cover bound \(\ell\) is the quantity written \(k\) in Subsection 7.5, where \(\ell\) denotes instead the site occupancy. Each factor \(\lambda W_r\) is the new weight \(\delta^2 W_r e^{- (\nu + 1 / 2) k} / h\) of the single old mark \(r\), and their product over \(r \in A\) is the numerator computed at the end of that section, so that \(8 \tilde{W}_A = \prod_{r \in A} (\lambda W_r)\). These statements also retain the exact marked polynomial after regrouping, its covariance between copies, its integrability, and its permitted local representation. The analytic proofs of these two local representation estimates remain construction inputs. In particular their weight rules alone are not a proof that a marked representation exists.

Choose one \(0 < \alpha \leqslant 1\) no larger than every grouping factor \(\lambda\) that will be needed. If \(j\) is the initial scale, \(i\) the current scale and \(s \leqslant h\) the current number of groups, put

\(\displaystyle C (i, s) = \left( \frac{a_j}{a_i} \right)^{4 h} (\alpha / 8)^{h (h - s)} \prod_{r = 1}^h W_r^{\text{initial}} .\)

We claim that the product of the current group weights is at least \(C (i, s)\). This is equality initially. For an ordinary descent,

\(\displaystyle C (i, s) = L_i^{- 4 h} C (i + 1, s) \leqslant L_i^{- 4 s} \prod_r W_r,\)

since \(s \leqslant h\) and \(L_i \geqslant 1\). For a genuine grouping, \(q < p \leqslant h\) and \(\alpha / 8 \leqslant 1\) imply

\(\displaystyle C (i, q) \leqslant C (i, p) (\alpha / 8)^h .\)

Thus

\(\displaystyle 8^q C (i, q) \leqslant \alpha^h C (i, p) \leqslant \lambda^p \prod_r W_r .\)

We used \(8^q \leqslant 8^h\) and \(\alpha^h \leqslant \alpha^p \leqslant \lambda^p\). The last expression is exactly the product of the new numerators in (15.21), because the groups partition the old marks. This proves the induction after the subsequent descent as well. The regrouping of marked polynomials commutes with descent: it is a fixed linear map on a finite coefficient space, so it commutes with integration against the transition kernel. Its composition law is the composition law of the partitions. These observations keep the induction on the original exact product.

The initial single-field estimates give weights \(W_r^{\text{initial}} = \delta_j \eta_j p_r (j) / 8\), where \(p_r (j) > 0\) is the paid weight of the \(r\)th field. At the final parent scale \(m + 1\) merge all remaining groups. Their number \(s\) is at most \(h\), and the last grouping factor is also at least \(\alpha\). The terminal merge uses the weight \(\lambda^s \prod_r W_r\) without the factor \(1 / 8\) reserved at intermediate regroupings; it supplies the final local projection directly. Since \(\alpha^h \leqslant \lambda^s\) and \(C (m + 1, 0) \leqslant C (m + 1, s)\), the resulting weight is at least

\(\displaystyle X = \alpha^h \left( \frac{a_j}{a_{m + 1}} \right)^{4 h} (\alpha / 8)^{h^2} (\delta_j \eta_j / 8)^h \prod_{r = 1}^h p_r (j) .\) (15.22)

The final local projection estimate produces a degree-\(q\) coefficient whose coefficient-size and remainder norm are each bounded by

\(\displaystyle P = a_{m + 1}^q \hspace{0.17em} \mathcal{B}_m \frac{4}{X} .\) (15.23)

Here \(\mathcal{B}_m\) is the purchased local projection bound. This projection estimate, together with the marked-representation estimates above, is still an analytic input. The preceding argument proves the precise scale cost it must be given; no factor depends on the number of copies.

15.5.2A single subpower allowance for all intermediate levels

The halo estimate ensures that each grouping factor has zero logarithmic rate. Explicitly the purchased bound is \((\nu_{i + 1} + 1) \ell_i \leqslant 16 \sqrt{\hat{\beta}_i}\), while \(\sqrt{\hat{\beta}_i} / s_i \to 0\) and \(\log \delta_{i + 1} = o (s_i)\). Taking logarithms of (15.21) therefore gives \(\log \lambda_i = o (s_i)\) for fixed \(h\). These are schedule and running-coupling estimates; their production is a separate input. The cover inequality is the form in which condition (19.12) of Subsection 19.3 is used: by (15.20), with parent and child radii \(\lceil (\log x)^2 \rceil\) and \(\lceil (\log y)^2 \rceil\) for the hatted couplings \(x, y\) of the transition, the connecting cover is at most \(16 H_L (x, y)\), and (19.12) with \(A = 101 = \nu + 1\) gives \((\nu + 1) \ell_i \leqslant 16 \sqrt{x}\). At the transition from level \(i\) the hatted couplings are \(x = \hat{\beta}_i\) and \(y = \hat{\beta}_{i + 1}\), as displayed in Subsection 19.3. Since \(\hat{\beta}_i\) is an affine function of \(s_i\) on the selected scale sequence (Subsection 19.2), \(\sqrt{\hat{\beta}_i} / s_i \to 0\).

This pointwise-in-scale fact also gives a uniform allowance for all previous scales. If \(u_i > 0\) and \(\log u_i = o (s_i)\) for an increasing \(s_i \to \infty\), set \(\alpha_j = \min (1, u_0, \ldots, u_j)\). Then \(\alpha_j \leqslant 1\), \(\alpha_j \leqslant u_i\) for \(i \leqslant j\), and \(\log \alpha_j = o (s_j)\). Indeed, for any \(\epsilon > 0\), eventually \(u_i \geqslant e^{- \epsilon s_i} \geqslant e^{- \epsilon s_j}\) for \(i \leqslant j\) in the tail. 15.5.4Keeping the match in the continuum limit

For fixed intermediate levels and a fixed cluster on the \(j\)-grid, require

\(\displaystyle 8 \max_r |x_r | < a_{m + 1}, \qquad |x_r - x_s | > 2 a_j B_j^2 \quad (r \ne s) .\)

Cross-cell separation persists during every intervening descent. The initial room condition is \((B_{m + 1}^2 + 1) a_{m + 1} \leqslant a_k\), which follows eventually from subpower \(B_{m + 1}\) and \(a > b\). At an intermediate level \(i \geqslant m + 1\), put \(L = a_{m + 1} / a_i\). Distinct parent cell centers at level \(m + 1\) have lattice separation at least \(a_k / a_{m + 1} \geqslant B_{m + 1}^2 + 1\). Their embeddings at level \(i\) therefore have separation at least \(L (B_{m + 1}^2 + 1)\). The children each deviate from their embedded parent by less than \(L / 2\). The triangle inequality leaves separation greater than \(LB_{m + 1}^2\). The purchased support propagation bound \(B_i^2 \leqslant LB_{m + 1}^2\) then gives the required separation at level \(i\). This propagation bound is one of the local RG inputs retained above. The argument applies simultaneously to every pair of marks belonging to different copied cells.

For sufficiently large periods there is no wrapping in this finite configuration. Its points have the required periodic separation and have common parent zero at level \(m + 1\): each is inside half the parent block by the first bound and the maximum-coordinate estimate. For sufficiently fine cutoff \(N\), the fixed stencils of the cluster fields and of every degree-\(q\) field fit in the centered positive chamber. The cutoff comparison can consequently be applied on the ordered tail, first in period and then in \(N\).

Choose its labels \(Q_{N, p}\) on that tail and set them to zero elsewhere. Their quotas are bounded by the same \(V\). Equation (7.29) bounds their reference norms by a constant depending on the fixed matching level, so finite-dimensional compactness gives a convergent family on a finer nontrivial filter. Every eventual discrepancy and quota bound persists on that same filter. Subsection 7.3 converts the discrepancy bound to all character phases, and Subsection 7.4 gives the physical low-degree distances and the whole-label bound. These are all statements about the same limiting label \(Q\).

Finally the geometric requirements hold for the grid clusters of Subsection 15.6. If their scale is \(x\) with \(a < x < c\), their radius has rate \(- x < - a\), so they fit strictly inside the \(m + 1\) block. Their pairwise separation is eventually larger than \(e^{- (x + \epsilon) t_n}\), while \(a_j B_j^2\) has rate \(- c\) because \(B_j\) is subpower. Choosing \(x + \epsilon < c\) proves the separation condition. 15.6From a matched local field to the raw OPE

The raw comparison in Input 15.3 is obtained in three steps. At a fine lattice configuration the product is replaced by a single local field of a sufficiently high degree. The finite tests defining the prescription then identify its low-degree coefficients. Finally, derivatives with respect to the cluster positions extend the estimate from the lattice to every separated configuration. The last step uses the filtration: taking a derivative increases the field degree by one.

We use upper rates as in Subsection 15.7, with any sequence \(t_n \to \infty\). A cluster \(X_n = (x_{i, n})_{i \in I}\) is at scale \(x > 0\) if \(\max_i |x_{i, n} |\) has upper rate \(- x\) and, for every \(\delta > 0\),

\(\displaystyle |x_{i, n} - x_{j, n} | > e^{- (x + \delta) t_n} \quad (i \ne j) \quad \text{eventually} .\)

These conditions tolerate factors with zero logarithmic rate. In particular, they include every cluster of radius \(Mr_n\) and separation \(\eta r_n\), where \(t_n = - \log r_n\). Spectators in this section remain a fixed positive distance from the origin and one another, although their positions and phases can vary with \(n\).

15.6.1Intermediate scales and generic grids

Write \(a_k\) for the mesh and \(s_k = \log (1 / a_k)\) for its logarithmic scale. The following elementary consequence of the mesh schedule will be useful. Suppose \(s_k\) is strictly increasing, tends to infinity, and \(s_{k + 1} / s_k \to 1\). Then for every \(d > 0\) and every \(t_n \to \infty\) there are integers \(j_n \to \infty\) such that \(s_{j_n} / t_n \to d\). Indeed choose the first \(j_n\) with \(s_{j_n} > dt_n\). Eventually \(j_n > 0\), and

\(\displaystyle s_{j_n - 1} \leqslant dt_n < s_{j_n}, \qquad 1 < \frac{s_{j_n}}{dt_n} \leqslant \frac{s_{j_n}}{s_{j_n - 1}} \longrightarrow 1.\)

The zero-rate variant follows by replacing \(dt_n\) with \(\sqrt{t_n}\). The gentle-mesh condition used in the construction gives exactly the ratio property, since \(s_{k + 1} - s_k = \log L_k = o (s_k)\). The proof here uses that condition; its production for the chosen trajectory remains part of the construction estimates.

A configuration is coordinate-generic if, for each of the four coordinates separately, the coordinates of its distinct points are pairwise different. Such configurations can approximate arbitrary ones at the mesh scale with a bound independent of their location. To see this, enumerate \(I\) by \(0, \ldots, N - 1\), set \(K = N + 1\), and for mesh \(h > 0\) put

\(\displaystyle y_{i, \mu} = h \left( K \left\lfloor \frac{x_{i, \mu}}{hK} \right\rfloor + i \right), \qquad \mu = 1, \ldots, 4.\)

Different point indices have different residues modulo \(K\), so each coordinate is injective. The floor inequality and \(0 \leqslant i < K\) give \(|y_{i, \mu} - x_{i, \mu} | \leqslant hK\), hence

\(\displaystyle \max_i |y_i - x_i | \leqslant 2 Kh.\) (15.28)

This is the empty-exterior version of the grid construction; no claim is made that it preserves coordinate distances from fixed exterior points. Only ordinary Euclidean separation of the full configuration is needed below.

If \(h_n = e^{- dt_n + o (t_n)}\) with \(d > x\), the rounded cluster and every configuration in the closed ball

\(\displaystyle B_n = \{Z : \|Z - X_n \| \leqslant \|Y_n - X_n \|\}\)

remain at scale \(x\), using the maximum of the Euclidean norms on the configuration space. Here is the separation check. Given \(\delta > 0\), choose \(0 < \epsilon < \delta\) with \(x + \epsilon < d\) and use the original separation at exponent \(x + \epsilon\). The displacement has strictly smaller exponential order, so eventually twice its norm is at most half that separation. Thus the perturbed separation is at least \(\tfrac{1}{2} e^{- (x + \epsilon) t_n} > e^{- (x + \delta) t_n}\). The radius bound follows from the triangle inequality. Fixed exterior clearances also persist, because these clusters tend to the origin.

15.6.3Interpolation by descending through the grading

We give the interpolation argument explicitly. Suppose functions \(h_n (p, X)\) are indexed by finite field lists of the same length, with integer total degree \(w (p)\). Assume that for every scale-\(x\) sequence:

  1. \(h_n (p, X_n)\) has a rough upper rate \(xw (p) + c\);

  2. on every coordinate-generic grid sequence of logarithmic mesh rate \(d > x\), it has upper rate \(dw (p) + e\);

  3. it is eventually differentiable along each such sequence, with identity

    \(\displaystyle \partial_{i, \mu} h_n (p, X_n) = h_n (p', X_n) .\)

    Here \(p'\) is obtained by differentiating the corresponding field, so \(w (p') = w (p) + 1\).

The constants \(c, e\) are independent of \(p\); they may depend on fixed truncation and exterior data. Then at every scale-\(x\) sequence the improved upper rate is \(dw (p) + e\).

Choose an integer \(N \geqslant 0\) with \((d - x) N \geqslant c - e\). We prove by induction on \(k \geqslant 0\) that the improved rate holds whenever \(N \leqslant w (p) + k\). For \(k = 0\) the rough rate is already no larger than the improved one. For the induction step round \(X_n\) to \(Y_n\) by (15.28). Every choice \(Z_n \in B_n\) stays at scale \(x\). The induction hypothesis applied to the differentiated lists shows that each coordinate derivative at \(Z_n\) has upper rate \(d (w (p) + 1) + e\). In the fixed finite-dimensional configuration space,

\(\displaystyle \|Dh_n (p, Z_n)\| \leqslant \sum_{i, \mu} | \partial_{i, \mu} h_n (p, Z_n) |,\)

so the derivative norm has that rate too.

These bounds and differentiability hold eventually uniformly on \(B_n\). For any specified positive slack, failure would give infinitely many indices with a bad point; choosing a bad point there and the center elsewhere produces a sequence \(Z_n \in B_n\) contradicting the corresponding eventual assertions. The mean-value inequality on the convex ball thus gives

\(\displaystyle |h_n (p, Y_n) - h_n (p, X_n) | \leqslant \sup_{Z \in B_n} \|Dh_n (p, Z)\| \hspace{0.17em} \|Y_n - X_n \| .\)

The displacement has upper rate \(- d\), so the difference has upper rate \(dw (p) + e\). The grid value has the same rate, which proves the induction step. Taking \(k = N\) covers every field list. Only finitely many derivative levels are used for any one application, although the assumptions must be available at every finite degree.

For clarity, the mean-value inequality here needs no continuity of the derivative. On each segment in \(B_n\), differentiability implies continuity, and the scalar mean-value theorem applied to real projections of the complex-valued function bounds its increment by the derivative bound times the segment length. The finite-dimensional operator estimate above follows by expanding a vector in its coordinate basis and applying the triangle inequality.

15.6.4Removing the intermediate-scale losses

The differentiability identity (3) needed for this interpolation is the slot derivative identity (5.21): one grade-raising choice of raw derivative representative \(\delta_j v\) for every raw label, whose slot identity holds at distinct points with arbitrary finite lists of exterior insertions, and, because \(h_n\) is a phase discrepancy, in every phase table used here, with the chosen representatives having the indicated values in that phase. Subsection 5.4 assumes the same identity and does not produce it; it is a named input of the present section. The identities persist under finite linear combinations of detector tests and under subtraction of the finite raw truncation. At a separated configuration its neighborhood remains separated, so the totalized values away from that neighborhood play no role in differentiation.

Suppose the rough separated correlation estimates give upper rate \(xw (p)\) for the cluster with fixed-clearance spectators, uniformly in the phase. They give the same rate for each raw coefficient, since its detector list is fixed and finite. A finite truncation also has rough rate \(xw (p)\): its coefficient has that rate and each fixed single-field spectator insertion has rate zero. Therefore both the raw coefficient and the raw discrepancy satisfy the rough interpolation bound with \(c = 0\).

For a coefficient of degree \(m\), the grid bound just proved is

\(\displaystyle dw (p) + 4 |I| (d - a) - ma.\)

Use interpolation with \(e = 4 |I| (d - a) - ma\) to obtain this bound at all scale-\(x\) clusters. For a remainder through degree \(D\), use instead \(e = 4 |I| (d - a) - (D + 1) a\). These offsets do not change when a cluster field is differentiated, as required by the induction.

Now set \(a = x - \epsilon\), \(d = x + \epsilon\), and \(b = a / 2\), with \(0 < \epsilon < x\). The mesh schedule supplies all three intermediate levels. The resulting coefficient upper rate is

\(\displaystyle (w (p) - m) x + \epsilon (w (p) + 8| I| + m),\)

and the remainder upper rate is

\(\displaystyle (w (p) - D - 1) x + \epsilon (w (p) + 8| I| + D + 1) .\)

Given any desired positive slack above the limiting exponent, choose \(\epsilon\) small enough that the displayed excess is less than half the slack, then use the remaining half in the upper-rate bound. This proves rates \((w (p) - m) x\) and \((w (p) - D - 1) x\), respectively. No uniformity of the intermediate constructions as \(\epsilon \to 0\) is needed.

Translating the cluster center to the origin preserves all distances and the complete correlation. The raw coefficients use relative positions, so the same estimates hold at arbitrary centers. If the cluster is empty, its product is the identity. Exact reconstruction of that degree-zero field makes every raw truncation exact; positive-degree detectors annihilate it, and degree-zero coefficients are constant. Thus this case also satisfies the stated rates. Taking \(x = 1\) proves Input 15.3, conditional on Input 15.2, the rough separated correlation estimates, and the slot derivative identity (5.21) with the phase compatibility described above. The remaining task is to produce those matching and correlation estimates for the actual Wilson trajectory.

15.7Transferring the OPE estimates to homogeneous fields

The raw prescription and its covariant homogeneous version need not agree exactly on a product of fields. Their difference must therefore be estimated before the homogeneous coefficients can be used in the expansion. We give that transfer, retaining the complete correlations, including all unit and disconnected terms. The quantitative estimate for the raw prescription remains the construction input identified below.

Fix cluster fields \(\boldsymbol{v}= (v_i)_{i \in I}\), with \(w = \sum_i \deg v_i\). For a sequence of positive numbers \(r_n \to 0\), set \(t_n = - \log r_n \to \infty\). Say that a sequence \(f_n\) has upper rate \(a\) if for every \(\delta > 0\),

\(\displaystyle \|f_n \| \leqslant e^{(a + \delta) t_n} = r_n^{- a - \delta} \quad \text{for all sufficiently large } n.\)

This notation permits zero values. Finite sums preserve a common upper rate, products add rates, and fixed linear maps preserve rates. For sums, first use half the desired slack and then absorb the finite number of terms into \(e^{\delta t_n / 2}\). The same device absorbs a fixed operator norm. Products follow by dividing the slack between the factors. In particular any bounded sequence has upper rate zero.

If \(\ell_n : E \to \mathbb{C}\) is a sequence of linear functionals on a fixed finite-dimensional space, and \(\ell_n (e)\) has upper rate \(b\) for every fixed \(e\), then applying it to vectors \(u_n\) of upper rate \(a\) gives upper rate \(a + b\). To prove this, expand \(u_n\) in a fixed basis. Each coordinate has rate at most \(a\), each basis evaluation has rate at most \(b\), and the finite sum has rate at most \(a + b\). The identical finite-basis argument works for multilinear forms with several varying inputs. We will use it for bounded rotated detector labels.

Input 15.3. (Raw product comparison) For every fixed cluster label list, every \(M \geqslant 0\), \(\eta > 0\), every positive sequence \(r_n \to 0\) and configurations with

\(\displaystyle |x_{i, n} - o_n | \leqslant Mr_n, \qquad |x_{i, n} - x_{j, n} | \geqslant \eta r_n \quad (i \neq j),\)

the raw coefficients of (7.5) have upper rate \(w - | \alpha |\). For any fixed spectator label list \(\boldsymbol{z}\), any \(c > 0\), any positions satisfying

\(\displaystyle |y_{j, n} - o_n | \geqslant c, \qquad |y_{j, n} - y_{k, n} | \geqslant c \quad (j \neq k),\)

and any sequence of phases or mixed-state choices \(\chi_n\), the following assertions hold. For every \(D\), the raw discrepancy

\(\displaystyle S_{\chi_n} (\boldsymbol{v}, \boldsymbol{z}; \boldsymbol{x}_n, \boldsymbol{y}_n) - \ell_{\chi_n, D, o_n, \boldsymbol{y}_n, \boldsymbol{z}} (H_D (\boldsymbol{x}_n, \boldsymbol{v}; o_n))\) (15.34)

has upper rate \(w - D - 1\). For every fixed \(u \in E_j\), the separated insertion \(\ell_{\chi_n, j, o_n, \boldsymbol{y}_n, \boldsymbol{z}} (u)\) has upper rate zero. These statements hold for every finite spectator list, including empty lists, and for conjugated cluster fields as well. The same underlying hierarchy and physical inclusions are used throughout.

The input includes uniform control along arbitrarily moving configurations and phases at the stated fixed positive exterior clearance. A bound for each fixed configuration with a threshold depending on that configuration would be insufficient.

15.7.1The averaged readout error

Fix a raw truncation degree \(D\) and an output degree \(m\), which may differ. Abbreviate \(h_n = H_D (\boldsymbol{x}_n, \boldsymbol{v}; o_n)\). We claim that

\(\displaystyle C_m (\boldsymbol{x}_n, \boldsymbol{v}; o_n) - L_{mD} h_n \quad \text{has upper rate } w - D - 1.\) (15.35)

First consider only the rotation average. Let

\(\displaystyle Z_m (K ; \boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) = R_m (K) H_m (K^{- 1} \boldsymbol{x}, R (K^{- 1})\boldsymbol{v}; K^{- 1} o)\)

be its integrand. On the common separated region, expanding the finite tests gives the exact identity

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} & Z_m (K ; \boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}; o) - Z_m (K ; (o), (h) ; o)\\ & = \sum_{| \alpha | \leqslant m} \sum_{\lambda} b_{\alpha, \lambda} [S_{\text{mix}} (\boldsymbol{v}, R (K)\boldsymbol{w}_{\lambda} ; \boldsymbol{x}, o + K\boldsymbol{z}_{\lambda}) - S_{\text{mix}} (h, R (K)\boldsymbol{w}_{\lambda} ; o, o + K\boldsymbol{z}_{\lambda})] R_m (K) \iota_{| \alpha |, m} u_{\alpha} . \hspace{3cm} \text{(15.36)} \end{array}\)

Here each sum over \(\lambda\) is restricted to the finite support of \(b_{\alpha}\). To verify the identity, write each raw coefficient as its exterior correlation at relative origin. Rotate the entire correlation by \(K\) and translate by \(o\); by the covariance and translation laws (7.8) neither operation changes its value. This is the only place where covariance of the correlations enters, and it cannot be omitted: the assumptions on the actions \(R_n\) and \(J_n\) alone do not imply (15.36). The cluster positions and labels become \(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{v}\), and the detector positions and labels become exactly those displayed. Subtract the same evaluation with the single field \(h\) at \(o\), and use linearity in that slot.

Take \(h = h_n\) and any sequence \(K_n \in O (4)\). All the rotated detector positions have a common positive separation from \(o_n\) and from one another: these are the fixed finite nonzero detector distances, preserved by rotations. All rotated detector labels and output vectors are bounded, by continuity of the representations on the compact group. Apply Input 15.3 to each fixed basis choice of detector labels in (15.36), then use the multilinear finite-basis argument above to allow their bounded variation with \(K_n\). Every summand and hence their finite sum has upper rate \(w - D - 1\).

This statement for every rotation sequence implies a uniform estimate in \(K\) at each sufficiently large \(n\). Indeed, for a fixed slack, if there were a violating rotation at infinitely many indices, choose one at each such index and any rotation at the remaining indices. The resulting sequence would contradict the rate estimate just proved. Thus the norm of the integrand difference is eventually bounded uniformly by \(e^{(w - D - 1 + \delta) t_n}\). Both integrands are continuous on the compact group once the shrinking cluster lies in \(\mathcal{U}_m\). Integrating against probability Haar measure gives the same bound for the difference of their averages.

Apply the same argument to the conjugated part. Its single comparison field is \(J_D h_n\), rather than the raw truncation recomputed from the conjugated cluster. This distinction causes no extra assumption. By the reality law in (7.8), conjugating a complete correlation conjugates all its labels, and the raw discrepancy is linear in each spectator slot. Hence the discrepancy with cluster \(J\boldsymbol{v}\), comparison field \(J_D h_n\), and arbitrary spectator labels is the complex conjugate of the original discrepancy with conjugated spectator labels. That original discrepancy is covered by the input and the bounded-label argument. It has the same norm and upper rate. Output conjugation is a fixed real-linear map on a finite-dimensional space and preserves the rate. Adding the two averages and dividing by two proves (15.35); translation invariance (7.8) identifies its centered singleton readout with \(L_{mD} h_n\). Applying the fixed projection \(K_m\) yields

\(\displaystyle F_m (\boldsymbol{x}_n, \boldsymbol{v}; o_n) - P_{mD} h_n \quad \text{has upper rate } w - D - 1.\) (15.37)

15.7.2Coefficient and remainder bounds

Set \(D = m\) in (15.37). Applying \(P_{mm}\) to the raw shadow \(H_m\) kills every term of degree less than \(m\), by inclusion compatibility and (7.13). The remaining finitely many terms consist of degree-\(m\) raw coefficients, of upper rate \(w - m\), times fixed vectors. Their sum has that rate. The difference in (15.37) has the smaller rate \(w - m - 1\), so \(F_m\) has upper rate \(w - m\). Taking its fixed channel coordinates gives the same rate for each coefficient in (7.15).

For the remainder, retain the raw truncation \(h_n = H_D\) and abbreviate the spectator functional at degree \(j\) by \(\ell_{n, j}\). The exact reconstruction identity in (7.12) and compatibility of spectator insertions give

\(\displaystyle \ell_{n, D} (h_n) = \sum_{j \leqslant D} \ell_{n, j} (P_{jD} h_n) .\)

Consequently the final homogeneous discrepancy is exactly the raw discrepancy (15.34) plus

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j \leqslant D} \ell_{n, j} (P_{jD} h_n - F_j (\boldsymbol{x}_n, \boldsymbol{v}; o_n)) .\) (15.38)

Each vector difference has upper rate \(w - D - 1\) by (15.37). The insertion functional has rate zero on every fixed vector by the input. The finite-dimensional evaluation argument therefore gives rate \(w - D - 1\) for every term of (15.38), and for their finite sum. Adding the raw discrepancy proves the same rate for the final discrepancy, along any prescribed configuration and phase sequence.

Theorem 15.4. Under Input 15.3 and the standing assumptions of Subsection 7.2, including the covariance, translation and reality laws (7.8) of the mixed correlations, the coefficients of (7.15) satisfy the public separated OPE bounds. Specifically, for every \(\epsilon > 0\), fixed channel \((m, \alpha)\) and fixed \(M \geqslant 0\), \(\eta > 0\), at all sufficiently small \(r > 0\),

\(\displaystyle |c_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{m, \alpha} (\boldsymbol{x}; o) | \leqslant r^{m - w - \epsilon}\)

uniformly over \(|x_i - o| \leqslant Mr\) and \(|x_i - x_j | \geqslant \eta r\). For each finite \(D\), fixed spectator labels and \(c > 0\), one also has

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{l} | S_{\chi} (\boldsymbol{v}, \boldsymbol{z}; \boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y}) - \sum_{j \leqslant D} \sum_{\alpha} c_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{j, \alpha} (\boldsymbol{x}; o) S_{\chi} (e_{j, \alpha}, \boldsymbol{z}; o, \boldsymbol{y}) | \leqslant r^{D + 1 - w - \epsilon}, \end{array}\)

uniformly over the same clusters, all spectators with center and mutual clearance at least \(c\), and all phases and the mixed state.

Proof. The preceding arguments prove these inequalities eventually along every admissible sequence, by taking slack \(\epsilon\) in the rate definition. If a uniform threshold for the coefficient failed, choose for each \(n\) a violating radius \(0 < r_n < 1 / n\) and an admissible cluster at that radius. The resulting sequence contradicts its eventual rate bound. If the uniform remainder threshold failed, choose in addition a violating spectator configuration and a violating phase at each radius. The rate bound was proved for arbitrary such choices, so gives the same contradiction. A finite initial segment where a cluster might meet a spectator is immaterial: for sufficiently small \(r\), \(Mr < c / 2\) makes the combined configuration separated, while the stated lower bounds separate all points within either group. The center and spectators are already separated by \(c\).\(\Box\)

The coefficients used in this theorem were chosen before \(D\), the phase and the spectators. The small-radius threshold may depend on the cluster and spectator labels, on \(D\), \(M\), \(\eta\), \(c\), \(\epsilon\) and, for a coefficient, on the channel; it does not depend on the phase, on the mixed-state choice or on the positions. The theorem transfers the quantitative raw comparison; it does not produce that comparison from the Wilson measure.
