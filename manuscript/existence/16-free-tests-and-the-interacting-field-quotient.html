16.1Free-reference rank from finite point probes

This section proves the finite-dimensional reference-rank facts used in the trace calibration. The calculation uses only four-dimensional Euclidean Green-function derivatives, the signed coordinate symmetries, and simplicity of the compact gauge Lie algebra. The divergence-rank conclusion also uses the compatible derivative plan specified below. All spaces below are real, even though polynomial coefficients are temporarily complexified to encode real observables.

16.1.1Low-grade invariant labels

Write \(\mathfrak{g}\) for the compact simple real gauge Lie algebra, with its invariant inner product, and \(e_0, e_1, e_2, e_3\) for the coordinate basis of \(\mathbb{R}^4\). A grade-\(d\) label is a polynomial in all radial jets, with jet order \(r\) having engineering weight \(r + 2\). In particular, grades four and five may include linear terms in higher jets; those terms are removed below by gauge simplicity. After that reduction, their nonlinear terms depend only on the curvature \(F\) and, at grade five, its first derivative \(J\). A signed permutation \(s\) acts on a coordinate tensor by permuting its indices and multiplying by the product of the corresponding coordinate signs. The raw grade-four space consists of symmetric two-index arrays of grade-four labels, real under complex conjugation and covariant under this signed action. The raw grade-five space consists of one-index arrays of grade-five labels with the same reality and covariance conditions.

The following elementary color fact is used repeatedly. If a real linear functional \(\ell\) on \(\mathfrak{g}\) is invariant under the adjoint action, then differentiating \(\ell (\text{Ad}_{\exp (tx)} \hspace{0.17em} y) = \ell (y)\) at \(t = 0\) gives \(\ell ([x, y]) = 0\). Simplicity and nonabelianity imply \([\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] =\mathfrak{g}\), hence \(\ell = 0\). For an invariant real bilinear form \(B\), define \(T\) by \(B (x, y) = \langle Tx, y \rangle\). The invariance of \(B\) and of the positive inner product implies \(T [a, x] = [a, Tx]\) for every \(a, x\). Therefore its self-adjoint and skew-adjoint parts \(S = (T + T^{\ast}) / 2\) and \(A = (T - T^{\ast}) / 2\) both commute with the adjoint action. Every eigenspace of \(S\) is an ideal; the spectral theorem and simplicity show \(S = \lambda I\). Also \(- A^2\) is positive self-adjoint and commutes with the adjoint action, so the same argument gives \(- A^2 = cI\) for some \(c \geqslant 0\). If \(c > 0\), then \(J = A / \sqrt{c}\) is a skew-adjoint complex structure commuting with every \(\text{ad}_a\). This implies \(\text{ad}_{Jx} = J \text{ad}_x\). The right side is self-adjoint, because it is a product of commuting skew-adjoint operators, whereas the left side is skew-adjoint by invariance of the inner product. It must vanish; invertibility of \(J\) would then make every \(\text{ad}_x\) vanish, contradicting nonabelianity. Thus \(c = 0\) and \(A = 0\), so \(T = \lambda I\). Every invariant bilinear form is consequently a scalar multiple of the inner product. Applying this argument to real and imaginary parts covers complex-valued coefficients too. Consequently the color dependence of an invariant quadratic term is just the inner product. The engineering weights are \(2\) for \(F\) and \(3\) for its first derivative \(J\); a jet of order \(r\) has weight \(r + 2\). At grade four or five, a monomial can therefore contain at most two factors. The possible terms are a constant, one linear functional of each jet of weight at most the grade, a symmetric quadratic form in \(F\), and (only at grade five) a mixed bilinear form in \((F, J)\). Every linear jet term vanishes by the preceding color argument, including the higher-derivative linear terms allowed by these two grades. Under simultaneous reversal of all four coordinate signs, \(F\) is even while the output vector index and \(J\) are odd. Hence constants and quadratic \(F\) terms vanish in a covariant vector. Only the mixed term remains in grade five.

The radial degree-zero and degree-one potentials are represented by the following spanning families, for \(c \in \mathfrak{g}\):

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} (\ell^0_{ij} c)_a (z) & = \tfrac{1}{2} (\delta_{aj} z_i - \delta_{ai} z_j) c,\\ (\ell^1_{rkl} c)_a (z) & = \tfrac{1}{3} (\delta_{al} z_k - \delta_{ak} z_l) z_r c. \end{array}\)

The first family is antisymmetric in \(i, j\); the second is antisymmetric in \(k, l\) and satisfies

\(\displaystyle \ell^1_{rkl} + \ell^1_{klr} + \ell^1_{lrk} = 0.\)

This identity follows by adding the three displayed quadratic monomials: each term cancels its opposite. The families span the actual radial jet spaces: for every degree-zero potential \(A_0\) and degree-one potential \(A_1\), respectively,

\(\displaystyle A_0 = \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i, j} \ell^0_{ij} (\text{curl}_0^{ij} \hspace{0.17em} A_0), \qquad A_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{r, k, l} \ell^1_{rkl} (\text{curl}_1^{rkl} \hspace{0.17em} A_1) .\)

Here a radial jet of index \(r\) is a homogeneous polynomial one-form \(A = (A_a)\) of polynomial degree \(r + 1\), satisfying \(\sum_i z_i A_i (z) = 0\). Its linear curl is the two-form \(F_{ia} (z) = \partial_i A_a (z) - \partial_a A_i (z)\); no nonlinear commutator is included in this jet calculation. Differentiating the radial identity with respect to \(z_a\) and using Euler's identity gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_i z_i \partial_a A_i = - A_a, \qquad \sum_i z_i \partial_i A_a = (r + 1) A_a .\)

Subtracting proves the reconstruction formula

\(\displaystyle A_a (z) = \frac{1}{r + 2} \sum_i z_i F_{ia} (z) .\) (16.1)

In particular curl is injective on this radial homogeneous space: a zero curl forces every component of \(A\) to vanish. For \(A_0\), write \(f_{ij} = \partial_i (A_0)_j - \partial_j (A_0)_i\), which is constant. For \(A_1\), write \(j_{rkl} = \partial_r (\partial_k (A_1)_l - \partial_l (A_1)_k)\), which is also constant. These are derivative values, with no factorial rescaling. Homogeneity gives \(F_{kl} (z) = \sum_r z_r j_{rkl}\) in the second case. Thus (16.1) reads

\(\displaystyle (A_0)_a (z) = \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_i z_i f_{ia}, \qquad (A_1)_a (z) = \tfrac{1}{3} \sum_{r, k} z_k z_r j_{rka} .\)

Antisymmetry now checks the asserted spanning identities directly:

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i, j} (\ell^0_{ij} f_{ij})_a (z) & = \tfrac{1}{4} \left( \sum_i z_i f_{ia} - \sum_j z_j f_{aj} \right) = \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_i z_i f_{ia},\\ \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{r, k, l} (\ell^1_{rkl} j_{rkl})_a (z) & = \tfrac{1}{6} \left( \sum_{r, k} z_k z_r j_{rka} - \sum_{r, l} z_l z_r j_{ral} \right) = \tfrac{1}{3} \sum_{r, k} z_k z_r j_{rka} . \end{array}\)

Equality as polynomial functions is equality of their symmetric coefficients, so these are identities of radial jets. A bilinear form in these slots is therefore determined by its values on pairs of legs; the legs need not be linearly independent. Signed covariance and pair antisymmetry follow from their displayed formulas. Commuting the ordinary partial derivatives in the definition of \(j\) gives \(j_{rkl} + j_{klr} + j_{lrk} = 0\), the Bianchi relation used below.

For a grade-five vector label, let \(M_p\) denote the mixed bilinear term in its low-grade decomposition. Choose a unit color vector \(v\) and define

\(\displaystyle A_{p ; r ; ij ; kl} = M_p (\ell^0_{ij} (v), \ell^1_{rkl} (v)) .\)

Reality makes these coefficients real. The adjoint-invariant bilinear-form calculation shows that this unit color vector records the full color coefficient. The leg spanning identities show that the coefficients determine every \(M_p\). The cycle relation for \(\ell^1\) gives the Bianchi relation for \(A\). Hence \(A_{p ; r ; ij ; kl}\) is antisymmetric in \(i, j\) and in \(k, l\), and obeys

\(\displaystyle A_{p ; r ; ij ; kl} = s_p s_r s_i s_j s_k s_l \hspace{0.17em} A_{s (p) ; s (r) ; s (i) s (j) ; s (k) s (l)} \) (16.2)

for every signed permutation, where the signs \(s_a \in \{\pm 1\}\) are those of the coordinate reflection. The finite signed-coordinate orbit count in dimension four has the following explicit form: if a coefficient satisfying (1) is nonzero, then its two antisymmetric pairs have distinct entries, and either (i) \(p = r\) and the two pairs are parallel, or (ii) \(p \ne r\) and there is a third coordinate \(u\), distinct from \(p, r\), such that the two pairs are \((p, u), (r, u)\) in either order. Permuting coordinates and reversing an antisymmetric pair reduces these cases to the four rows

\(\displaystyle (p, r ; ij ; kl) = (0, 0 ; 01 ; 01), (0, 0 ; 12 ; 12), (0, 2 ; 01 ; 21), (0, 2 ; 21 ; 01) . \) (16.3)

To check the count directly, a reflection in coordinate \(a\) multiplies the coefficient by \(- 1\) exactly when \(a\) belongs to an odd number of the six indices \(p, r, i, j, k, l\); a nonzero entry requires even parity at every coordinate. If \(p = r\), this forces the supports of the two antisymmetric pairs to agree, giving case (i). If \(p \ne r\), parity forces the pairs to share exactly one coordinate, distinct from both \(p, r\), giving case (ii). Coordinate permutations put the resulting supports in the forms in (2), and antisymmetry fixes the order signs. This proves the stated orbit count.

Lemma 16.1. (Three probes determine every covariant vector label) Let \(A_{p ; r ; ij ; kl}\) be a real array satisfying the antisymmetries, Bianchi relation, and signed covariance above. If

\(\displaystyle A_{0 ; 0 ; 01 ; 01} = A_{0 ; 2 ; 01 ; 21} = A_{0 ; 2 ; 21 ; 01} = 0,\)

then every coefficient of \(A\) vanishes.

Proof. By the orbit count, it suffices to check the four rows in (16.3). Three are the displayed hypotheses. For the remaining row, use Bianchi in the differentiated pair with derivative index \(0\) and pair \(12\):

\(\displaystyle A_{0 ; 0 ; 12 ; 12} + A_{0 ; 1 ; 12 ; 20} + A_{0 ; 2 ; 12 ; 01} = 0.\)

The third term is minus the third displayed probe by reversing the first pair \(12\) to \(21\). Interchanging \(1\) and \(2\) carries the second term to the same third probe, up to the sign from reversing its first or differentiated pair. Both terms therefore vanish, so \(A_{0 ; 0 ; 12 ; 12} = 0\). Thus all four representatives vanish; signed covariance and antisymmetry propagate zero to every entry.\(\Box\)

16.1.2The raw spaces have dimensions at most four and three

For a raw grade-four tensor \(Q = (Q_{ab})\), choose a unit color vector \(v\). The four real linear probes are: the real constant coefficient of \(Q_{00}\); and the coefficients of \(\langle F_{01}, F_{01} \rangle\) and \(\langle F_{12}, F_{12} \rangle\) in \(Q_{00}\) and \(\langle F_{01}, F_{21} \rangle\) in \(Q_{02}\). If all four vanish, the constant vanishes. Polarization in the two \(F\) slots extracts the symmetric quadratic coefficient. For the quadratic coefficient array, signed parity says that if \(a = b\), the two antisymmetric field pairs have the same support. Relative to the fixed output axis \(a\), their common support either contains \(a\) or avoids \(a\); coordinate permutations fixing \(a\) reduce these to the probes \(Q_{00}\) on \((01, 01)\) and \((12, 12)\). If \(a \ne b\), the two field-pair supports have symmetric difference \(\{a, b\}\), so they share a third coordinate distinct from \(a, b\). Coordinate permutation reduces this to \(Q_{02}\) on \((01, 21)\). Pair reversals change only signs; exchanging the output indices gives the same coefficient by \(Q_{ab} = Q_{ba}\). These are exactly the three quadratic probes listed above. The invariant-color argument makes vanishing on the chosen unit color vector sufficient: every color coefficient is its inner product multiple. The linear term is zero by color simplicity. Thus these four probes are injective, and

\(\displaystyle \dim \mathcal{R}_4 \leqslant 4,\)

where \(\mathcal{R}_4\) denotes the raw symmetric grade-four table space.

For a raw grade-five vector \(Q = (Q_p)\), polarize the mixed term by evaluating the grade-zero and grade-one slots at \((F, J)\), \((F, 0)\), \((0, J)\), and \((0, 0)\) and taking the alternating sum. This cancels the constant and pure-degree terms and leaves exactly the mixed bilinear coefficient. The three probes are the three entries in Lemma 16.1. The Bianchi identity supplies the fourth orbit representative, while the color argument and the all-coordinate reflection remove the other grades as shown above. Thus the probe map is injective into \(\mathbb{R}^3\) and

\(\displaystyle \dim \mathcal{R}_5 \leqslant 3.\)

This proves the two raw-dimension bounds without assuming a tensor classification beyond the finite parity count just given.

16.1.3The normalized free stress basis

Let \(\nu = (4 \pi^2)^{- 1}\) and \(d_G = \dim \mathfrak{g}> 0\). The normalized four-dimensional massless Green function is \(G (x) = \nu |x|^{- 2}\). At the unit vector \(e_0\), direct differentiation gives

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \partial_i \partial_j G (e_0) & = - 2 \nu \delta_{ij} + 8 \nu \delta_{i 0} \delta_{j 0}, \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.4)} \\ \partial_k \partial_i \partial_j G (e_0) & = 8 \nu (\delta_{ij} \delta_{k 0} + \delta_{ki} \delta_{j 0} + \delta_{i 0} \delta_{kj}) - 48 \nu \delta_{i 0} \delta_{j 0} \delta_{k 0} . \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.5)} \end{array}\)

For example, differentiating \(G\) once gives \(\partial_i G (x) = - 2 \nu x_i |x|^{- 4}\). Differentiating again gives

\(\displaystyle - 2 \nu \delta_{ij} |x|^{- 4} + 8 \nu x_i x_j |x|^{- 6} .\)

One more derivative and \(x = e_0\) give the displayed formulas.

For antisymmetric index pairs define the free curl kernel

\(\displaystyle C_{ab ; km} (x) = \delta_{kb} \partial_a \partial_m G (x) - \delta_{mb} \partial_a \partial_k G (x) - \delta_{ka} \partial_b \partial_m G (x) + \delta_{ma} \partial_b \partial_k G (x),\)

and the two-contraction chain

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{C}_{ab, cd ; ij, kl} (x) = C_{ab ; ij} (x) C_{cd ; kl} (x) + C_{ab ; kl} (x) C_{cd ; ij} (x) .\)

The Wick pairing of two separated point insertions with coefficient vector \(w = (w_0, w_1, w_2, w_3)\) is \(d_G\) times

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{D}_w^{ab} (H) = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} w_1 \sum_i H_{ai, ai} + w_2 \sum_{i, j} H_{ij, ij}, & a = b,\\ w_3 \sum_i H_{ai, bi}, & a \ne b, \end{array} \right.\)

For fixed field pairs \((ij, kl)\) set \(H_{pqrs} (x) = C_{pq ; ij} (x) C_{rs ; kl} (x) + C_{pq ; kl} (x) C_{rs ; ij} (x)\). The probe is \(S_w (ij, kl ; ab ; x) = d_G \mathcal{D}_w^{ab} (H (x))\); the vacuum contribution to a one-point \(00\) entry is \(w_0\). Substitution of (16.4) into the curl factors gives

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} S_w (01, 01 ; 00 ; e_0) & = d_G \nu^2 (32 w_1 + 64 w_2), \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.6)}\\ S_w (12, 12 ; 00 ; e_0) & = d_G \nu^2 (64 w_2), \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.7)}\\ S_w (02, 12 ; 01 ; e_0) & = d_G \nu^2 (- 16 w_3) . \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.8)} \end{array}\)

After factoring out \(d_G \nu^2\), the coefficient rows for these three probes, in columns \((w_1, w_2, w_3)\), are

\(\displaystyle (32, 64, 0), \qquad (0, 64, 0), \qquad (0, 0, - 16) .\)

These finite sums are evaluated by inserting the displayed Kronecker-delta Hessian into \(C\) and then into \(H\) and \(\mathcal{D}_w\). The second equation recovers \(w_2\), the first then recovers \(w_1\), and the third recovers \(w_3\), since \(d_G \nu^2 > 0\). Proof. The four probes above prove injectivity from a four-dimensional space; the raw upper bound proves equality of dimensions, hence bijectivity.\(\Box\)

16.1.4The two conserved free directions

Let \(\mathcal{T}_4\) be the free symmetric grade-four stress table and \(K_0 = \ker (\text{skim} : \mathcal{T}_4 \to \mathbb{R})\). Let \(\mathcal{V}_5\) be the grade-five vector table after imposing the free Maxwell equation. Fix on this same free field table a derivative plan, whose grade-raising maps on the complexified label spaces are \(\mathscr{D}_a^{(d)} : \mathcal{F}_d \to \mathcal{F}_{d + 1}\). They are complex-linear, commute with the real involution and have vector covariance:

\(\displaystyle \rho_{d + 1} (s) \mathscr{D}_b^{(d)} = \sum_a s_{ab} \mathscr{D}_a^{(d)} \rho_d (s)\)

for every signed permutation matrix \(s\), where \(\rho_d\) is its action on grade-\(d\) labels. Their compatibility with the smooth separated free correlations is as follows. For every grade-\(d\) label \(B\), every finite spectator word \(B_1, \ldots, B_n\) of arbitrary grades, and every pairwise distinct tuple \((x, x_1, \ldots, x_n)\),

\(\displaystyle \left\langle (\mathscr{D}_a^{(d)} B) (x) \prod_{k = 1}^n B_k (x_k) \right\rangle_0 = \partial_{x_a} \left\langle B (x) \prod_{k = 1}^n B_k (x_k) \right\rangle_0 .\) (16.9)

The derivative is taken in the first insertion, holding every spectator fixed, within the open set of separated points. Here is the construction on the reference table used in this chapter. Represent a label by its invariant polynomial in curvature jets, using radial reconstruction to identify these with the radial labels. On a jet generator \(\partial^{\alpha} F_{bc}\) define the translation derivation in direction \(a\) by \(\partial^{\alpha} F_{bc} \mapsto \partial^{\alpha + e_a} F_{bc}\), and extend by the Leibniz rule. It increases engineering weight by one, annihilates constants, preserves invariant polynomials, and commutes with complex conjugation. Changing coordinates by a signed permutation in this formula gives exactly the displayed vector-covariance identity.

To check its action on correlations, expand a Wick moment of any fixed finite word into its finite set of contractions. Each contraction is a product of differentiated curvature covariance kernels. All are smooth at separated positions. Differentiation in the distinguished position acts on each of its incident contractions once, by the product rule; raising the corresponding jet in that slot gives exactly that term. Summing proves (16.9) at polynomial level, for every spectator word, including the empty word. No differentiation under an infinite sum is involved.

The field table is the quotient by labels with all separated moments zero. If a polynomial has zero field, its moments are identically zero on each open configuration space. Their derivatives are zero there as well. The identity just proved therefore shows that its translation derivation also has zero field. The derivation descends to the quotient and defines \(\mathscr{D}_a^{(d)}\) independently of the representative. Reality, covariance and the unit identity descend with it. Thus the compatibility needed below holds on this particular reference table; it is not an additional choice of a derivative plan. Define the restricted divergence by

\(\displaystyle (D_0 T)_b = \sum_{a = 0}^3 \mathscr{D}_a^{(4)} T_{ab}, \qquad D_0 : K_0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{V}_5 .\)

Reality and the covariance law make this a real linear map to the stated vector table: the two transformed indices contracted in the sum cancel by orthogonality of \(s\), leaving its vector action on \(b\). Write \(\pi\) for the quotient projection from the raw vector table onto \(\mathcal{V}_5\). The projection is onto by the equivariant section obtained by averaging any real section over the finite signed permutation group. The equation-of-motion array is

\(\displaystyle E_b = \sum_{i, a} \langle F_{bi}, \partial_a F_{ai} \rangle .\)

It is a nonzero raw covariant vector: take a nonzero color vector \(v\), set \(F_{01} = v\) and \(\partial_0 F_{01} = v\) at one jet, with all other independent jet coordinates zero. The defining sum then evaluates its \(b = 0\) component to \(\langle v, v \rangle > 0\). The Maxwell equation makes \(E\) vanish after the quotient. Thus the kernel of \(\pi\) is nonzero. Rank–nullity and \(\dim \mathcal{R}_5 \leqslant 3\) give \(\dim \mathcal{V}_5 \leqslant 2\).

Let \(\phi_1, \phi_2\) be the two linear functionals on \(\mathcal{V}_5\) obtained by correlating the component with output index \(0\) at \(e_0\) with the respective quadratic curvature probes \((01, 01)\) and \((12, 12)\) at the origin. These are functionals of a vector label, not definitions by differentiating a stress correlation. As above, \(S_w (ij, kl ; ab ; x)\) is the stress response with field-pair insertion \((ij, kl)\), output component \((a, b)\), and separation \(x\). For the stress \(T_w\) with coefficient vector \(w\) and zero skim, linearity and (16.9) give

\(\displaystyle \phi_1 (D_0 T_w) = \sum_u \partial_u S_w (01, 01 ; u 0 ; e_0), \qquad \phi_2 (D_0 T_w) = \sum_u \partial_u S_w (12, 12 ; u 0 ; e_0) .\)

Differentiating the chain product gives

\(\displaystyle \partial_u \mathcal{C}= (\partial_u C_{ab ; ij}) C_{cd ; kl} + C_{ab ; ij} (\partial_u C_{cd ; kl}) + (\partial_u C_{ab ; kl}) C_{cd ; ij} + C_{ab ; kl} (\partial_u C_{cd ; ij}) .\)

Inserting (16.4)– (16.5) and summing \(u = 0, 1, 2, 3\) gives

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \sum_u \partial_u S_w (01, 01 ; u 0 ; e_0) & = d_G \nu^2 (- 256 w_1 - 512 w_2 + 128 w_3), \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.10)}\\ \sum_u \partial_u S_w (12, 12 ; u 0 ; e_0) & = d_G \nu^2 (- 512 w_2 - 128 w_3) . \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.11)} \end{array}\)

The two coefficient rows in \((w_1, w_2, w_3)\) are linearly independent. Consequently \(\phi_1 \circ D_0\) and \(\phi_2 \circ D_0\) are independent, so \(\phi_1, \phi_2\) are independent on \(\mathcal{V}_5\). Together with the upper bound \(\dim \mathcal{V}_5 \leqslant 2\), this gives \(\dim \mathcal{V}_5 = 2\), and \(\Phi = (\phi_1, \phi_2) : \mathcal{V}_5 \to \mathbb{R}^2\) is an isomorphism. The two probe equations have common kernel \(\mathbb{R} (1, - 1 / 4, 1)\) on \((w_1, w_2, w_3)\): the second gives \(w_2 = - w_3 / 4\), and then the first gives \(w_1 = w_3\). The scalar coefficient \(w_0\) is removed by zero skim. Thus the kernel of \((\text{skim}, \Phi \circ D_0)\) is exactly the Weyl line \((0, 1, - 1 / 4, 1)\). Since \(\Phi\) is an isomorphism, this also proves the Weyl tensor has zero divergence. The zero-skim domain has dimension three, and the restricted divergence has one-dimensional kernel, so it has rank two and is onto the two-dimensional target. Its kernel is precisely the Weyl line.

16.2The free Maxwell stress and its collar Ward identities

The reference stress is fixed by its action on every insertion, not only by conservation. We prove that action using the same free curvature covariance and Wick polynomial fields as in Subsection 16.1. All integrals involving the stress below are taken on compact collars separated from every insertion. No product of singular kernels at coincident points is used.

Let \(\mathfrak{g}\) carry the fixed invariant real inner product. A smooth \(\mathfrak{g}\)-valued two-form \(f = (f_{ab})\) is a Maxwell wave on an open set if it is antisymmetric and satisfies

\(\displaystyle \partial_a f_{bc} + \partial_b f_{ca} + \partial_c f_{ab} = 0, \qquad \sum_a \partial_a f_{ab} = 0.\)

Indices run from zero to three. Set \(\nu = (4 \pi^2)^{- 1}\) and \(G (z) = \nu |z|^{- 2}\) for \(z \ne 0\). The covariance between curvature components is

\(\displaystyle C_{ab ; il} (z) = \delta_{ib} \partial_a \partial_l G (z) - \delta_{lb} \partial_a \partial_i G (z) - \delta_{ia} \partial_b \partial_l G (z) + \delta_{la} \partial_b \partial_i G (z),\) (16.12)

times the identity on colour. A linear probe \(p\) of a homogeneous Maxwell jet of order \(j\) acts on \(D^j f (x) / j\)!; write its value as \(p [f ; x]\). Its covariance pulse \(P_{p, x} (y)\) is the covariance of that probe at \(x\) with curvature at \(y\). For a coordinate curvature probe \((\gamma, i, l)\), with colour basis vector \(e_{\gamma}\), this is \(C_{ab ; il} (y - x) e_{\gamma}\); higher pulses are obtained by the corresponding normalized derivatives in \(x\). Such probes span the dual of the finite jet space, and these formulas extend linearly. The raised probe \(\partial_u p\) is defined by \((\partial_u p) [f ; x] = \partial_u (p [f ; \hspace{0.17em} \cdot \hspace{0.17em}]) (x)\). It includes the factor \(j + 1\) arising from normalized Taylor coefficients. Differentiating the covariance gives

\(\displaystyle \partial_{x, u} P_{p, x} (y) = P_{\partial_u p, x} (y) .\) (16.13)

Each pulse is a Maxwell wave away from its centre, as follows by commuting derivatives in (16.12) and using \(\Delta G = 0\) there.

16.2.1The polarized current and its divergence

For closed two-forms \(f, g\), define the polarized stress

\(\displaystyle T_{ab} (f, g) = \sum_c (\langle f_{ac}, g_{bc} \rangle + \langle f_{bc}, g_{ac} \rangle) - \frac{\delta_{ab}}{2} \sum_{c, d} \langle f_{cd}, g_{cd} \rangle .\)

It is symmetric in \(a, b\) and in \(f, g\), and its trace is zero in four dimensions. Put \((\text{div} \hspace{0.17em} f)_c = \sum_a \partial_a f_{ac}\). The Bianchi identity implies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{a, c} \langle f_{ac}, \partial_a g_{bc} \rangle = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{a, c} \langle f_{ac}, \partial_b g_{ac} \rangle .\)

Indeed substitute \(\partial_a g_{bc} = \partial_b g_{ac} - \partial_c g_{ab}\); exchanging \(a, c\) and using both antisymmetries makes the last sum equal to the left side. The product rule, followed by this identity for \((f, g)\) and \((g, f)\), cancels the differentiated trace terms and gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_a \partial_a T_{ab} (f, g) = \sum_c \left( \langle \text{div} \hspace{0.17em} f_c, g_{bc} \rangle + \langle f_{bc}, \text{div} \hspace{0.17em} g_c \rangle \right) .\) (16.14)

We need \(v (y) = e_b\) and \(v (y) = A (y - x)\) for a real skew matrix \(A\). For either vector field define \(j_a (v ; f, g) = \sum_b v_b T_{ab} (f, g)\). The term \(\sum_{a, b} (\partial_a v_b) T_{ab}\) vanishes by symmetry; the same holds for dilation \(v (y) = y - x\) by tracelessness. Thus, if both forms are Maxwell waves, \(\text{div} \hspace{0.17em} j = 0\). For \(\chi \in C_c^{\infty} (\mathbb{R}^4 ; \mathbb{R})\) define outward collar flux by

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{F}_{\chi} (j) = - \sum_a \int (\partial_a \chi) (y) j_a (y) \hspace{0.17em} dy.\) (16.15)

Whenever \(j\) is smooth on a neighborhood of \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \chi\), integration by parts identifies this with \(\int \chi \text{div} \hspace{0.17em} j\). In particular it vanishes for two waves on that neighborhood. If the only singularity is at \(x\) and both cutoffs equal one near \(x\), their fluxes agree: apply the same identity to their difference, which vanishes on a neighborhood of \(x\).

16.2.2A unit-mass regulator fixes the sign

For \(t > 0\) set

\(\displaystyle G_t (z) = \frac{\nu}{t^2 + |z|^2}, \qquad \rho_t (z) = \frac{8 \nu t^2}{(t^2 + |z|^2)^3} .\)

Direct differentiation gives \(- \Delta G_t = \rho_t\) and \(t^4 \rho_t (tz) = \rho_1 (z)\). These kernels are nonnegative, and polar coordinates give

\(\displaystyle \int \rho_1 (z) \hspace{0.17em} dz = 16 \pi^2 \nu \int_0^{\infty} \frac{r^3}{(1 + r^2)^3} \hspace{0.17em} dr = 1.\)

The radial integral is \(1 / 4\), since an antiderivative is \(\tfrac{1}{4} (1 - (1 + r^2)^{- 1})^2\). For any continuous compactly supported \(h\), substitution \(y = x + tz\) and dominated convergence therefore give

\(\displaystyle \int \rho_t (y - x) h (y) \hspace{0.17em} dy \longrightarrow h (x) .\) (16.16)

The dominating function is \(\rho_1 (z) \|h\|_{\infty}\).

Fix a coordinate probe \((\gamma, i, l)\) and form a regulated pulse \(R_t = dA_t\), where

\(\displaystyle (A_t)_b (y) = (\delta_{ib} \partial_l G_t (y - x) - \delta_{lb} \partial_i G_t (y - x)) e_{\gamma} .\)

It is closed and satisfies

\(\displaystyle (\text{div} \hspace{0.17em} R_t)_b = (\delta_{lb} \partial_i \rho_t (y - x) - \delta_{ib} \partial_l \rho_t (y - x)) e_{\gamma} .\)

For the latter identity, \(\sum_b \partial_b (A_t)_b = 0\) by commuting derivatives, so \(\text{div} \hspace{0.17em} dA_t = \Delta A_t\); now use \(\Delta G_t = - \rho_t\). Away from \(x\), \(R_t\) and all its derivatives converge locally uniformly to \(P_{(\gamma, i, l), x}\).

Suppose \(f\) is a Maxwell wave on a neighborhood of \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \chi\), and \(\chi = 1\) near \(x\). Put \(a_b (y) = \sum_u v_u (y) f_{ub} (y)\) and \(H_b (y) = \chi (y) \langle e_{\gamma}, a_b (y) \rangle\). Equation (16.14) gives

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{F}_{\chi} (j (v ; R_t, f)) = \int H_l \partial_i \rho_t (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - x) - \int H_i \partial_l \rho_t (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - x) = \int \rho_t (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - x) \partial_l H_i - \int \rho_t (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - x) \partial_i H_l .\)

All integrations by parts are with smooth compactly supported \(H_b\). The right side tends to \(\partial_l H_i (x) - \partial_i H_l (x)\) by (16.16). The left side tends to the unregulated flux because \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} d \chi\) is compact and separated from \(x\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{F}_{\chi} (j (v ; P_{(\gamma, i, l), x}, f)) = - \langle e_{\gamma}, (d \iota_v f)_{il} (x) \rangle .\) (16.17)

This proves the sign directly from the Green normalization and the outward-flux convention.

For an arbitrary jet probe \(p\), differentiate this identity in the enclosed centre while keeping both \(v\) and \(\chi\) fixed. The moving centre stays in a neighborhood on which \(\chi = 1\). That neighborhood can be chosen with positive distance from the compact collar. On its product with the collar, the pulse derivatives in (16.13) are continuous and bounded. They therefore provide an integrable constant times the collar indicator for each fixed differentiation. Differentiation under the integral is justified. Induction on the jet order and linearity in \(p\) prove

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{F}_{\chi} (j (v ; P_{p, x}, f)) = - p [d \iota_v f ; x] .\) (16.18)

Normalized jets cause no change: the raising factors occur on both sides and are nonzero. This argument applies whenever \(d \iota_v f\) is a Maxwell wave locally, as it is for the affine motions used here.

For completeness, the Bianchi identity expands the variation as

\(\displaystyle (d \iota_v f)_{ab} = \sum_i (v_i \partial_i f_{ab} + (\partial_a v_i) f_{ib} + (\partial_b v_i) f_{ai}) .\)

For \(v = e_b\) this is \(\partial_b f\). For \(v = A (y - x)\), it is the infinitesimal inverse-rotation action on the jet at \(x\). Differentiating the two index factors and the argument in \(e^{tA}_{ia} e^{tA}_{lb} f_{il} (x + e^{tA} z)\) at \(t = 0\), where \(z\) is displacement from the fixed rotation centre \(x\), gives exactly this formula. Orthogonal covariance preserves the Maxwell equations, so this variation is a wave. Translation has the same property by commutation of derivatives. Write \(\mathscr{D}_v p\) for the resulting probe: the raised derivative probe for translation and the angular probe for rotation about \(x\). Equation (16.18) becomes \(- (\mathscr{D}_v p) [f ; x]\).

16.2.3Two contractions at the enclosed point

When both pulses are centred at \(x\), the preceding formula cannot be used with one pulse as a smooth wave across \(x\). Instead use collar independence and homogeneity. For a probe of jet order \(j\),

\(\displaystyle P_{p, x} (x + tz) = t^{- (4 + j)} P_{p, x} (x + z) \qquad (t > 0, z \ne 0),\)

because \(G\) has degree \(- 2\) and the curvature covariance has two derivatives, followed by \(j\) centre derivatives. The drive has degree \(e = 0\) for translation and \(e = 1\) for rotation about \(x\). Replace \(\chi (y)\) by \(\psi (y) = \chi (x + (y - x) / t)\). Both cutoffs equal one near \(x\), so their fluxes agree. In the latter integral set \(y = x + tz\). The volume contributes \(t^4\), the cutoff derivative \(t^{- 1}\), the drive \(t^e\), and the two pulses \(t^{- 8 - j - l}\). Consequently their common flux \(Z\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle Z = t^{e - 5 - j - l} Z.\)

Take \(t = 2\). Since \(e \leqslant 1\) and \(j, l \geqslant 0\), the factor is strictly less than one; hence \(Z = 0\). This proves vanishing without extending the product of the two poles through \(x\).

16.2.4All Wick polynomials and all spectators

The Wick stress polynomial is

\(\displaystyle J^0_{ab} = \sum_c : \hspace{-0.17em} \langle F_{ac}, F_{bc} \rangle \hspace{-0.17em} : - \frac{\delta_{ab}}{4} \sum_{c, d} : \hspace{-0.17em} \langle F_{cd}, F_{cd} \rangle \hspace{-0.17em} : .\) (16.19)

Here the colons mean Wick ordering at that insertion. In Wick moments there are no contractions between factors at the same insertion. Fix distinct positions \(x_0, \ldots, x_n\) and a smooth compact cutoff \(\chi\) equal to one near \(x_0\), whose support excludes every other \(x_i\). Let \(P_i\) be arbitrary Wick polynomials in finitely many jet probes at \(x_i\). Extend \(\mathscr{D}_v\) from probes to polynomials by the Leibniz rule, and denote this derivation by \(\delta_v\). Here \(v = e_b\) for a coordinate direction \(b\), or \(v (y) = A (y - x_0)\) with \(A\) real skew; by linearity the claim then also holds for every affine Killing field. We claim

\(\displaystyle - \sum_{a, b} \int (\partial_a \chi) (y) v_b (y) \left\langle J^0_{ab} (y) \prod_{i = 0}^n P_i (x_i) \right\rangle_0 dy = - \left\langle (\delta_v P_0) (x_0) \prod_{i = 1}^n P_i (x_i) \right\rangle_0 .\) (16.20)

First suppose each \(P_i\) is a monomial. Label its individual factor occurrences, including repeated factors, by a finite set \(I\). Let \(x_{u (h)}\) be the position of occurrence \(h\), with probe \(p_h\), and put \(f_h = P_{p_h, x_{u (h)}}\). For a subset of remaining occurrences \(A\), write \(W (A)\) for its Wick pairing sum, with pair weights zero at the same insertion. Extract the two stress factors from the Wick sum. The exact integrand contracted with \(v_b\) is

\(\displaystyle \frac{1}{2} \sum_{h \ne l} j_a (v ; f_h, f_l) (y) \hspace{0.17em} W (I \setminus \{h, l\}) .\) (16.21)

To verify the factor \(1 / 2\), initially assign the first stress factor to \(h\) and the second to \(l\). The quadratic expression in (16.19) gives one ordered contraction. Exchanging \(h, l\) gives its transpose; their sum is exactly the polarized tensor \(T_{ab} (f_h, f_l)\). Averaging these two equal ordered sums gives (16.21). The stress factors cannot contract to each other because of Wick ordering. After choosing their partners, the remaining pairing sum is exactly \(W (I \setminus \{h, l\})\).

Every term is smooth on the compact collar, so the finite sum can be integrated term by term. There are three cases. If neither partner is at \(x_0\), both pulses are smooth waves on the support of \(\chi\), and the flux vanishes. If both are at \(x_0\), the preceding homogeneous two-pulse argument gives zero. If exactly one, say \(h\), is at \(x_0\), (16.18) gives minus the covariance of \(\mathscr{D}_v p_h\) at \(x_0\) with \(p_l\) at its spectator position. Both orders \((h, l)\) occur, and symmetry cancels the factor \(1 / 2\). For a fixed \(h\) at \(x_0\), sum over its possible partner \(l\) and over the remaining Wick pairings. This is precisely the Wick moment with occurrence \(h\) replaced by \(\mathscr{D}_v p_h\). Summing over all such occurrences is the Leibniz rule for \(\delta_v P_0\), proving (16.20) for monomials. Expand general polynomials in their finite monomial bases. Multilinearity and the linearity of the compact integrals prove the identity for arbitrary complex coefficients and every finite spectator word.

16.2.5The fixed reference normalization

For this final step assume the gauge Lie algebra is nonzero, nonabelian and simple, as in the construction theorem. The preceding collar and Wick calculations used only its finite-dimensional real inner product; the rank and nontriviality assertions below use this additional hypothesis. The polynomial (16.19) is real, symmetric, gauge-invariant and of grade four. Its expectation vanishes by Wick ordering. Its divergence is zero already as a Maxwell jet polynomial: apply (16.14) with \(f = g\) and divide by two. Polynomial differentiation is the same Leibniz rule as the field derivative. Its reference field is nonzero by the explicit stress probes in Subsection 16.1. That section also proves surjectivity of divergence on centered covariant arrays and that its kernel is the line through this tensor. These facts supply the conserved-line certificate used in Subsection 17.3.

We use the following three properties of the reference field table. Their application to the selected reference is justified below.

    (R1)

    Faithfulness: a field label whose separated correlations with every spectator word vanish is zero.

    (R2)

    The reference field labels are the images of the invariant Wick jet polynomials, and this identification preserves Wick moments, sends the raised probe \(\partial_u p\) to the derivative plan in direction \(u\), and sends the rotation variation \(\mathscr{D}_v p\), \(v (y) = A (y - x)\), to the infinitesimal rotation action.

    (R3)

    The canonical reference stress \(J^0\) is the image of the Wick polynomial (16.19).

By (R2) and (R3), (16.20) descends to all field labels. For the reference tensor \(\tau J^0\), \(\tau \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}\), both its translation and rotation Ward maps have the common coefficient \(- \tau\). The sign comes from the collar calculation, not a choice made after comparing with the physical theory.

There is also a useful uniqueness check on the reference packaging. Suppose the same stress field has translation Ward identities with coefficients \(c_0, c_1\) multiplying the same derivative plan. Subtracting the two identities shows that every separated correlation with \((c_0 - c_1) \partial_b v\) vanishes. Faithfulness (R1) gives \((c_0 - c_1) \partial_b v = 0\) for every label \(v\). The derivative plan is nonzero already in grade four, as detected by the nonzero divergence probes in Subsection 16.1; that detection uses the compatibility of the derivative plan with the field derivatives in (R2). Hence \(c_0 = c_1\). In particular, retaining the canonical tensor \(J^0\) and the same derivative plan forces the coefficient to be \(- 1\).

In the native construction the reference is fixed before the weak comparison curve is chosen. By (R3) its canonical tensor has polynomial (16.19): the quadratic coordinate labels map to the corresponding products of curvature probes, their sum gives the first term, and subtracting one quarter of the diagonal sum gives the second. Selecting this tensor does not change the hierarchy, derivative plan, rotation or vacuum of the reference table, so (R1) and (R2) apply to the same data. The uniqueness argument therefore fixes its common Ward coefficient to \(- 1\) on that same data. Combined with the actual conserved-line certificate, this justifies the reference input to the physical normalization step without substituting another comparison model.

Corollary 16.4. (Ward normalization of the selected reference) The reference table used in the selected comparison satisfies (R1)–(R3). Its canonical tensor \(J^0\) has translation and rotation Ward coefficient \(- 1\). The tensor \(- J^0\) has coefficient \(1\).

Proof. Use the reference identification (18.161). It takes a Wick polynomial to its class in the very reference table used for the comparison. The finite separating tests of Theorem 16.6 identify its kernel with the moment-null ideal. Consequently the induced map is faithful, which proves (R1), and its defining moments are the Wick moments. The translation derivation descends to this quotient by the all-spectator argument in Subsection 16.1. Likewise the signed and continuous coordinate actions descend because their changes of variables preserve the moment-null ideal. Differentiating the finite-dimensional tensor-index and jet rotation action gives the rotation derivation, so its action on a polynomial class is exactly the class of that derivation. These are precisely the two identities (18.162), proving (R2). Finally the coordinate formula for the canonical stress is the image of (16.19); the sign of the tensor used for the branch comparison is recorded in (18.163). This proves (R3) without rescaling the reference field space. Apply the polynomial Ward identity and linearity in the stress. The reference dictionary and the free detector use Wick contractions and moment-nullity, not a Ward normalization, so this application is not circular.\(\Box\)

16.3The free radial flux and the weighted Euler operator

The classification of free vector charges also uses dilation about the marked insertion. Its generator preserves weighted grade, so its flux can be used without increasing the spectator capacity. We derive the normalization from the same stress as in Subsection 16.2.

Fix a centre \(x \in \mathbb{R}^4\) and the vector field \(v_x (y) = y - x\). For the polarized stress \(T_{ab} (f, g)\) used there, differentiation of \(j_a = \sum_b (v_x)_b T_{ab}\) gives the trace of the stress plus its contracted divergence. The trace is zero in four dimensions. Thus the current is divergence free wherever both arguments are Maxwell waves. This is the precise reason dilation is allowed even though its matrix is not skew-symmetric.

Let \(p\) be a linear probe of homogeneous jet order \(j \in \mathbb{N}\), let \(f\) be a Maxwell wave on a neighborhood of the support of a smooth compact cutoff \(\chi\), and assume \(\chi = 1\) near \(x\). The regulator calculation proving (16.17) remains valid for \(v_x\): the extra differentiated-drive term is the vanishing stress trace, including for the regulated closed pulse. Differentiation in the enclosed pulse centre, with the drive fixed at \(v_x\) and the cutoff fixed, therefore gives

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{F}_{\chi} (j (v_x ; P_{p, x}, f)) = - p [d \iota_{v_x} f ; x] .\)

The differentiation under the collar integral has the same compact, separated dominator as in (16.18).

The Bianchi expansion of the variation is now

\(\displaystyle (d \iota_{v_x} f)_{ab} (y) = (y - x) \cdot \nabla f_{ab} (y) + 2 f_{ab} (y) .\)

This is again a Maxwell wave: locally it is the derivative at \(s = 0\) of \(e^{2 s} f (x + e^s (y - x))\), and the Maxwell and Bianchi differential equations are preserved by that rescaling. Its homogeneous Taylor coefficient of order \(j\) at \(x\) is \((j + 2)\) times that of \(f\). Indeed the degree-\(j\) coefficient of the rescaled wave is \(e^{(j + 2) s}\) times the original coefficient; differentiating gives the factor, including for the normalized Taylor coefficient \(D^j f / j\)!. Consequently

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{F}_{\chi} (j (v_x ; P_{p, x}, f)) = - (j + 2) p [f ; x] .\) (16.22)

Write \(X_{j, \alpha}\) for the polynomial jet coordinates and define the weighted Euler derivation

\(\displaystyle \mathscr{E} = \sum_{j, \alpha} (j + 2) X_{j, \alpha} \frac{\partial}{\partial X_{j, \alpha}} .\)

The sum acts finitely on every polynomial. On a monomial it multiplies by its weighted degree, and hence preserves every finite weighted filtration. It preserves the gauge-invariant polynomial space since colour rotations mix only coordinates of the same jet order.

Proposition 16.5. (Radial Ward identity for all polynomial words) Let \(x_0, \ldots, x_n\) be distinct points, and let \(P_i\) be arbitrary Wick polynomials of Maxwell jets. Choose \(\chi \in C_c^{\infty}\) equal to one near \(x_0\), with support avoiding \(x_i\) for \(i > 0\). For the same Wick stress \(\textsf{T}_{ab}\) as before,

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{F}_{\chi} \hspace{-0.17em} \left( y \longmapsto \left[ \sum_b (y - x_0)_b \mathcal{M}_{(y, x_0, \ldots, x_n)} (\textsf{T}_{ab}, P_0, \ldots, P_n) \right]_a \right) = -\mathcal{M}_{(x_0, \ldots, x_n)} (\mathscr{E} P_0, P_1, \ldots, P_n) .\) (16.23)

All integrals on the left are absolutely convergent.

Proof. The cutoff derivative has compact support separated from every insertion; the integrand is smooth there. This proves absolute integrability and allows the finite Wick sums to pass through the integral. Extract the two factors in the quadratic stress, as in the proof of (16.20). If neither contracts with \(P_0\), the associated two pulses are smooth throughout the cutoff support, and the divergence-free current has zero flux. If both contract with \(P_0\), collar independence and pulse homogeneity give \(Z = t^{1 - 5 - j - l} Z\) for jet orders \(j, l\) of those two legs: the radial drive is homogeneous of degree one. Taking \(t = 2\) forces \(Z = 0\). This argument uses only the compact collar, not a product of singular pulses through their common centre.

If exactly one stress leg contracts with \(P_0\), apply (16.22) to the other, smooth pulse. Each occurrence of an order-\(j\) coordinate in \(P_0\) acquires the factor \(- (j + 2)\). Summing occurrences is exactly the action of \(- \mathscr{E}\). The two choices of stress leg cancel the factor \(1 / 2\) in the polarized Wick stress, with the same normalization as the preceding polynomial Ward identity. The remaining Wick pairings are those of the word on the right of (16.23). Linearity in all polynomials completes the proof.\(\Box\)

16.4A positive free detector at every jet order

This section proves the all-grade reference-field separation needed in Subsection 17.2 and for the finite point scanner. The point is that positivity must hold for a mixture of derivative orders, not just separately at each order. We establish it by an explicit triangular change of probes. All vector spaces in the one-leg calculation are real; ordinary complex conjugation is added only when passing to the Wick-polynomial pairing.

16.4.1The Kelvin response and a triangular family of probes

Let \(\mathscr{S}_j\) be the finite-dimensional real space of homogeneous degree-\(j\) Maxwell field strengths in four-dimensional Euclidean space, with the fixed gauge-color representation. Thus \(P \in \mathscr{S}_j\) is an antisymmetric two-tensor-valued homogeneous polynomial satisfying the Maxwell differential and algebraic identities. Let \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_F\) denote the positive Frobenius inner product, including the positive color inner product. For a nonzero point \(x\), write

\(\displaystyle r_x = |x|^{- 2}, \qquad I (x) = r_x x, \qquad \vartheta_x = I - 2 r_x \hspace{0.17em} x \otimes x.\)

The matrix \(\vartheta_x\) is the orthogonal reflection that reverses the line through \(x\) and fixes its orthogonal complement. Its induced action on two-tensors is denoted \(\vartheta_x^{(2)}\).

For a symmetric coefficient tensor \(A\) of order \(j\) with two antisymmetric field indices, write componentwise

\(\displaystyle (\mathcal{D}_A f)_{im} (x) = \sum_{(a_1, \ldots, a_j) \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}^j} \frac{1}{j!} D^j f (x) [e_{a_1}, \ldots, e_{a_j}] \hspace{0.17em} A_{im} (e_{a_1}, \ldots, e_{a_j}) .\)

The sum is over ordered derivative tuples; later the two field indices are summed in both orders in the Frobenius pairing. This is the normalized Taylor coefficient convention. The colour pairing is the fixed positive real inner product. Write \(c_j = (- 2)^j / (4 \pi^2)\), the coefficient of the four-dimensional Green function.

We first establish the differentiated Green identity used below:

\(\displaystyle (\mathcal{D}_A G)_{im} (x) = c_j |x|^{- 2 (j + 1)} A_{im} (x), \qquad x \ne 0, \quad A \in \mathscr{S}_j, \qquad G (x) = \frac{1}{4 \pi^2 |x|^2} .\) (16.24)

For \(j = 0\) this is evaluation of the Green function. Suppose it holds at order \(j\) and let \(A\) have order \(j + 1\). For each coordinate direction \(e_a\), freeze the first tensor slot to get \(A_a \in \mathscr{S}_j\). The normalized Taylor coefficient satisfies

\(\displaystyle (\mathcal{D}_A f)_{im} = \frac{1}{j + 1} \sum_a \partial_a (\mathcal{D}_{A_a} f)_{im} .\)

This follows by expanding the ordered derivative tuples: each of the \(j + 1\) possible first slots gives the same symmetric coefficient. Applying the induction hypothesis and differentiating gives

\(\displaystyle (\mathcal{D}_A G)_{im} = \frac{c_j}{j + 1} \left( - 2 (j + 1) |x|^{- 2 (j + 2)} \sum_a x_a (A_a) (x) + |x|^{- 2 (j + 1)} \sum_a \partial_a (A_a) (x) \right) .\)

For completeness, the trace identity here is harmonicity in the derivative slots, not the Maxwell divergence equation alone. Write \(F_{im} (x)\) for the homogeneous field components represented by \(A\). The Maxwell equations give \(\partial_a F_{ai} = 0\) and \(\partial_a F_{im} + \partial_i F_{ma} + \partial_m F_{ai} = 0\). Apply \(\partial_a\) and sum over \(a\) in the second identity. The two terms involving the divergence vanish, so \(\Delta F_{im} = 0\). By the normalized coefficient convention, \((A_a)_{im} = (j + 1)^{- 1} \partial_a F_{im}\); hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_a \partial_a (A_a)_{im} = (j + 1)^{- 1} \Delta F_{im} = 0.\)

Euler's homogeneous identity, or direct contraction of the first slot, gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_a x_a (A_a)_{im} (x) = F_{im} (x) = A_{im} (x) .\)

Since \(c_{j + 1} = - 2 c_j\), this proves (16.24). It is an off-origin identity; no distributional contact term is involved.

The Euclidean Green kernel and its two curls give a response field associated with \(P\). The Kelvin calculation needed here is the following identity, with the normalization fixed by the Green function:

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R} (P) (y) = \text{strength} (j) |y|^{- 4} \vartheta_y^{(2)} P (I (y)), \qquad y \ne 0, \quad \text{strength} (j) = 4 (j + 1) (j + 2) (- 2)^j \text{normal} .\) (16.25)

Here \(\text{normal} = 1 / (4 \pi^2) > 0\) is the scalar in (16.24). In particular,

\(\displaystyle (- 1)^j \text{strength} (j) > 0.\) (16.26)

The sign follows directly from the displayed formula: the factors \(4 (j + 1) (j + 2)\) and \(\text{normal}\) are positive and \((- 1)^j (- 2)^j = 2^j\).

We recall the differential reduction giving this normalization. Put \(R_i (x) = \sum_a x_a P_{ai} (x)\) and \(c_j = (- 2)^j \text{normal}\). The differentiated Green potential is \(c_j |x|^{- 2 (j + 1)} P_{ai} (x)\). The divergence-free equation removes the derivatives falling on \(P\) in its divergence, so the resulting vector is

\(\displaystyle D_i (x) = - 2 (j + 1) c_j |x|^{- 2 (j + 2)} R_i (x) .\)

The curl of the field response is \(2 (\partial_m D_i - \partial_i D_m)\). Closedness, the Bianchi identity, and Euler homogeneity give

\(\displaystyle \partial_i R_m (x) - \partial_m R_i (x) = (j + 2) P_{im} (x) .\)

Also \(\partial_i |x|^{- 2 (j + 2)} = - 2 (j + 2) x_i |x|^{- 2 (j + 3)}\). Substitution yields

\(\displaystyle 4 (j + 1) (j + 2) c_j |x|^{- 2 (j + 2)} [P_{im} (x) - 2| x|^{- 2} (x_i R_m (x) - x_m R_i (x))] .\)

The bracket is the two-index reflection of \(P (x)\). Homogeneity gives

\(\displaystyle |x|^{- 2 (j + 2)} \vartheta_x^{(2)} P (x) = |x|^{- 4} \vartheta_x^{(2)} P (I (x)) .\)

This proves (16.25) off the origin and identifies its coefficient; no contact term is used.

For an orthogonal reflection \(s\), define its full action on a solution by \((s \cdot Q) (v) = s^{(2)} Q (sv)\): it acts on both field indices and every derivative slot. For \(x \ne 0\), let \(\text{Leg}_x (P, Q)\) be the one-leg kernel: the covariance of the probe \(P\) at a point \(y_0\) with the probe \(Q\) at \(y_0 - x\), that is, the second derivatives of the scalar Green kernel at separation \(x\) (first point minus second point) contracted with the dual probes of \(P\) and \(Q\), followed by the two curls. The result does not depend on \(y_0\). The one-leg reflected pairing is

\(\displaystyle B_x^{j, k} (P, Q) = \text{Leg}_x (P, \vartheta_x \cdot Q) .\)

Orthogonal covariance and exchange symmetry of the Green kernel imply

\(\displaystyle B_x^{j, k} (P, Q) = B_x^{k, j} (Q, P) .\)

The use of the reflection in the second slot is essential: this is the one-leg reflected form, not the unreflected covariance.

To triangularize all grades at once, for \(0 \leqslant k \leqslant j\) let

\(\displaystyle \left. T_{j, k}^x P = \frac{\text{strength} (j)}{\text{strength} (k)} \text{Taylor}_k (P (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - I (x)) \right), \qquad P \in \mathscr{S}_j .\)

The Taylor coefficient is taken at the origin, and the translated coefficient is again a homogeneous degree-\(k\) Maxwell solution. Set

\(\displaystyle \Sigma_x P = \sum_{k = 0}^j T_{j, k}^x P \in \mathscr{S}_{< j + 1}, \qquad P \in \mathscr{S}_j .\)

The sum is finite. By the exact Taylor formula for a degree-\(j\) polynomial, for every \(y\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{k = 0}^j \text{strength} (k) (T_{j, k}^x P) (y) = \text{strength} (j) P (y - I (x)) .\) (16.27)

Apply (16.25) term by term and use (16.27). The resulting response is \(\mathcal{R}_x (\Sigma_x P) := \sum_{k = 0}^j \mathcal{R} (T_{j, k}^x P)\). For \(y\) near \(x\), inversion gives

\(\displaystyle I (x + tv) - I (x) = t \hspace{0.17em} V_{x, v} (t), \qquad V_{x, v} (t) = |x + tv|^{- 2} (\vartheta_x v - t|v|^2 I (x)),\)

where \(V_{x, v}\) is smooth near \(t = 0\). Since \(P\) is homogeneous of degree \(j\), the shifted response along this line is \(t^j\) times a smooth function. Consequently its Taylor coefficients through order \(j - 1\) vanish, while its order-\(j\) coefficient is

\(\displaystyle \text{strength} (j) |x|^{- 2 (j + 2)} (\vartheta_x \cdot P) (v),\)

where the reflected solution is defined by \((\vartheta_x \cdot P) (v) = \vartheta_x^{(2)} P (\vartheta_x v)\). To connect the response computation to the one-leg pairing, the two-curl Green identity gives

\(\displaystyle B_x (\Sigma_x P, Q_k) = (- 1)^k \langle \vartheta_x \cdot Q_k, \text{Taylor}_k (\mathcal{R}_x (\Sigma_x P)) (x) \rangle_F .\)

Here \(Q_k \in \mathscr{S}_k\), and the normalized Taylor tensor includes all ordered derivative tuples. The sign \((- 1)^k\) is the sign from differentiating the second insertion, which sits at \(y_0 - x\): the pulse of a grade-\(k\) probe is odd or even under \(y \mapsto - y\) according to the parity of \(k\). The triangular transform below uses this orientation; with the opposite one the sign would attach to the components of the first argument. The vanishing lower Taylor coefficients imply \(B_x (\Sigma_x P, Q_k) = 0\) when \(k < j\); at \(k = j\) the order-\(j\) coefficient gives \(B_x (\Sigma_x P, Q_j) = \lambda_j (x) \langle P, Q_j \rangle_F\), because \((\vartheta_x \cdot Q_j, \vartheta_x \cdot P)\) have the same Frobenius product as \((Q_j, P)\). When the second input is also transformed, its components have grades at most its original grade, and its top component is unchanged because \(T_{k, k}^x\) is the identity. Thus these same two identities give the triangular identities for the full one-leg kernel. Extend \(B_x\) by bilinearity to the finite tower. If \(\mathscr{S}_{< m} = \bigoplus_{k < m} \mathscr{S}_k\), then the formulas

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} B_x (\Sigma_x P, \Sigma_x Q) & = 0 & & (P \in \mathscr{S}_j, Q \in \mathscr{S}_k, k < j), \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.28)} \\ B_x (\Sigma_x P, \Sigma_x Q) & = \lambda_j (x) \langle P, Q \rangle_F & & (P, Q \in \mathscr{S}_j), \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.29)} \end{array}\)

where

\(\displaystyle \lambda_j (x) = (- 1)^j \text{strength} (j) |x|^{- 2 (j + 2)} > 0.\) (16.30)

The factor \((- 1)^k\) in the one-leg kernel comes from differentiating the second insertion; at equal grade it combines with the Taylor coefficient to give precisely (16.30).

Now fix a finite cutoff \(n\) and form the tower

\(\displaystyle \mathscr{S}_{< n} = \bigoplus_{j = 0}^{n - 1} \mathscr{S}_j .\)

On this tower the reflected Gram form is

\(\displaystyle B_x (P, Q) = \sum_{j, k < n} B_x^{j, k} (P_j, Q_k) .\)

Apply the triangular transform \(\Sigma_x\) separately in each input grade. The cross-grade identities (16.28) and their transposes remove every off-diagonal block, while (16.29) gives

\(\displaystyle B_x (\Sigma_x P, \Sigma_x Q) = \sum_{j < n} \lambda_j (x) \langle P_j, Q_j \rangle_F .\) (16.31)

Every coefficient on the right is positive. Since each Frobenius inner product is positive definite, the right side is positive for every nonzero tower. Moreover, \(T_{j, j}^x\) is the identity: the top-degree Taylor coefficient of \(P (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - I (x))\) is \(P\). Therefore the matrix of \(\Sigma_x\) on the ordered grades is lower triangular with identity diagonal. Its strictly grade-lowering part \(N_x = \Sigma_x - I\) satisfies \(N_x^n = 0\), so

\(\displaystyle \Sigma_x^{- 1} = I - N_x + N_x^2 - \cdots + (- 1)^{n - 1} N_x^{n - 1} .\)

Thus the diagonalization is an invertible change of probes, and the full mixed-grade Gram form is positive definite. This is a simultaneous statement about the entire tower; positivity of the separate diagonal blocks alone would not imply it.

16.4.2Wick polynomials and strict reflected positivity

Let \(\mathscr{A}\) be the complex polynomial algebra generated by the formal field-strength jet coordinates at all derivative orders. Evaluation on homogeneous Maxwell solutions restricts each polynomial to the on-shell jet space, imposing both the differential Maxwell equations and the algebraic Bianchi identities. The filtered shell sector \(\mathscr{Q}_{\leqslant d}\) is the image of this restriction map through grade \(d\), hence is canonically the quotient by its kernel. Full reflection preserves each grade and this filtered sector. Each linear coordinate restricted to \(\mathscr{S}_j\) is a linear functional on a finite-dimensional inner-product space, so the finite-dimensional Riesz representation theorem writes it as pairing with a vector of \(\mathscr{S}_j\). Consequently every polynomial in finitely many jet coordinates is a polynomial in the coordinates of one sufficiently large finite tower. A homogeneous solution \(P \in \mathscr{S}_j\) defines its linear Wick symbol \(\textsf{d} (P)\). Products of these symbols generate the polynomial Wick algebra.

For two distinct points \(x, y\), put \(z = x - y\). Let \(\mathcal{W}_{x, y} (A, B)\) denote the free two-point moment of the polynomial insertions \(A\) and \(B\). The reflected pairing is linear in its first argument and conjugate-linear in its second:

\(\displaystyle \langle A, B \rangle_{x, y}^{\text{ref}} =\mathcal{W}_{x, y} (A, \vartheta_z \cdot \bar{B}),\)

where \(\vartheta_z\) acts on every tensor index of the second field insertion. Complex conjugation of polynomial coefficients is a separate operation in this formula. The free moment is the normal-ordered cross-site Gaussian matching moment: only pairings joining the two distinct insertion sites occur. Removing one linear field factor from the first insertion therefore gives the sum of its contractions with the factors in the second insertion. This follows by separating the unique partner of the removed factor in the finite matching formula for a free moment. With two insertion points there is only one possible partner site. In orthogonal coordinates the identity reads

\(\displaystyle \langle X_i p, q \rangle_{x, y}^{\text{ref}} = d_i \langle p, \partial_i q \rangle_{x, y}^{\text{ref}}, \qquad \langle 1, X_i q \rangle_{x, y}^{\text{ref}} = 0, \qquad \langle 1, 1 \rangle_{x, y}^{\text{ref}} = 1.\) (16.32)

The first identity is the differentiated removal rule, and the second is the same rule with no factor available to contract on the first side.

Choose a real orthogonal basis \(e_1, \ldots, e_M\) for the tower Gram form (16.31) at the separation \(z = x - y\), with squared norms \(d_i > 0\). Such a basis exists by finite-dimensional Gram–Schmidt: at each step, the orthogonal complement of the span already chosen is positive definite, so a nonzero vector in it has strictly positive squared norm; normalize or retain its positive norm. In the polynomial coordinates \(X_1, \ldots, X_M\) associated with \(e_i\), the reflected pairing satisfies

\(\displaystyle \langle X^{\alpha}, X^{\beta} \rangle_{x, y}^{\text{ref}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \prod_{i = 1}^M \alpha_i !d_i^{\alpha_i}, & \alpha = \beta,\\ 0, & \alpha \ne \beta . \end{array} \right.\)

For completeness, this formula follows by induction on \(| \alpha |\). When \(\alpha = 0\), the last two identities in (16.32) give \(1\) if \(\beta = 0\) and \(0\) otherwise. If \(\alpha_i > 0\), apply the first identity with \(p = X^{\alpha - e_i}\) and \(q = X^{\beta}\):

\(\displaystyle \langle X^{\alpha}, X^{\beta} \rangle = \beta_i d_i \langle X^{\alpha - e_i}, X^{\beta - e_i} \rangle,\)

where the right side is zero when \(\beta_i = 0\). Induction shows that the pairing vanishes unless \(\alpha = \beta\). When they agree, removing each occurrence of coordinate \(i\) contributes successively \(d_i \alpha_i, d_i (\alpha_i - 1), \ldots, d_i\), so its total factor is \(\alpha_i !d_i^{\alpha_i}\). Multiplication over coordinates gives the formula. For arbitrary polynomials \(p = \sum_{\alpha} p_{\alpha} X^{\alpha}\) and \(q = \sum_{\alpha} q_{\alpha} X^{\alpha}\), it follows that

\(\displaystyle \langle p, q \rangle_{x, y}^{\text{ref}} = \sum_{\alpha} p_{\alpha} \overline{q_{\alpha}} \prod_{i = 1}^M \alpha_i !d_i^{\alpha_i}, \qquad \langle p, p \rangle_{x, y}^{\text{ref}} = \sum_{\alpha} |p_{\alpha} |^2 \prod_{i = 1}^M \alpha_i !d_i^{\alpha_i} .\) (16.33)

Each weight is strictly positive, including the empty multi-index, whose weight is \(1\). Thus the reflected norm is strictly positive for every nonzero polynomial. Every polynomial uses only finitely many jet orders; choose a tower cutoff containing those orders, and the argument applies. If two representatives define the same shell polynomial, their difference restricts to zero on every solution and has zero pairing with every insertion. The formula is therefore well defined on the shell quotient and strictly positive on each nonzero class.

Theorem 16.6. (Strict all-grade free detector) For any two distinct Euclidean points, the reflected free two-point form on the polynomial algebra of gauge-invariant field-strength jets, after quotienting by the Maxwell equations and the Bianchi identities, is strictly positive: if \(P\) is a nonzero quotient class, then

\(\displaystyle \langle P, P \rangle_{x, y}^{\text{ref}} > 0.\)

Consequently, the family of separated two-point evaluations at any fixed pair detects every field in each finite-dimensional grade.

Proof. The Kelvin calculation gives the one-leg triangular identities and the strictly positive tower pivots in (16.28)–(16.30). Equation (16.31) therefore diagonalizes the entire finite tower with positive diagonal entries. The removal-rule calculation gives (16.33), whose summands are nonnegative and whose summand for any nonzero coefficient is positive. Every polynomial lies in a finite tower, proving strict positivity for each nonzero shell class.

For the detector statement, fix a grade \(d\) and a nonzero class \(v\) in its free field space. Choose any separated pair. The reflected evaluation of \(v\) against its reflected conjugate is the strictly positive number just proved. Hence at least one vector in any spanning basis of the grade has a nonzero pairing with \(v\); otherwise linearity would make its pairing with every grade-\(d\) vector, including the reflected conjugate of \(v\), vanish. Thus the full family of basis-column evaluations is injective.\(\Box\)

16.4.3The finite point scanner

We spell out the map used by the construction. Fix a reference frame and a grade \(d\). Choose a basis \(b_0, \ldots, b_m\) of its free field space with \(b_0 = 1\), the scalar unit; the remaining basis elements are images of vectors in that same reference frame. At a separated pair \((x, y)\) define the row scanner

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R}_{x, y} (v) = (\text{Use}_{x, y} (v, b_j))_{j = 0}^m, \qquad \text{Face}_{x, y} (w) = \vartheta_{x - y} (\bar{w}) .\)

Here the bar is ordinary coefficient conjugation, and the reflection acts on all tensor indices. By the definitions of the free-field quotient and the reflected polynomial pairing,

\(\displaystyle \text{Use}_{x, y} (v, \text{Face}_{x, y} (w)) = \langle v, w \rangle_{x, y}^{\text{ref}} .\) (16.34)

If \(\mathcal{R}_{x, y} (v) = 0\), then the bilinear form \(w \mapsto \text{Use}_{x, y} (v, w)\) vanishes on the chosen basis and therefore on the whole grade. In particular, take \(w = \text{Face}_{x, y} (v)\). Equation (16.34) and strict positivity imply \(v = 0\). So the scanner is injective. Notice that it is the conjugate and the reflection together that place the second entry in the reflected positive form; reflection alone or conjugation alone is not the required operation.

The scalar unit and source-frame basis make this an admissible finite scanner for the later construction. The unit is retained as the first column, and the other columns use the same frame from which the free table was built. This proves the all-grade reference detector only. It makes no positivity claim about the interacting measure, and the eventual interacting comparison and replication bounds remain separate inputs.

16.5Erasing the internal Ward kernel in the free theory

The internal defect in a proposed rotation action has two skew-matrix arguments. Its vanishing requires a different test from the classification of vector charges. We prove that test using the free stress and the strict free detector already constructed in Sections 16.2 and 16.4.

Let \(V_d\) be the complex space of gauge-invariant Maxwell jet polynomials of weighted degree at most \(d\), modulo the Maxwell and Bianchi relations. A jet coordinate of order \(r\) has weight \(r + 2\). We identify the inclusions \(V_k \hookrightarrow V_d\) with inclusions of polynomial spaces. Write \(\mathcal{M}_x (P_1, \ldots, P_n)\) for their separated free Wick moment, and \(\rho (g)\) for the action of \(g \in O (4)\) on the jets. The real structure is complex conjugation of the polynomial coefficients and the real jets. For \(A \in \mathfrak{so} (4)\), denote the infinitesimal action by \(L_A\); it preserves each \(V_d\). The stress polynomials \(\textsf{T}_{ab}\) belong to \(V_4\).

Theorem 16.7. (Vanishing of the internal Ward defect) Suppose the gauge group is compact and simple and \(d \in \mathbb{N}\), \(d \geqslant 4\). Let

\(\displaystyle T : \mathfrak{so} (4) \times \mathfrak{so} (4) \longrightarrow \text{End}_{\mathbb{C}} (V_d)\)

be real bilinear. Suppose that \(T (A, A) = 0\), that every \(T (A, B)\) kills the unit and commutes with the real structure, and that it preserves every \(V_k\) with \(0 \leqslant k \leqslant d\). Suppose also that for each determinant-one signed coordinate transformation \(g\),

\(\displaystyle \rho (g) T (A, B) P = T (gAg^{- 1}, gBg^{- 1}) \rho (g) P.\) (16.35)

If for every \(n\), every separated \(x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)\), every \(P_i \in V_d\) and all \(A, B\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i = 1}^n \mathcal{M}_x (P_1, \ldots, T (A, B) P_i, \ldots, P_n) = 0,\) (16.36)

then \(T = 0\).

The quantifier over spectators in (16.36) is essential: the word length is arbitrary, although every label belongs to the same finite grade \(d\). We first work entirely in grade four, and then use the stress flux to reach all of \(V_d\).

16.5.1Changing the direction of a grade-four pairing

Put

\(\displaystyle B_x (P, Q) =\mathcal{M}_{(x, 0)} (P, Q) \qquad (x \ne 0), \qquad h_x = I - 2 \frac{xx^{\textsf{t}}}{|x|^2} .\)

Thus \(h_x\) is the orthogonal reflection in the hyperplane perpendicular to \(x\). On zero-order two-form probes, the covariance (16.12) is

\(\displaystyle C (x) = 4 \nu |x|^{- 4} \hspace{0.17em} \bigwedge^2 h_x \otimes I_{\mathfrak{g}} .\) (16.37)

Here the exterior square acts on the two-form indices, with their usual orthonormal basis \(e_a \wedge e_b\), \(a < b\). To check the coefficient and sign, use orthogonal covariance to take \(x = re_0\). The Hessian of \(\nu |x|^{- 2}\) has diagonal entries \(6 \nu r^{- 4}\) in direction \(0\) and \(- 2 \nu r^{- 4}\) in the other directions. Substitution in (16.12) gives \(- 4 \nu r^{- 4}\) on \(e_0 \wedge e_b\), \(4 \nu r^{- 4}\) on \(e_a \wedge e_b\) with \(a, b \ne 0\), and zero off the diagonal. These are exactly the eigenvalues in (16.37).

Consequently the covariance of \(\rho (h_x) p\) at \(x\) with a zero-order probe \(q\) at \(0\) depends on \(x\) only through \(|x|\). This extends to products of such probes by Wick's rule: same-insertion contractions are zero, and every cross-contraction has the stated invariance. It extends further by linearity to their span. The low-grade decomposition in Subsection 16.1 says that \(V_4\) is spanned by the unit and invariant quadratic products of zero-order curvature probes. We therefore obtain, for \(|x| = |y| > 0\),

\(\displaystyle B_x (P, Q) = B_y (\rho (h_y h_x) P, Q) \qquad (P, Q \in V_4) .\) (16.38)

Indeed apply the preceding Wick identity to \(\rho (h_x) P\), using \(h_x^2 = I\). This argument concerns zero-order probes; it makes no assertion that (16.38) holds on higher jet sectors.

The form \(B_{- re_a}\) is nondegenerate on every \(V_k\) when \(r > 0\). To see this, translate the pair \((- re_a, 0)\) to \((- re_a / 2, re_a / 2)\). If \(P \ne 0\), its reflected conjugate lies in the same \(V_k\), and its pairing with \(P\) is strictly positive by Theorem 16.6. In particular, vanishing of \(B_{- re_a} (P, Q)\) for every \(Q \in V_k\) implies \(P = 0\).

Fix \(A, B\) and abbreviate \(U = T (A, B)\). The two-point case of (16.36) is

\(\displaystyle B_x (UP, Q) + B_x (P, UQ) = 0.\) (16.39)

Choose \(x = - re_a\) and \(y\) with \(|y| = r\), and set \(s = h_x h_y\). Since \(U\) preserves \(V_4\), the identity at \(y\), followed by (16.38), gives

\(\displaystyle B_x (\rho (s) UP, Q) + B_x (\rho (s) P, UQ) = 0.\)

Subtract the identity at \(x\) for \(\rho (s) P, Q\). Nondegeneracy on \(V_4\) then yields

\(\displaystyle \rho (s) UP = U \rho (s) P \qquad (P \in V_4) .\) (16.40)

For \(a \ne b\), take \(y = e_a + e_b\) and \(r = \sqrt{2}\). Direct application of the reflection formula shows that \(s = h_{- re_a} h_{e_a + e_b}\) is the quarter-turn \(q_{ab}\) sending \(e_a\) to \(- e_b\), \(e_b\) to \(e_a\) and fixing the other axes. Thus \(U\) commutes with all these quarter-turns on \(V_4\).

16.5.2An alternating tensor killed by quarter-turns

We use the following elementary fact. If \(W\) is a real vector space and \(F : \mathfrak{so} (4) \times \mathfrak{so} (4) \to W\) is bilinear, alternating and invariant under simultaneous conjugation by every \(q_{ab}\), then \(F = 0\).

Here is a basis proof, which also explains the role of four dimensions. Write \(E_{ab} = e_a e_b^{\textsf{t}} - e_b e_a^{\textsf{t}}\), so that \(E_{ba} = - E_{ab}\). Repeated arguments vanish by alternation. For \(F (E_{ab}, E_{ac})\) with \(a, b, c\) distinct, choose the fourth axis \(l\). The half-turn \(q_{cl}^2\) fixes \(E_{ab}\) and negates \(E_{ac}\). Invariance therefore makes this value equal to its negative, hence zero. Changing the signs or order of indices covers every pair with a common axis. For four distinct indices \(a, b, i, l\), conjugation by \(q_{ai}\) sends \((E_{ab}, E_{il})\) to \((- E_{ib}, E_{al})\), and conjugation by \(q_{bl}\) sends \((E_{ib}, E_{al})\) to \((- E_{il}, E_{ab})\). Hence

\(\displaystyle F (E_{ab}, E_{il}) = - F (E_{ib}, E_{al}) = F (E_{il}, E_{ab}) = - F (E_{ab}, E_{il}) .\)

This value is zero as well. Bilinearity and the fact that the \(E_{ab}\) span \(\mathfrak{so} (4)\) finish the proof.

For \(P \in V_4\), apply this fact to \(F_P (A, B) = T (A, B) P\). Equation (16.40) and signed covariance give

\(\displaystyle T (A, B) \rho (q_{ab}) Q = T (q_{ab} Aq_{ab}^{- 1}, q_{ab} Bq_{ab}^{- 1}) \rho (q_{ab}) Q.\)

The quarter-turn acts bijectively on \(V_4\), so take \(Q = \rho (q_{ab})^{- 1} P\). This proves the invariance required for \(F_P\). We conclude that

\(\displaystyle T (A, B) P = 0 \quad (P \in V_4), \qquad T (A, B) \textsf{T}_{ab} = 0.\) (16.41)

16.5.3The flux commutator at the same spectator capacity

We now prove the step which propagates this conclusion. Let \(U\) be any endomorphism of \(V_d\) with zero Ward rows and \(U \textsf{T}_{ab} = 0\). For a fixed \(K \in \mathfrak{so} (4)\) and \(P \in V_d\), we claim that

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{M}_x ([L_K, U] P, Q_1, \ldots, Q_m) = 0\) (16.42)

for every separated \(x = (x_0, \ldots, x_m)\) and all \(Q_i \in V_d\). Choose a smooth compactly supported cutoff \(\chi\) equal to one near \(x_0\), whose support avoids the other points. Use the rotational vector field \(v (y) = K (y - x_0)\) in the polynomial flux identity (16.20). All its integrals are finite: the weight \(d \chi\) has compact support separated from all insertions, and each moment is smooth there.

Write \(\mathbf{P}= (P, Q_1, \ldots, Q_m)\), and let \(\mathbf{P}^{(i)}\) be this word with \(U\) applied in slot \(i\). At each point on the cutoff collar, apply the zero Ward row for \(U\) to the enlarged word consisting of the stress insertion and \(\mathbf{P}\). This is a permitted row at grade \(d\) because \(d \geqslant 4\). Its term with \(U\) on the stress is zero. The remaining terms say that the sum, over \(i\), of the stress moments with words \(\mathbf{P}^{(i)}\) vanishes. Multiply by \(v_b \partial_a \chi\), sum over \(a, b\), and integrate. The finite sum may be interchanged with the integrals by the compact smoothness just noted. The polynomial flux identity at the distinguished slot \(0\) then gives

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{M}_x (L_K UP, Q_1, \ldots, Q_m) + \sum_{i = 1}^m \mathcal{M}_x (L_K P, Q_1, \ldots, UQ_i, \ldots, Q_m) = 0.\)

On the other hand the zero Ward row applied to the word \((L_K P, Q_1, \ldots, Q_m)\) gives the same equation with \(UL_K P\) in place of \(L_K UP\). This second application stays at grade \(d\), since \(L_K\) preserves \(V_d\). Subtracting proves (16.42).

The commutator \([L_K, U] P\) itself lies in \(V_d\). In (16.42), take one spectator to be its reflected conjugate at an axis-separated point. Strict reflection positivity forces the commutator to vanish. Thus

\(\displaystyle L_K U = UL_K \quad \text{on } V_d .\) (16.43)

The preservation of grade was used twice: to apply the second Ward row and to remove a vector detected by spectators of that same grade. No increase in the spectator capacity is hidden in this argument.

Completion of Theorem 16.7. For \(U = T (A, B)\), (16.41) supplies the stress hypothesis of the preceding argument. Consequently \(U\) commutes with every \(L_K\) on \(V_d\). For fixed \(P\), the functions

\(\displaystyle f (t) = \rho (e^{tK}) UP, \qquad g (t) = U \rho (e^{tK}) P\)

solve \(f' = L_K f\), \(g' = L_K g\) and have the same value at zero. Uniqueness is elementary in finite dimension: the derivative of \(e^{- tL_K} (f (t) - g (t))\) is zero, so this difference is identically zero. Taking \(K = E_{ab}\) and \(t = \pi / 2\) shows that \(U\) commutes with \(\rho (q_{ab})\) on all of \(V_d\). For arbitrary \(P \in V_d\), signed covariance and the surjectivity of \(\rho (q_{ab})\) now make \((A, B) \mapsto T (A, B) P\) quarter-turn invariant, exactly as in grade four. The alternating-tensor argument proves it zero. Since \(P\) was arbitrary, \(T = 0\).\(\Box\)

16.6Separating the harmonic residue of a free collision

A quadratic probe approaching a polynomial insertion can contract once or twice with that insertion. The double contractions are generally more singular. We isolate the single-contraction residue by its harmonicity and its precise radial denominator, rather than by assuming that the unmodified rescaled moment has a limit. This is the collision statement needed for the finite-capacity free detector. All orders, cutoffs and capacities \(r, s, j, k, l, d, M\) used below are nonnegative integers; pole orders satisfy \(r \geqslant 2\).

16.6.1Harmonic poles and radial clearing

For \(z \in \mathbb{R}^4 \setminus \{0\}\) put \(R (z) = |z|^2\). A complex function \(f\) is a harmonic pole of order \(r \geqslant 2\) if it is smooth and harmonic away from zero, satisfies

\(\displaystyle f (tz) = t^{- r} f (z) \qquad (t > 0),\)

and \(R^{r - 1} f\) extends smoothly to all of \(\mathbb{R}^4\). We say that \(f\) can be cleared with \(s\) radial powers if \(R^s f\) has such a smooth extension. The same definitions apply componentwise to finite-dimensional vector-valued functions.

The covariance of a probe of jet order \(k\) with one of order \(s\) is a harmonic pole of order \(4 + k + s\). Indeed, the curvature covariance is obtained by two derivatives and linear combinations of \(G (z) = \nu |z|^{- 2}\), and higher jet probes give further normalized constant-direction derivatives. The function \(G\) is a pole of order two. If \(f\) is a pole of order \(r\) and \(g = R^{r - 1} f\) is its smooth extension, then

\(\displaystyle R^r \partial_u f = R \partial_u g - (r - 1) (\partial_u R) g \qquad (z \ne 0) .\) (16.44)

This gives a smooth extension of the new numerator. Differentiating the scaling identity gives degree \(- (r + 1)\), and constant derivatives commute with the Laplacian. Thus \(\partial_u f\) is a pole of order \(r + 1\). Induction, followed by finite linear combinations, proves the assertion for all probes, including their Taylor normalization factors.

The following strict separation is the essential point.

Lemma 16.8. (One extra radial cancellation forces zero) A harmonic pole \(f\) of order \(r \geqslant 2\) that can be cleared with \(r - 2\) radial powers vanishes away from zero.

Proof. Euler's identity is \(z \cdot \nabla f = - rf\). Since \(\Delta f = 0\), the product rule in four dimensions gives, for \(s \geqslant 0\),

\(\displaystyle \Delta (R^{s + 1} f) = 4 (s + 1) (s + 2 - r) R^s f.\) (16.45)

For completeness, \(\nabla R^a = 2 aR^{a - 1} z\) and \(\Delta R^a = 4 a (a + 1) R^{a - 1}\). The cross term in the product Laplacian is therefore \(4 aR^{a - 1} (z \cdot \nabla f)\); taking \(a = s + 1\) proves the formula.

Suppose \(R^{s + 1} f\) has a smooth extension and \(s + 1 < r - 1\). The coefficient in (16.45) is nonzero. Apply the Laplacian to that extension and divide by the coefficient; this gives a smooth extension of \(R^s f\). Starting at \(r - 2\) and repeating, we obtain a smooth extension \(g\) of \(f\) itself. For fixed \(z \ne 0\), homogeneity now gives

\(\displaystyle f (z) = t^r g (tz) \longrightarrow 0 \qquad (t \downarrow 0) .\)

Thus \(f (z) = 0\). When \(r = 2\), the initial clearing already supplies \(g\), so the same last argument applies directly.\(\Box\)

Products have precisely the weaker clearing property which this lemma excludes for a nonzero harmonic pole. If \(f_1, f_2\) are poles of orders \(r_1, r_2 \geqslant 2\), their product is homogeneous of degree \(- (r_1 + r_2)\) and

\(\displaystyle R^{r_1 + r_2 - 2} f_1 f_2 = (R^{r_1 - 1} f_1) (R^{r_2 - 1} f_2)\)

extends smoothly. Harmonicity of the product is not asserted. Finite sums with the same degree have the same properties.

Lemma 16.9. (Extraction in the presence of product poles) Let \(H\) be a harmonic pole of order \(r \geqslant 2\). Suppose finitely many functions \(W_q\), indexed by nonnegative integers \(q < M\), are homogeneous of degree \(- q\) and can be cleared with \(\max (q - 2, 0)\) radial powers. If for every \(z \ne 0\)

\(\displaystyle \lim_{t \downarrow 0} t^r \left( F (tz) - \sum_{q < M} W_q (tz) \right) = H (z),\) (16.46)

and \(F (tz) = 0\) for all sufficiently small positive \(t\) (with the threshold allowed to depend on \(z\)), then \(H = 0\) away from zero.

Proof. The hypotheses give \(t^r \sum_{q < M} W_q (tz) \to - H (z)\). If the largest index \(q\) is strictly greater than \(r\), multiplication by \(t^{q - r}\) makes this limit zero, while homogeneity shows that the same expression tends to \(W_q (z)\): every smaller exponent has an extra positive power of \(t\). Thus \(W_q\) is zero away from zero and can be deleted. Repeat finitely many times. At the end only indices at most \(r\) remain. Their rescaled limit is \(W_r\), if that index occurs, and zero otherwise. Hence \(H = - W_r\) or \(H = 0\). In the first case \(H\) can be cleared with \(r - 2\) radial powers, so Lemma 16.8 applies.\(\Box\)

16.6.2Contraction derivations at a fixed jet cutoff

We use the free Wick polynomial algebra underlying Subsection 16.2. Let \(X_{s, \alpha}\) be coordinates of the real Maxwell jet space of order \(s\), complexified for polynomial coefficients. The index \(\alpha\) includes colour. Let \(\mathcal{R}_j\) be the subalgebra using coordinates only of orders \(s \leqslant j\). This is a cutoff on jet order, distinct from the weighted grade \(d\), where \(X_{s, \alpha}\) has weight \(s + 2\).

For a linear probe \(p\) of order \(k\), let \(K (p, e_{s, \alpha} ; z)\) be its covariance with the coordinate probe at separation \(z\). Define

\(\displaystyle D_{p, s} (z) P = \sum_{\alpha} K (p, e_{s, \alpha} ; z) \frac{\partial P}{\partial X_{s, \alpha}}, \qquad D_p (z) P = \sum_{s = 0}^j D_{p, s} (z) P \quad (P \in \mathcal{R}_j) .\)

The second expression is the Wick contraction derivation of \(p\) with \(P\). The definitions agree on nested cutoffs because derivatives in unused coordinates vanish. Both derivations preserve \(\mathcal{R}_j\). Each coefficient in \(D_{p, s}\) is a pole of order \(4 + k + s\).

Let \(Q_z\) be any multilinear functional of a finite word of fixed polynomials such that its evaluation on each fixed word is continuous at \(z = 0\). Contracting \(p\) with one slot \(P \in \mathcal{R}_j\) gives

\(\displaystyle \lim_{t \downarrow 0} t^{4 + k + j} Q_{tz} (\ldots, D_p (tz) P, \ldots) = Q_0 (\ldots, D_{p, j} (z) P, \ldots) .\) (16.47)

To verify this, expand the finite sum over \(s, \alpha\). Homogeneity extracts \(t^{- 4 - k - s}\) from the covariance coefficient. The remaining functional converges by continuity. Terms with \(s < j\) acquire a factor \(t^{j - s}\) and vanish, while those with \(s = j\) give the stated limit. The right side, as a function of \(z\), is a harmonic pole of order \(4 + k + j\), being a finite constant linear combination of such poles.

If two probes \(p, q\) of orders \(k, l\) both contract with the same slot, then a fixed linear evaluation \(Q\) of that slot gives

\(\displaystyle Q (D_q (z) D_p (z) P) = \sum_{s, b \leqslant j} \sum_{\alpha, \beta} K (p, e_{s, \alpha} ; z) K (q, e_{b, \beta} ; z) Q \hspace{-0.17em} (\partial_{X_{b, \beta}} \partial_{X_{s, \alpha}} P) .\) (16.48)

The derivations act on polynomial coordinates, not on \(z\), so no covariance coefficient is differentiated here. Every summand is a product pole of order \(8 + k + l + s + b\), hence at most \(8 + k + l + 2 j\). Group the finitely many terms of each degree into \(W_q\). Their radial clearing property is the one proved above. These are the terms which cannot be discarded by a simple collision limit.

16.6.3The two-probe residue and null observables

Fix a separated configuration \(x = (x_0, \ldots, x_n)\), a polynomial \(P \in \mathcal{R}_j\) at \(x_0\), and arbitrary polynomial spectators \(P_1, \ldots, P_n\). Insert the Wick product \(pq\) at \(x_0 + z\), where \(p, q\) have orders \(k, l\) with \(l < k\). Write \(F (z)\) for this enlarged moment and put \(r = 4 + k + j\).

Wick expansion contracts the two probe factors into the original word; they cannot contract with one another at their own insertion. There are four cases. If both hit \(P\), their contribution is exactly (16.48), with \(Q\) the moment of the unchanged spectators. If \(p\) hits \(P\) and \(q\) hits a spectator, the latter contraction is continuous at \(z = 0\), since the spectator point differs from \(x_0\). Apply (16.47) to obtain the order-\(r\) residue. If \(q\) hits \(P\) and \(p\) hits a spectator, the analogous residue has order \(4 + l + j < r\) and therefore disappears after multiplication by \(t^r\). Finally, if both hit spectators, every involved kernel is smooth near \(z = 0\), so this contribution disappears as well. These statements also cover two hits on the same spectator: the polynomial derivatives are finite and both covariance factors stay smooth near zero.

Sum the surviving terms in the second case. The elementary one-leg Wick identity says that summing all contractions of \(q\) with the spectators is the same as adjoining the factor \(q\) to the polynomial at \(x_0\). Hence the residue is

\(\displaystyle H (z) =\mathcal{M}_x (qD_{p, j} (z) P, P_1, \ldots, P_n) .\) (16.49)

All sums are finite. We have proved (16.46), with this harmonic pole \(H\) and the double-hit product poles \(W_q\), for any \(M > 8 + k + l + 2 j\). For each fixed direction \(z \ne 0\), the enlarged configuration is separated for all sufficiently small positive \(t\): \(tz \ne 0\) separates it from \(x_0\), and convergence to \(x_0\) separates it from the finitely many other points.

Call a polynomial \(P\) null at capacity \(d\) if

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{M}_x (P, P_1, \ldots, P_n) = 0\)

for every finite number of spectators, every separated configuration, and all gauge-invariant spectator polynomials of weighted grade at most \(d\). There is no bound on the number of spectators.

To make the two-probe insertion gauge invariant, choose uncoloured linear probes \(p, q\) of orders \(k, l\) and an orthonormal colour basis. Write \(p_c, q_c\) for the probes in colour \(c\). Their quadratic contraction \(\sum_c p_c q_c\) is invariant and has weighted grade \(k + l + 4\). Define the circulation polynomial

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{C}_{j ; p, q} (z) P = \sum_c q_c D_{p_c, j} (z) P.\)

The covariance is the identity in colour times the scalar jet covariance, so changing orthonormal colour basis leaves both this expression and the quadratic insertion unchanged.

Theorem 16.10. (Circulation preserves the null test) Let \(P \in \mathcal{R}_j\) be null at capacity \(d\). If \(l < k\) and \(k + l + 4 \leqslant d\), then for each \(z \ne 0\) the polynomial \(\mathcal{C}_{j ; p, q} (z) P\) is null at capacity \(d\).

Proof. Fix any allowed spectators and separated configuration. Sum the preceding residue expansions over the finite colour basis. The residue is now the moment with first insertion \(\mathcal{C}_{j ; p, q} (z) P\). The enlarged moment \(F (tz)\) is zero for all sufficiently small positive \(t\): reorder its slots so that \(P\) is first and treat \(\sum_c p_c q_c\) as one additional spectator. Its grade is at most \(d\), and all the other spectators were already admissible. This uses only the null condition at the original capacity. Lemma 16.9 therefore annihilates the residue, since \(r = 4 + k + j \geqslant 4\). As the original spectators and configuration were arbitrary, this proves the null test for the circulation at every fixed nonzero \(z\).\(\Box\)

16.7Free external detection, quiet charges, and collinear tests

This section proves three free-theory inputs used in the selected finite-level Ward argument: a uniform finite external detector, the classification of quiet vector charges, and reduction to finitely many collinear tests. Internal Ward-map erasure is treated separately in Subsection 16.5. The arguments here use finite generation of a polynomial ideal, explicit Wick collision limits, the strict all-grade free detector, and the signed symmetries of four coordinates.

16.7.1Polynomial grading and a finite external detector

Write \(X_{r, \alpha}\) for a polynomial coordinate of jet order \(r \in \mathbb{N}_0\); the finite color-slot indices \(\alpha\) and the four coordinate axes carry their fixed finite orderings. Assign weight \(r + 2\) to this coordinate. Let \(\mathcal{R}_j\) be the polynomial subalgebra generated by coordinates of jet order at most \(j\), and let \(\mathcal{S}_D\) be the gauge-invariant observables of weighted degree at most \(D\). Denote by \(\pi_k\) projection to ordinary polynomial length \(k\), counting each coordinate as one factor. For \(D, k \geqslant 0\), define \(\mathcal{H}_{D, k}\) as the linear span of products \(v_1 \cdots v_m\), where each \(v_i \in \mathcal{S}_D\) is homogeneous of ordinary length \(k_i\) and \(\sum_i k_i = k\); the empty product is allowed when \(k = 0\).

An observable \(p\) is null at capacity \(D\) if

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{M} (x ; p, v_1, \ldots, v_m) = 0 \quad \text{for every } m, x \in \text{Conf}_{m + 1}, v_i \in \mathcal{S}_D . \) (16.50)

Here \(\mathcal{M}\) is the free Euclidean moment and \(p\) is the distinguished first insertion. This quantifies over arbitrary finite spectator words, all separated positions, and all spectator observables in the fixed capacity.

Lemma 16.11. (Uniform generation at bounded jet order) For each \(j\) there is \(D_j < \infty\) such that every gauge-invariant \(p \in \mathcal{R}_j\) homogeneous of ordinary length \(k\) belongs to \(\mathcal{H}_{D_j, k}\), for every \(k\).

Proof. There are finitely many coordinates of jet order at most \(j\), so \(\mathcal{R}_j\) is a polynomial ring in finitely many variables and is Noetherian. Let \(I\) be the ideal generated by all positive-length, homogeneous, gauge-invariant elements of \(\mathcal{R}_j\). Choose finite generators \(g_1, \ldots, g_s\) of this ideal from that generating family, and let \(D_j\) bound their weighted degrees. Each \(g_i\) has positive ordinary length \(\ell_i\).

Induct on \(k\). Constants are in \(\mathcal{H}_{D_j, 0}\). For positive length, an invariant homogeneous \(p\) is in \(I\), so write it as a finite sum \(p = \sum_i r_i g_i\). Let \(\text{Av}\) be compact-group averaging. Since \(p\) and \(g_i\) are invariant and averaging commutes with length projection,

\(\displaystyle p = \sum_i \text{Av} (\pi_{k - \ell_i} (r_i)) g_i,\)

where terms with \(\ell_i > k\) are zero. Indeed, projecting \(r_i g_i\) to length \(k\) gives exactly \(\pi_{k - \ell_i} (r_i) g_i\), by additivity of ordinary degree. Each averaged first factor is invariant, homogeneous, and of length \(k - \ell_i < k\). The induction hypothesis puts it in \(\mathcal{H}_{D_j, k - \ell_i}\), and multiplication by \(g_i \in \mathcal{S}_{D_j}\) puts the term in \(\mathcal{H}_{D_j, k}\). This proves the claim with a capacity independent of \(k\).\(\Box\)

Lemma 16.12. (Coalescence of bounded spectators) If \(p\) is null at capacity \(D\) and \(\pi_{\ell} p = 0\) for every \(\ell < k\), then for any \(q \in \mathcal{H}_{D, k}\) and any \(x \ne y\),

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{M} ((x, y) ; \pi_k p, q) = 0.\)

Proof. It is enough to take \(q = v_1 \cdots v_m\) with homogeneous factors of lengths \(k_i\) summing to \(k\). Choose distinct real numbers \(c_i\) and replace the spectator factors by insertions at \(y + tc_i e_0\). For all sufficiently small positive \(t\), these points are mutually distinct and avoid \(x\). The null condition makes the resulting moment zero. Expand into Wick monomials. By hypothesis, the distinguished components of lengths below \(k\) are zero and can be removed before taking any limit. A component of length above \(k\) gives no Wick pairing at all: the distinguished insertion has more than \(k\) legs, while the entire spectator word has only \(k\) legs, and legs at the distinguished insertion cannot pair with one another. For a distinguished monomial of length exactly \(k\), every spectator leg must pair with a distinguished leg. Hence there are no spectator–spectator contractions, at any \(t\). The only kernels in these surviving terms connect \(x\) to a spectator point; they are smooth there because \(x \ne y\), and converge to their values at \(y\) as \(t \downarrow 0\). Their limit is precisely the two-insertion moment \(\mathcal{M} ((x, y) ; \pi_k p, q)\). Since the original moments vanish, this limit is zero. Extend by linearity to \(\mathcal{H}_{D, k}\).\(\Box\)

Lemma 16.13. (Shell reduction at a fixed jet cutoff) Fix a jet cutoff \(j\) and a gauge-invariant polynomial \(p \in \mathcal{R}_j\) null at capacity \(D_j\). Then \(p = 0\).

Proof. Induct on ordinary length \(k\). Suppose the components below \(k\) vanish. The coalescence lemma gives

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{M} ((x, y) ; \pi_k p, q) = 0 \quad (q \in \mathcal{H}_{D_j, k}, x \ne y) .\)

By Lemma 16.11, the time-reflected conjugate \(\Theta \pi_k p\) belongs to \(\mathcal{H}_{D_j, k}\): reflection preserves the jet cutoff, the gauge-invariant sector, and ordinary length. If \(\pi_k p \ne 0\), the strict all-grade free detector (Theorem 16.6, with time reflection and coefficient conjugation) at the separated pair \((- re_0, re_0)\) gives

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{M} ((- re_0, re_0) ; \pi_k p, \Theta \pi_k p) > 0 ;\)

reflection positivity alone would give only \(\geqslant 0\). This contradicts the preceding vanishing. Hence \(\pi_k p = 0\) for every \(k\). The polynomial is a finite sum of these components, so it is zero.\(\Box\)

To remove the fixed-jet restriction, put \(j = \dim \mathfrak{g}\) and \(L = j + 1\). Suppose the gauge-invariant polynomial \(p\) is supported in jet orders at most \(s + 1\). For each color \(c\) choose a lower probe \(q_c\) of order \(c\) and a sheet vector \(w_c\) with \(q_c (w_c) = 1\). These sheets are nonzero: if the order-\(c\) sheet were zero, every order-\(c\) probe would be zero, so the nonzero zero-order coordinate cell (which takes value one on its matching basis two-form) would pair to zero with all such probes at every nonzero displacement, contrary to the all-order separation argument below. The replacement equations have polynomial matrix entries

\(\displaystyle M_{ci} = m_{ci},\)

where \(m_{ci}\) is the coordinate polynomial obtained by evaluating the order-\(c\) probe \(q_c\) on the field coordinate of color \(i\) at jet order \(c\); \(i\) ranges over the color slots of the order-\((s + 1)\) derivative. Its determinant is not the zero polynomial. To see this, evaluate the Taylor tower by putting \(w_c\) in the matching slot of color \(c\) and zero in every other color slot. The matrix becomes the identity. This specialization proves that its determinant polynomial is nonzero; it does not claim that the matrix is invertible at every field value.

Fix any upper probe of order \(L\) and any nonzero displacement. Let \(R_i\) be the finite linear combination of order-\((s + 1)\) partial derivatives whose coefficients are the free two-point pairings of that upper probe with the color-\(i\) jet coordinates. The circulation equations for the chosen \(q_c\) state that \(MR = 0\) as a polynomial identity. Multiplying by the adjugate gives the determinant times each component equal to zero. The polynomial coordinate ring is an integral domain, so each component \(R_i\) vanishes. This holds for every upper probe and every nonzero displacement. We use the following separation fact at all jet orders: if a jet functional of order \(h\) pairs to zero at every nonzero displacement with every probe of order \(\ell\), it is zero. Induct on \(\ell\). At \(\ell = 0\), its color-resolved pulse vanishes off the insertion by the free-kernel formula. Testing the single-charge radial flux identity (16.22) against arbitrary homogeneous Maxwell waves of order \(h\) gives \(- (h + 2)\) times its value on each order-\(h\) jet, hence all coefficients vanish. For \(\ell > 0\), fix a probe \(q\) of order \(\ell - 1\) and let \(K (x)\) be its pairing with the order-\(h\) functional. Every directional derivative of \(K\) is a pairing against a raised probe of order \(\ell\), so it vanishes by hypothesis. The kernel's homogeneity gives the Euler identity \(x \cdot \nabla K (x) = - (4 + h + \ell - 1) K (x)\). Its coefficient is nonzero, so \(K\) vanishes at every nonzero \(x\); the induction hypothesis now applies. Therefore the color-resolved two-point functionals, indexed by every nonzero displacement and every upper probe of the chosen order, span the full dual of the order-\((s + 1)\) jet space. Applying this spanning statement to each color slot shows that every ordinary partial derivative in an order-\((s + 1)\) variable vanishes. In characteristic zero, \(p\) is independent of those variables and lies in the lower jet shelf.

Suppose the gauge-invariant polynomial \(p\) is null at capacity \(D_{\ast}\) and lies in weighted shell \(w + 1\). Whenever the highest jet order satisfies \(s + 1 > j\), test the circulation with each lower probe of order \(\ell < j\); then \(\ell < s + 1\), as required for weighted-degree lowering. The upper probe has order \(L = j + 1\), so the separate collision condition \(\ell < L\) also holds. The mixed-collision nullity theorem (Subsection 16.6, Theorem 16.10) now applies: the current polynomial has jet cutoff \(s + 1\) and is a ghost at capacity \(D_{\ast}\); the circulation uses upper probe order \(k = L\), lower order \(\ell\), nonzero displacement, and satisfies \(\ell < L\) and \(L + \ell + 4 \leqslant D_{\ast}\). It therefore remains a ghost at capacity \(D_{\ast}\). The admissibility bound \(L + \ell + 4 \leqslant D_{\ast}\), with \(L = j + 1\) and \(\ell < j\), is exactly what allows this upper and lower probe in the extra test. Each term replaces a coordinate of weight \(s + 3\) by one of weight \(\ell + 2\), so the circulation lies in weighted shell \(w\). Strong induction on \(w\) therefore makes it zero. The top-shelf elimination then applies.

Iterating lowers the jet shelf to the fixed order \(j\). If \(r_j\) is the host bound from finite generation, take

\(\displaystyle D_{\ast} = \max \{r_j, \hspace{0.17em} 4 + j + (j + 1)\} .\)

The second term dominates \((j + 1) + \ell + 4\) for all lower probe orders \(\ell < j\). The fixed-shelf argument above then annihilates the polynomial. This proves the following uniform result.

Theorem 16.14. (Uniform external detection) For a compact simple gauge group there is a finite \(D_{\ast}\) such that every gauge-invariant observable in every finite shell which is null at capacity \(D_{\ast}\) is zero.

16.7.2Quiet charges and collinear tests

For a four-component vector of linear operators \(A = (A_a)_{a = 1}^4\) on the finite-level field space, say that \(A\) is quiet at capacity \(D\) if, for each component \(a\) and every separated word with labels in \(\mathcal{S}_D\), the sum of moments obtained by applying \(A_a\) to the label in each slot and summing over the slots vanishes. Write \(\partial_a\) for the canonical translation derivative, raising jet order by one and extended by the Leibniz rule.

Theorem 16.15. (Classification of quiet charges) Let \(\mathcal{P}_k\) be the finite field space at level \(k\), and let \(A = (A_a)_a\) be a four-component charge in the specified vector-spin subspace. Thus \(A_a (\mathcal{P}_k) \subseteq \mathcal{P}_{k + 1}\) for every \(k \leqslant d\), the maps have the signed-coordinate covariance and reality conditions of the vector representation, and they annihilate the unit. If \(d \geqslant \max \{4, D_{\ast} \}\) and the charge is quiet at capacity \(d\), then there is one real scalar \(c\) with \(A_a = c \partial_a\) for all four axes.

Proof. Let \(S_0\) and \(S_1\) be the real sheet spaces of zero-jet and one-jet curvature coordinates, and put \(V_i = S_i^{\ast}\). If \(p\) is a linear functional on a sheet, write \(\phi_{r, c} (p)\) for its color-\(c\) field coordinate and let \(\textsf{Q} (p, q)\) denote the color-contracted quadratic \(\sum_c \phi_{r, c} (p) \phi_{s, c} (q)\), with the jet orders indicated by the spaces of \(p, q\). The degree-four shell is the direct sum of the unit and the span of \(\textsf{Q} (p, q)\) for \(p, q \in V_0\). The degree-five shell is the sum of that degree-four shell and the mixed products \(\textsf{Q} (p, q_1)\) with \(p \in V_0\), \(q_1 \in V_1\). The charge kills the unit. By the vector filtration and opposite-frame parity, its response to a degree-four quadratic stress label lies in the mixed degree-five subspace. It is real because the charge preserves the real structure. Each mixed product is real; taking the real part of a complex linear combination shows that a real element of their complex span lies in their real span. The mixed products therefore represent that response by a real linear panel \(D_{a, p} : S_0 \to V_1\): in a basis \((e_{\alpha})\) of \(S_0\) with dual basis \((e^{\alpha})\), its mixed polynomial is \(\sum_{\alpha} \textsf{Q} (e^{\alpha}, D_{a, p} (e_{\alpha}))\). This expression is basis-independent because it is the contraction of a linear map with the identity tensor. The representation is unique: evaluate the polynomial on a Taylor tower with independently prescribed order-zero and order-one sheets in a fixed common color slot and all other slots zero. The value is exactly \(D_{a, p} (X) (Y)\), so vanishing of the polynomial forces every panel coefficient to vanish. This constructs the finite-level panel without assuming any formula for the charge on higher shells.

Write \(\mathcal{M}_3 (x, y, z ; A, B, C)\) for the free moment with labels \(A, B, C\) inserted at \(x, y, z\). The free six-leg Wick computation used here is the following identity:

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} & \mathcal{M}_3 (x, y, z ; \textsf{Q} (a, v), \textsf{Q} (p, p), \textsf{Q} (q, q))\\ & \quad = 4 \dim (\mathfrak{g}) (F (a, p ; x - y) F (v, q ; x - z) + F (a, q ; x - z) F (v, p ; x - y)) F (p, q ; y - z) . \end{aligned} \ \) (16.51)

It follows by pairing the two legs at the first insertion with the two legs at the other insertions in the two possible assignments; the last pair joins the remaining sites. The color deltas leave one free color sum, and the two choices of the leg within each quadratic give the factor \(4\). For a general panel, expand in a basis of \(S_0\) and use multilinearity. Choose the third quadratic with label \(w\) so that \(F (q, w ; - z) = q (X)\) for every \(q \in V_0\). To see this explicitly, write \(R_z\) for the invertible exterior-square action of the House reflection associated with \(z\) (so \(R_{- z} = R_z\)), and put \(\kappa_z = 4 \nu \lVert z \rVert^{- 4} > 0\), where \(\nu = (4 \pi^2)^{- 1}\) is the positive free-kernel normalization. The zero-jet covariance is

\(\displaystyle F (q, \text{cell}_{ij} ; z) = \kappa_z \hspace{0.17em} q (R_z \text{blade}_{ij}) .\)

For given \(X\), set \(W = R_z^{- 1} X\) and define

\(\displaystyle w = \kappa_z^{- 1} \sum_{i, j} \frac{1}{2} \text{cell}_{ij} (W) \hspace{0.17em} \text{cell}_{ij} .\)

The coordinate resolution \(W = \sum_{i, j} \frac{1}{2} \text{cell}_{ij} (W) \text{blade}_{ij}\) then gives \(F (q, w ; z) = q (X)\) for every \(q\). Thus this third quadratic exists for each \(X \in S_0\) and nonzero \(z\).

Fix a vector component \(a\) and abbreviate \(D_p = D_{a, p}\). Insert the three quadratic probes at \(tu, 0, z\), multiply their moment by \(t^5\), and let \(t \downarrow 0\). The identity above gives the first near-collision limit. Exchanging the first two insertion points gives a second near-collision limit with \(p, q\) interchanged and displacement \(- u\); the one-jet/zero-jet kernel is odd, so this term has the opposite sign. The third placement has its first insertion at the fixed point \(z\) and its scaled limit is zero. Every separated three-point Ward row vanishes, so the sum of these limits is zero. Cancelling the common nonzero factor \(4 \dim (\mathfrak{g})\) gives, for every \(X \in S_0\) and every nonzero separation \(u\),

\(\displaystyle F (D_p (X), q ; u) \hspace{0.17em} q (X) = F (D_q (X), p ; u) \hspace{0.17em} p (X),\)

where \(F (v, q ; u)\) is the free pairing for \(v \in V_1\), \(q \in V_0\) at separation \(u \ne 0\). If \(p (X) = 0\), the identity gives \(F (D_{a, p} (X), q ; u) = 0\) for every \(q\) with \(q (X) \ne 0\) and every nonzero \(u\). For \(X \ne 0\), these \(q\) form the complement of a hyperplane and span \(V_0\), so linearity extends the equality to every upper probe. For \(X = 0\) the panel already vanishes by linearity. We justify the separation used here and below. If \(v \in V_1\) pairs to zero with every zero-jet probe at every nonzero displacement, the free-kernel formula shows that the color-resolved pulse of \(v\) vanishes componentwise away from its marked point. Test the single-charge radial-flux identity (16.22) against an arbitrary order-one sheet \(A \in S_1\), represented by its homogeneous Maxwell wave, using a compact cutoff equal to one near the marked point. The flux vanishes because the pulse vanishes on the support of the cutoff gradient. Since \(v\) has jet order one, the radial identity evaluates the same flux as \(- 3\) times the pairing of \(v\) with \(A\) at the marked point. Since \(A\) is arbitrary in \(S_1\), this gives \(v = 0\). Thus the free pairings over all nonzero displacements have zero annihilator and span \(V_1^{\ast}\). In particular \(D_{a, p} (X) = 0\) on \(\ker p\). For \(p \ne 0\), choose \(X_0\) with \(p (X_0) = 1\) and define \(f_a (p) = D_{a, p} (X_0)\). Linearity of the panel then gives \(D_{a, p} (X) = p (X) f_a (p)\) for every \(X\). Set \(f_a (0) = 0\). Thus \(D_{a, p} = p \otimes f_a (p)\) and \(A_a (\textsf{Q} (p, p)) = \textsf{Q} (p, f_a (p))\). The Taylor-tower extraction above shows this panel representation is unique. For nonzero \(p, q\), choose \(X\) with \(p (X) q (X) \ne 0\) and cancel these scalar factors in the triangular identity; when either probe is zero, the resulting identity is immediate. The coefficient identity becomes the reciprocity relation

\(\displaystyle F (f_a (p), q ; z) = F (f_a (q), p ; z), \qquad z \ne 0.\)

For fixed \(a\), reciprocity and bilinearity of \(F\) imply additivity: for every \(r, z\),

\(\displaystyle F (f_a (p + q) - f_a (p) - f_a (q), r ; z) = 0.\)

The same computation with \(p\) replaced by \(tp\) gives real homogeneity. The free two-point functionals over all nonzero displacements separate \(V_1\), so \(f_a\) is real-linear. Signed-coordinate covariance gives

\(\displaystyle \rho_s f_a (p) = \varepsilon (s, a) f_{sa} (\rho_s p),\)

with \(\rho_s\) the signed action and \(\varepsilon (s, a)\) its coordinate sign.

Here is the finite coefficient determination. Let \(C_{ij}\) be the six coordinate two-forms in \(V_0\) and let \(D_{a ; ij}\) be their one-jet curls in \(V_1\). Signed-reflection parity allows only \(D_{0 ; 01}, D_{2 ; 12}, D_{3 ; 13}\) in \(f_0 (C_{01})\). \(\displaystyle D_{2 ; 12} + D_{3 ; 13} = D_{0 ; 01}\)

therefore gives \(f_0 (C_{01}) = AD_{0 ; 01}\). For \(C_{12}\), signed-reflection parity first allows exactly

\(\displaystyle D_{0 ; 12}, \quad D_{1 ; 02}, \quad D_{2 ; 01} .\)

The negative eigenspace of the swap of axes \(1, 2\) then makes the last two occur as their difference. The Bianchi identity

\(\displaystyle D_{1 ; 02} - D_{2 ; 01} = D_{0 ; 12}\)

then gives \(f_0 (C_{12}) = BD_{0 ; 12}\). Evaluate the reciprocity relation at the nonzero cross-seed \(z = e_0 + e_2\). Since the \(C_{ij}\) form a basis, this proves \(f_0 = A \nabla_0\), where \(\nabla_0\) is the zero-to-one-jet derivative insertion; covariance gives \(f_a = A \nabla_a\) for every axis. The Leibniz rule gives

\(\displaystyle \partial_a \textsf{Q} (p, p) = 2 \textsf{Q} (p, \nabla_a p),\)

so the original charge is \(c \partial_a\) on the whole degree-four shell, with \(c = A / 2\). Its unit rule supplies the constant component. Subtract \(c \partial_a\) and call the remainder \(B_a\). \(\displaystyle \Gamma (B_a p) - B_a (\Gamma p)\)

has zero Ward rows at the same external capacity. The required dilation Ward identity is the radial flux formula of Subsection 16.3, equation (16.23). On a monomial \(X^{\nu}\), \(\Gamma\) has eigenvalue \(m = \sum_{r, \alpha} (r + 2) \nu_{r, \alpha}\). Opposite-coordinate reflection has eigenvalue \((- 1)^{\sum r \nu_{r, \alpha}}\).   1. At every level \(d\) there is a finite family of collinear scene tests whose simultaneous kernel on the space of charges is exactly the quiet-charge subspace.

  2. If \(d \geqslant \max \{4, D_{\ast} \}\), the simultaneous kernel of such a family on the vector-spin subspace of Theorem 16.15 is exactly \(\mathbb{R} \partial\).

For sufficiently small \(\delta > 0\), the perturbed projections \(q_i = x_i \cdot e_b + \delta \hspace{0.17em} \text{rank} (i)\) are pairwise distinct and preserve every previous strict inequality. Put \(y_i = q_i e_b\). For each pair, its projected difference along the segment from \(y\) to \(x\) keeps the same strict sign until the endpoint; at the endpoint the original configuration is separated. Thus the segment stays in configuration space. The moment is analytic on the separated configuration space. If all derivatives at the collinear endpoint vanish, its Taylor series vanishes near that endpoint, and the one-variable identity theorem propagates vanishing along the segment. Thus all separated Ward rows vanish, which is quietness.

An arbitrary intersection of kernels on a finite-dimensional space is already the intersection of a finite subfamily: add a functional whenever the current intersection is too large. Its dimension strictly drops, so after at most \(\dim V\) additions the process stops. The selected finite scene family has the required kernel, proving (1); no level threshold is used.

The clock purchase makes \(I_k \to \infty\), so \(\epsilon_k \to 0\). The empty coefficient is the unaltered scalar normalization and is not subject to this estimate. In the two-marker coefficient the singleton subtractions are retained; replacing it by an unsubtracted two-point observable would not give (16.53).

16.8.3Solving the normalization equations exactly

The reference transport estimates give a number \(D_k > 0\), independent of \(N \geqslant k\) and \(p\), such that

\(\displaystyle |v|_1 \leqslant D_k |A_{k, N, p} v|_1 .\) (16.54)

Explicitly one may take

\(\displaystyle D_k = a_k^d \prod_{u = 0}^{k - 1} C_{u + 1} L_u^d,\)

where \(C_{u + 1} \geqslant 1\) is the purchased front-column bound. This follows by applying that bound successively from the normalized reference level to \(k\); the extra earlier factors are at least one. The factor \(a_k^d\) undoes the normalization in \(A_{k, N, p}\).

For brevity suppress \(k, N, p\) in this paragraph. By the triangle inequality and (16.52),

\(\displaystyle |Av|_1 \leqslant 2 |Tv|_1 .\)

If \(Tv = 0\), (16.54) implies \(v = 0\). Thus \(T\) is injective and hence bijective on the finite-dimensional space \(V_d\). This also proves the inverse estimate \(|v|_1 \leqslant 2 D_k |Tv|_1\).

Each coordinate of \(V_d\) has two nonnegative integer degrees: a position degree and a factor degree. Denote multiplication by \(\rho\) to the first degree by \(W_{\rho}\), and multiplication by \(t\) to the second by \(S_t\). They are distinct diagonal maps. Put

\(\displaystyle t_{k, N, p} = \frac{\beta_{N, p, k}^{- 1 / 2}}{\theta_k} > 0, \qquad \rho_k =\mathcal{M}_k^b,\)

where \(\theta_k\) is the original clock threshold, \(\mathcal{M}_k\) its monitor and \(b > 0\) the fixed comparison exponent. For every \(i\) solve

\(\displaystyle S_{t_{k, N, p}} T_{k, N, p} u_i = W_{\rho_k} v_i .\) (16.55)

There is exactly one solution, since both maps on the left are invertible. Because all the maps commute with conjugation and \(v_i\) is real, conjugating the equation and using uniqueness shows \(\bar{u}_i = u_i\). Both diagonal maps fix \(e_0\), so uniqueness and \(Te_0 = e_0\) give \(u_0 = e_0\). All columns are therefore normalized simultaneously, with their actual unit and real structure.

The same equations give a bound independent of the two cutoffs at fixed \(k\). The coupling band and the clock identity \(\theta_k = \hat{\beta}_k^{- 1 / 2} \mathcal{M}_k\) imply

\(\displaystyle t_{k, N, p}^{- 1} = \theta_k \sqrt{\beta_{N, p, k}} \leqslant 2\mathcal{M}_k .\)

Since all factor degrees are nonnegative,

\(\displaystyle |T_{k, N, p} u_i |_1 = |S_{t_{k, N, p}^{- 1}} W_{\rho_k} v_i |_1 \leqslant |S_{2\mathcal{M}_k} W_{\rho_k} v_i |_1 .\)

Together with the inverse estimate this bounds every \(|u_i |_1\) by

\(\displaystyle B_k = 2 D_k \sum_{i = 0}^r |S_{2\mathcal{M}_k} W_{\rho_k} v_i |_1 .\) (16.56)

No uniform bound in \(k\) is asserted or needed. For the finitely many mesh cutoffs preceding the common eventual threshold, define every lift to be \(e_0\). Enlarging \(B_k\) by \(|e_0 |_1\) makes the bound valid for all \(N, p\), without changing any eventual marked estimate.

16.8.4Selecting a sequence and taking its ordered limits

Let \(\ell (k)\) denote the lower end of the comparison window. On the admissible comparison levels the calendar is constant in stage throughout \([\ell (k), k]\). Its depth tends to infinity, and its physical test scale is

\(\displaystyle \sigma_k = a_k \mathcal{M}_k^b \longrightarrow 0.\)

The generic lattice choices for each fixed measurement configuration converge, after division by \(\sigma_k\), to that configuration. Their physical coordinates lie in the middle half of a cell at level \(\ell (k)\); in particular they have the fixed relative boundary margin \(1 / 4\). These are the geometric fit and tuning conditions purchased for the finite configurations. They hold eventually at admissible levels.

Intersect the unbounded set of jointly funded comparison levels with these eventual conditions and with the eventual normalization conditions of Subsection 14.19. For any \(k_0, \ell_0\) and \(\eta > 0\) there remains a level \(k\) with

\(\displaystyle k > k_0, \qquad \ell (k) > \ell_0, \qquad \sigma_k < \eta .\)

For the middle inequality, choose a stage later than \(J (\ell_0)\). The plateau property forces its lower window end past \(\ell_0\). The other two inequalities follow from cofinality and \(\sigma_k \to 0\). Choose \(k_0\) in this set, and recursively choose \(k_{n + 1}\) with

\(\displaystyle k_{n + 1} > k_n, \qquad \ell (k_{n + 1}) > \ell (k_n), \qquad \sigma_{k_{n + 1}} < \sigma_{k_n} .\)

The physical scales are strictly decreasing and tend to zero, because \(k_n \to \infty\). The error bounds \(\epsilon_{k_n}\) also tend to zero; their monotonicity is unnecessary. Choose the simultaneous normalized lifts just constructed at each \(k_n\). Their cut-off uniform bounds are those of (16.56).

At each \(n\) and all sufficiently large \(N\), uniformly for every period, the insertion stencils lie in the comparison cell and every nonempty corrected coefficient has the root bound (16.53). There are only finitely many rows and columns at fixed \(d\), so their thresholds have a common maximum. The resulting sequence thus supplies the bounded lifts, geometry and moment estimates needed for the common extraction of Subsection 16.12.

Include all these bounded lift sequences, for every grade and comparison index, in that same countable extraction. First take the mesh limit for each fixed period, then the period limit. Denote the limiting lifts by \(u_i^{(n)}\). Reality and the unit identity survive these limits. The finite-cell moment inequality and the corrected root bound give, for every row and column,

\(\displaystyle | S (\sigma_{k_n} x^{(n)} ;, u^{(n)}) - S_{\text{free}} (x^{(n)} ;, q) | \leqslant P_n \epsilon_{k_n} .\) (16.57)

Here \(S\) is the extracted interacting correlation, \(q\) lists the free basis elements used by the row, and \(x^{(n)}\) is its rescaled lattice configuration. The factor \(P_n\) is the finite polynomial in the absolute free subset moments appearing when the connected subtractions are undone. It is bounded: \(x^{(n)}\) tends to the fixed generic configuration, all its subsets remain separated there, and the free correlations are continuous on those domains. Hence the right side tends to zero.

To justify the order of this passage, fix \(n\) first. Its root bound holds for every period and all mesh cutoffs past one threshold. Apply the finite-cell inequality at those cutoffs; joint convergence of the lifts and correlations gives the inequality after the mesh limit at each period, and then after the period limit. Only then let \(n \to \infty\). There is no interchange between the comparison-scale limit and either finite-volume limit. Equation (16.57) is exactly the finite measurement convergence used in the rank argument. Identification of the entire equation kernel additionally requires the all-spectator local equation comparison stated in that argument; finite one- and two-point measurements alone do not supply it.

Lemma 16.19. (Reduction of the pin) Fix \(k, N, p\) and a grade \(d' \leqslant d\), and constants \(D \in \mathbb{N}\), \(B \geqslant 1\), \(c \geqslant 1\), \(0 < t \leqslant 1\) and \(0 \leqslant \varepsilon \leqslant 1\). Suppose that for every label \(u\) of grade \(d'\) the following hold.

  1. \(\mathcal{H}u =\mathcal{N}_K p_u\), where \(p_u\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(D\) in the reader variables \(y_i\) with \(|p_u |_1 \leqslant B |A_{k, N, p} u|_1\). Here \(\mathcal{N}_K = \exp (- \frac{1}{2} \Delta_K)\), with \(\Delta_K = \sum_{i, j} K_{ij} \partial_{y_i} \partial_{y_j}\), is normal ordering with respect to a symmetric matrix \(K\) whose entries satisfy \(|K_{ij} | \leqslant \varepsilon\).

  2. In the rescaled frame coordinates, the actual reader substitution sends \(y_i\) to \(\ell_i + ta_i\). Here \(\ell_i\) is linear, every monomial of \(a_i\) has degree at least \(2\), and \(| \ell_i |_1 \leqslant B\), \(|a_i |_1 \leqslant B\).

  3. The reading \(\Lambda\), grade truncation followed by passage to frame coefficients, satisfies \(| \Lambda q|_1 \leqslant c |q|_1\).

  4. Pre-pin. \(|A_{k, N, p} u - \Lambda (p_u \circ (\ell + ta)) |_1 \leqslant \varepsilon |A_{k, N, p} u|_1\).

Let \(Tu = \Lambda ((\mathcal{H}u) \circ \ell)\) be the reading of (T1). Then

\(\displaystyle |A_{k, N, p} u - Tu|_1 \leqslant (\varepsilon + cB \hspace{0.17em} a_D) \hspace{0.17em} |A_{k, N, p} u|_1, \qquad a_D = DtB (2 B)^D + \varepsilon \hspace{0.17em} w_D B^D, \quad w_D = \tfrac{D^2}{2} e^{D^2 / 2} .\)

In particular, if \(\varepsilon + cBa_D \leqslant \frac{1}{2}\), then the estimate in (T2) holds at grade \(d'\).

Proof. Write \(A = A_{k, N, p}\) and \(p = p_u\). Three bounds are used, each for a polynomial \(q\) of degree at most \(D\).

Linear substitution. A monomial of degree \(m \leqslant D\) is sent to a product of \(m\) linear forms, of norm at most \(B^m \leqslant B^D\). Hence \(|q \circ \ell |_1 \leqslant B^D |q|_1\).

Nonlinear correction. For a monomial \(y_{i_1} \cdots y_{i_m}\), telescoping gives

\(\displaystyle \prod_{r = 1}^m (\ell_{i_r} + ta_{i_r}) - \prod_{r = 1}^m \ell_{i_r} = \sum_{r = 1}^m \prod_{r' < r} (\ell_{i_{r'}} + ta_{i_{r'}}) \hspace{0.27em} ta_{i_r} \prod_{r' > r} \ell_{i_{r'}} .\)

Each summand has norm at most \((2 B)^{m - 1} tB\), since \(t \leqslant 1\). Hence \(|q \circ (\ell + ta) - q \circ \ell |_1 \leqslant DtB (2 B)^D |q|_1\).

Normal ordering. For a monomial \(y^{\alpha}\) of degree \(m\), \(\partial_i \partial_j y^{\alpha} = \alpha_i (\alpha_j - \delta_{ij}) y^{\alpha - e_i - e_j}\) vanishes unless \(i, j\) lie in the support of \(\alpha\). So \(| \Delta_K y^{\alpha} |_1 \leqslant \varepsilon \sum_{i, j} \alpha_i \alpha_j = \varepsilon m^2\), independently of the number of variables. Since \(\Delta_K\) lowers the degree, \(| \frac{1}{2} \Delta_K q|_1 \leqslant \frac{1}{2} \varepsilon D^2 |q|_1\) on polynomials of degree at most \(D\). Summing the exponential series,

\(\displaystyle |\mathcal{N}_K q - q|_1 \leqslant (e^{\varepsilon D^2 / 2} - 1) |q|_1 \leqslant \varepsilon w_D |q|_1,\)

by \(e^x - 1 \leqslant xe^x\) with \(x = \varepsilon D^2 / 2 \leqslant D^2 / 2\). Also \(\mathcal{N}_K q\) has degree at most \(D\).

Now

\(\displaystyle Au - Tu = [Au - \Lambda (p \circ (\ell + ta))] + \Lambda (p \circ (\ell + ta) - p \circ \ell) + \Lambda ((p -\mathcal{N}_K p) \circ \ell) .\)

By the pre-pin estimate, the first term has norm at most \(\varepsilon |Au|_1\). By item 3 and the two bounds above, the others have norm at most \(c \hspace{0.17em} DtB (2 B)^D |p|_1\) and \(cB^D \varepsilon w_D |p|_1\). Since \(|p|_1 \leqslant B |Au|_1\), the claim follows.\(\Box\)

The polynomial pre-pin and its normalization.
The primary coefficient space and the polynomial jet space have different norms. Write \(E : V_d \to \mathscr{P}_d\) for exposure in the fixed radial jet frame, where \(\mathscr{P}_d\) is the space of polynomials of weighted grade at most \(d\). The weights of the finitely many jet variables are positive integers. Fix \(c_d \geqslant 1\) such that
\(\displaystyle |v|_1 \leqslant c_d |Ev|_1 \qquad (v \in V_d) .\) (16.58)

Such a constant exists because \(E\) is injective between fixed finite-dimensional spaces. It does not depend on the lattice volume. A bound for \(|EAu - q|_1\) is therefore not yet a bound with the same constant for the corresponding frame coefficients.

Here is the precise near/wide reduction. Its hypotheses distinguish the regular-germ identity from the two analytic ray estimates that are needed to use that identity.

Lemma 16.20. (Near and wide Taylor readings) Fix \(D \geqslant d\). There are finitely many real jet points \(x_1, \ldots, x_J\) and \(a_d \geqslant 1\) such that \(|q|_1 \leqslant a_d \max_j |q (x_j) |\) for \(q \in \mathscr{P}_d\). Let \(L_u = P_u + Q_u\) be polynomials of degree at most \(D\) in local link coordinates, and let \(\mathscr{U}\) be the linear operation taking their ordinary radial reading and truncating to weighted grade \(d\). Suppose \(M_u, \beta, C_b, \tau, \eta \geqslant 0\) and, for each \(j\), a linear functional \(\mathscr{R}_j\) on those polynomials satisfy

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} |P_u |_1 & \leqslant 3 \beta M_u, & |Q_u |_1 & \leqslant \eta \beta M_u, \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.59)} \\ \mathscr{R}_j (P_u) & = (EAu) (x_j), & & \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.60)} \\ | \mathscr{R}_j (P_u) - (\mathscr{U} P_u) (x_j) | & \leqslant C_b \tau |P_u |_1, & | (\mathscr{U} Q_u) (x_j) | & \leqslant C_b |Q_u |_1 . \hspace{3cm} \text{(16.61)} \end{array}\)

Then

\(\displaystyle |EAu - \mathscr{U} L_u |_1 \leqslant 3 a_d C_b \beta M_u (\tau + \eta), \qquad |L_u |_1 \leqslant (3 + \eta) \beta M_u .\) (16.62)

The estimate is uniform in every parameter for which the displayed hypotheses hold with the same constants.

Proof. The real evaluations separate \(\mathscr{P}_d\): a polynomial vanishing on all real points is zero, as follows by successively treating it as a polynomial in each variable. In a finite-dimensional dual space a spanning set contains a finite basis. Choose evaluations forming such a basis, and let \(q_j\) be the corresponding interpolation polynomials. Then \(q = \sum_j q (x_j) q_j\), so one may take \(a_d = \max (1, \sum_j |q_j |_1)\). If there are no jet variables, evaluation of the constant polynomial suffices.

Both \(EAu\) and \(\mathscr{U} L_u\) belong to \(\mathscr{P}_d\). At \(x_j\), linearity and (16.60) give

\(\displaystyle (EAu - \mathscr{U} L_u) (x_j) = \mathscr{R}_j (P_u) - (\mathscr{U} P_u) (x_j) - (\mathscr{U} Q_u) (x_j) .\)

Its modulus is at most \(C_b \beta M_u (3 \tau + \eta) \leqslant 3 C_b \beta M_u (\tau + \eta)\). Apply the sampling inequality. The last estimate is the triangle inequality and (16.59).\(\Box\)

In the intended application \(P_u\) sums the short-support terms of the actual regular insertion, and \(Q_u\) sums the remaining terms. The functional \(\mathscr{R}_j\) is its degree-\(d\) reading along the threshold-dependent holonomy ray. Equation (16.60) uses the prescribed local-section jet and the vanishing regular remainder jet, with all short supports inside the nonwrapping patch. It is an exact identity about this insertion; finite-difference reconstruction of a closed cochain alone does not imply it. The ordinary radial reading \(\mathscr{U}\) uses the linear edge integrals of the radial connection. The first estimate in (16.61) compares the two ray readings; the wide part only needs the second, absolute bound. In particular, after this comparison the reader in Lemma 16.19 may have \(a_i = 0\). Its nonlinear correction need not be paid a second time.

Constructing the raw polynomial and its Gaussian reading

The terminal approximation and its Gaussian reading use two related polynomials. We distinguish them before giving the estimates. For a symmetric matrix \(K\) write \(\mathcal{W}_K = \exp (\Delta_K / 2)\), where \(\Delta_K = \sum_{a, b} K_{ab} \partial_a \partial_b\). This is a finite sum on every polynomial; neither positivity nor a probability measure is needed. Write \(W_u\) for the raw curvature polynomial and \(\mathcal{H}u =\mathcal{W}_K W_u\) for its Gaussian reading. The healthy field is evaluated in \(W_u\), not in \(\mathcal{H}u\).

Lemma 16.23. (Changing the diagonal covariance) Let \(W_i\) be polynomials on separate, labelled copies of finite coordinate spaces. Let \(C\) be any symmetric matrix on their direct sum and \(K = \text{diag} (K_i)\) a block diagonal symmetric matrix. For every mean \(\mu\),

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{W}_{C, \mu} \left( \prod_i W_i \right) =\mathcal{W}_{C - K, \mu} \left( \prod_i \mathcal{W}_{K_i} W_i \right) .\)

The identity holds simultaneously for every subset of the slots, with all matrices restricted from the same direct sum.

Proof. All constant-coefficient partial derivatives commute. On the space of polynomials of degree at most the total degree of the product they are nilpotent, so the finite binomial formula gives \(\exp (\Delta_{C - K} / 2) \exp (\Delta_K / 2) = \exp (\Delta_C / 2)\). Each block of \(\Delta_K\) differentiates only its own factor. Hence its exponential sends the product to \(\prod_i \mathcal{W}_{K_i} W_i\). Evaluate the resulting polynomial at \(\mu\). The same proof on any subset proves the last assertion, including the empty subset.\(\Box\)

For the atlas of Subsection 14.13, take \(K_i\) to be the original same-slot covariance \(\kappa_k\). The conditional covariance before this change is \(C_{ij} = \kappa_k (z_i + \cdot, z_j + \cdot) - B_{ij}\), with \(B =\mathcal{L} \kappa_{\ell} \mathcal{L}^{\textsf{T}}\). Then \(C - K\) is exactly (14.83): its diagonal is \(- B_{ii}\) and its off-diagonal is \(\kappa_k - B_{ij}\). Thus the Gaussian cumulant calculation uses \(\mathcal{H}u\) with that subtracted covariance, whereas its terminal physical approximation uses \(W_u\). Repeated physical coordinates cause no difficulty: keep a separate slot copy and use their full cross-covariance. This identity is an identity of polynomial transports, not an assertion that a cutoff nonlinear sector integral is already Gaussian.

The Taylor polynomial of the calibrated regular list.
Here are explicit sufficient data for this construction, retaining the actual regular list and its original supports. Lemma 16.24. (Taylor reader from a summable regular list) Under these data there are polynomials \(P_u, Q_u, L_u = P_u + Q_u\) of degree at most \(D\) in the patch link coordinates, such that

\(\displaystyle |P_u |_1 \leqslant 3 C_D (1 + n_b)^D M_u, \qquad |Q_u |_1 \leqslant C_D (1 + n_b)^D \eta M_u,\)

where \(n_b\) is the number of real link coordinates and \(C_D\) depends only on \(D\) and the colour norm. On a fixed ball \(\|x\| \leqslant \rho_G\) with no flags,

\(\displaystyle |F_u (U (x)) - L_u (x) | \leqslant 4 M_u \|x\|^{D + 1} .\) (16.64)

The map \(u \mapsto L_u\) is linear, real, unit preserving and invariant under simultaneous adjoint rotation of its link variables. Moreover, the near polynomial satisfies (16.60).

Proof. Choose \(\rho_G \leqslant 1 / 8\) small enough that \(\text{chord} (\exp X) \leqslant 2 \|X\|\) on the required colour ball. Then every link of \(U (x)\) has chord at most \(\theta / 4\) and every plaquette has chord at most \(\theta\); the field has no flags. For \(\|v\| \leqslant 1\), the curve \(r \mapsto f_a (U (rv))\) on \(0 \leqslant r \leqslant \rho_G\) has derivative of order \(j \leqslant D + 1\) equal to its repeated left derivative in the single direction \(\theta v\). Indeed on each link \(\exp (\theta (r + h) v_e) = \exp (\theta hv_e) \exp (\theta rv_e)\); induct on \(j\). Its derivative is bounded by \(e_a\). Taylor's integral remainder therefore bounds the error by \(e_a \|x\|^{D + 1} / (D + 1) ! \leqslant e_a \|x\|^{D + 1}\).

Let \(p_a\) be this degree-\(D\) Taylor polynomial. To bound its coefficients, polarize each homogeneous term. For a symmetric \(j\)-linear form \(B\), its values on coordinate vectors are bounded by \(j^j / j\)! times its diagonal supremum on the unit ball: apply

\(\displaystyle B (v_1, \ldots, v_j) = \frac{1}{2^j j!} \sum_{\varepsilon \in \{- 1, 1\}^j} \left( \prod_i \varepsilon_i \right) B \left( \sum_i \varepsilon_i v_i, \ldots, \sum_i \varepsilon_i v_i \right) .\)

This formula follows by expansion; every term except the one using each \(v_i\) once cancels. Coordinate vectors have norm one. Expand the Taylor polynomial using ordered coordinate tuples; there are \(n_b^j\) such tuples at order \(j\). Summing for \(0 \leqslant j \leqslant D\) gives \(|p_a |_1 \leqslant C_D (1 + n_b)^D e_a\). Consequently both series \(\sum c_a p_a\) converge absolutely in the finite-dimensional degree-\(D\) polynomial space. Define \(P_u, Q_u\) by these two sums. Summing the remainder bounds proves (16.64) and the coefficient estimates. This also justifies applying any fixed jet functional term by term.

A polynomial of degree at most \(D\) which is \(O (\|x\|^{D + 1})\) at zero is zero: restrict to every real line and compare coefficients in its scalar parameter. Thus the Taylor polynomial is unique. Apply uniqueness to the actual field's linearity, real structure, constant-gauge invariance and unit to obtain the corresponding properties of \(L_u\). No linear choice of the intermediate regular lists is required.

Finally restrict to a fixed threshold holonomy ray through zero. Its link logarithm vanishes at zero. Composing the remainder with this smooth curve gives \(O (|r|^{D + 1})\), which has zero derivatives through order \(d \leqslant D\). Each near term's degree-\(d\) reading is therefore the reading of its Taylor polynomial. The nonwrapping local section has its prescribed reading and each regular remainder has zero reading, by Subsection 7.9. Absolute coefficient summability permits the fixed finite jet functional to pass through the sum. Their weighted sum is \(EAu\), proving (16.60).\(\Box\)

The remaining ray estimate also follows at the level of these actual polynomials. For each of the finitely many fixed radial tests let \(Z_{\theta} (r)\) be its link logarithm divided by \(\theta\), and let \(Z_0 (r)\) be its linearized link integral. The logarithmic endpoint map constructed in Subsection 7.9 is analytic, vanishes at zero and has that link integral as its derivative. Its terms of degree at least two have, after division by \(\theta\), at least one remaining factor of \(\theta\). Through any fixed \(r\)-derivative order the connection is a fixed polynomial in the link location. Thus, on links in the radius-\(b\) patch, the jets of \(Z_{\theta}, Z_0\) through order \(d\) are at most \(C (1 + b)^v\), and those of their difference are at most \(C (1 + b)^v \theta\). Subtract products by replacing one factor at a time. Every difference contains one jet of \(Z_{\theta} - Z_0\), hence one factor \(\theta\); all other factors have the preceding polynomial bound. 16.9.1A finite polynomial with coupled cutoff and limiting labels

Consider a finite sum of words

\(\displaystyle Z^{N, p} (u) = \sum_{\alpha = 1}^L c_{\alpha} \prod_{j = 1}^{n_{\alpha}} f_{\alpha j}^{N, p} (x_{\alpha j} + u), \qquad Z = \sum_{\alpha = 1}^L c_{\alpha} M (w_{\alpha}) .\)

Each word uses sites on the depth-\(k\) grid that are generic: in every coordinate its distinct slots have distinct coordinates. Different words in the sum may share sites. Empty words are allowed and represent the constant \(1\) at both cutoff and limit. The coefficients \(c_{\alpha}\) are fixed before the cutoff limits. Each cutoff label converges in the selected order to the label of the corresponding slot of \(w_{\alpha}\). We call this coupled finite list a polynomial packet. Suppose its cutoff polynomial at \(u = 0\) is chamber-interior and has root bound at most \(g + \varepsilon\) at depth \(b\), eventually for every \(\varepsilon > 0\).

For \(q \in \mathbb{N}\) and integers \(t \geqslant 1\) place \(2 q\) translates of this whole polynomial at physical displacements

\(\displaystyle u_j (t) = 2 ta_b j (1, 1, 1, 1), \qquad 0 \leqslant j < 2 q.\)

These are depth-\(k\) grid displacements because \(b \leqslant k\) and the scales are aligned. All coarse cell addresses are even in every coordinate. Consequently copying from the home cell to them is ordinary translation, without a reflection or conjugation of the copied polynomial. At even \(j\) use \(\overline{Z^{N, p}}\), and at odd \(j\) use \(Z^{N, p}\). Conjugation does not change a root bound: it conjugates the full copied product and its expectation, preserving its absolute value.

For each fixed \(t, q\), sufficiently large periods make the \(2 q\) cell addresses distinct. Indeed \(2 t (2 q)\) not exceeding the coarse period suffices; reducing modulo that period is then injective on the chosen addresses. The planted inequality on the same Wilson law gives

\(\displaystyle \left| \mathbb{E}_{N, p} \prod_{j = 0}^{2 q - 1} \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \overline{Z^{N, p} (u_j (t))}, & j \text{even},\\ Z^{N, p} (u_j (t)), & j \text{odd} \end{array} \right. \right| \leqslant (g + \varepsilon)^{2 q} .\) (16.75)

The assertion is eventual in period and then in the mesh tail at that period. It makes no claim at periods too small to hold the distinct copies. For \(q = 0\) both sides equal \(1\).

Expand the product into its finitely many choices of a monomial for each of the \(2 q\) packets. Within each chosen monomial the slots are generic. Between slots in different packets, equality of any one coordinate is an affine equation in \(t\) with nonzero slope, since their packet indices differ. It excludes at most one value of \(t\). There are finitely many slot pairs, coordinates and monomial choices. Thus all sufficiently large \(t\) make every expanded configuration generic. For each such fixed \(t\), take the fine-mesh and period limits in their prescribed order. The same limiting labels are used in every term. Ordinary conjugation of a cutoff insertion agrees with conjugation of its label once its fixed reference depth is admitted. Convergence of these conjugated labels follows from continuity of conjugation. The point-limit theorem therefore applies term by term. At finite cutoff the integrands are continuous on the compact configuration space, so their finite sums are integrable and expansion under the integral is legitimate. This proves a limiting finite sum \(P_q (t)\) with

\(\displaystyle |P_q (t) | \leqslant (g + \varepsilon)^{2 q}\) (16.76)

for all sufficiently large \(t\).

Now let \(t \to \infty\). The time-coordinate separation between packets \(j < h\) is \(2 ta_b (h - j) \to \infty\). The separated-cluster theorem permits the simultaneous spatial translations used here. Apply it to each expanded monomial, retaining the order of its packets. By (16.72), the even packets converge to adjoints of their compressed whole-word operators. Summing the finitely many terms gives

\(\displaystyle P_q (t) \longrightarrow \langle \Omega_0, (Z^{\ast} Z)^q \Omega_0 \rangle .\)

This is simply expansion of the ordered product \(Z^{\ast} Z \cdots Z^{\ast} Z\); commutativity is not needed for this identification. Take real parts in (16.76) and then let \(\varepsilon \downarrow 0\). We have proved

\(\displaystyle \langle \Omega_0, (Z^{\ast} Z)^q \Omega_0 \rangle \leqslant g^{2 q} \qquad (q = 0, 1, 2, \ldots) .\) (16.77)

The left side is real and nonnegative. The order of limits was: fix \(q, \varepsilon\); fix sufficiently large \(t\); take the cutoff limits; take \(t \to \infty\); finally take \(\varepsilon \downarrow 0\). No uniform-in-\(q\) cutoff threshold or interchange of those limits has been used.

16.10.2A common tiling for the finite column sum

We explain why (16.78) can be summed with cutoff-dependent subpower coefficients. The bounds at opened pairs do not have to hold at one common finest cell for all requests at once. To combine different column errors, transfer each such centered estimate to every required larger cell. Fix a cell depth \(m\) and a positive integer margin denominator \(J\). Choose a finer centered depth \(k \geqslant m\) such that \(3 Ja_k \leqslant a_m\), and at which the centered estimate holds. If its lattice span is \(h = a_k / a_N\), then a depth-\(m\) cell has span \(H = a_m / a_N\) and \(3 h \leqslant H / J\). The scales are aligned, so \(h\) divides \(H\).

Its copied orientations are coordinate reflections, with the prescribed complex conjugation. Translation invariance and reflection covariance identify each of their centered root tests with the test at the origin. Applying the planted inequality to the entire copy orbit and taking its root bounds the larger-cell root by the same number. A bound on just the unreplicated insertion would not suffice for this step.

For an arbitrary point in the larger cell with margin \(3 h\), bracket each coordinate by consecutive fine-grid centers. The reflection argument in Subsection 4.1 gives the integer maximum principle \(r_j^2 \leqslant r_{j - 1} r_{j + 1}\) for the nonnegative root tests along one segment. Its proof includes zero root tests. Applying it successively in four coordinates bounds every intermediate root by the largest corner root. Now let \(Q_{\alpha}\) be the finitely many oriented column differences in (16.79), and let \(r_{\alpha} (N, p)\) be subpower scalar coefficients, constant in the spatial translation variable. On any one of these larger tilings the root triangle inequality gives

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R} \left( \sum_{\alpha} r_{\alpha} Q_{\alpha} \right) \leqslant \sum_{\alpha} |r_{\alpha} | \mathcal{R} (Q_{\alpha}) \leqslant \left( \sum_{\alpha} |r_{\alpha} | \right) e^{- s_N / 16} .\)

The comparisons have first been transferred to the same tiling; intersecting unrelated sets of admissible cell depths would not justify this addition.
