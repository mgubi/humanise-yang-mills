Four-dimensional Yang–Mills theory
A constructive approach

Working exposition from the supplied manuscripts

Draft of 22 November 2026

Main theorem

The construction starts from Wilson lattice gauge theory on periodic four-dimensional lattices. Its continuum observables are local gauge-invariant fields. Their Euclidean correlations determine the relativistic theory described below.

Main theorem (continuum Yang–Mills fields). Let \(G\) be a compact matrix group with simple nonabelian real Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{g}\). Fix a faithful unitary representation and its normalized trace inner product, a positive penalty parameter for the block transformation, and a sufficiently large inverse coupling at the coarsest scale. There is a choice of lattice spacings tending to zero, admissible physical periods tending to infinity, bare Wilson couplings, and normalizations of local observables with the following properties.

    (i)

    Continuum limit. At each fixed admissible period the correlations converge along the full sequence of lattice spacings. From any increasing sequence of these periods one can extract a single subsequence along which the correlations of all orders converge to smooth functions at pairwise distinct points in \(\mathbb{R}^4\). The same subsequence works for all derivatives. The resulting local fields form nested finite-dimensional spaces, obtained by imposing successive bounds on the scaling dimension of the polynomial labels (curvature counts as two, each derivative as one, and dimensions add in products). They contain the identity and admit commuting spacetime derivatives. At each bound the field space and its gauge-invariant free Maxwell counterpart have the same dimension and carry isomorphic representations of \(O (4)\).

    (ii)

    Relativistic theory. The limiting correlations are averages, with respect to a probability measure of full support on a compact metrizable space of phases, of Euclidean covariant, reflection-positive correlations. Every phase has the same field spaces and reconstructs a local, Poincaré-covariant theory on four-dimensional Minkowski space. Its Wightman distributions are tempered, its energy–momentum spectrum lies in the closed forward light cone, and its invariant vacuum is unique up to scalar multiples. The weak commutant of the fields consists of scalars.

    (iii)

    Stress tensor and local products. The phases share a real, symmetric, conserved stress tensor whose local charges generate translations and rotations through the Ward identities. Products of nearby fields admit an operator product expansion to each fixed finite order, with coefficient and remainder bounds uniform in the phase. A fixed Taylor subtraction also extends the separated Euclidean correlations across coincident points as distributions of locally finite order.

Vacuum uniqueness here is a statement within each phase. Uniqueness of the infinite-volume limit and a positive mass gap remain open in this construction.

Where the theorem is proved

Theorem 19.1 (p. 326) assembles the conclusions for one and the same family of Wilson measures. The main steps of its proof can be read separately as follows.

The two limits in (i).
Theorems 10.1 and 10.3 tune the bare coupling and prove convergence of the renormalization group trajectory at each fixed scale. With the bounds on observable insertions from Chapter 11, Theorem 12.5 proves the full cutoff limit at fixed volume. Section 13.1 then extracts one subsequence of volumes for all separated correlations and their derivatives. Proposition 13.1 identifies the dimensions of the field spaces. Section 13.4 constructs their inclusions and derivatives, and Theorem 14.5 identifies their \(O (4)\) representations.
Phases and relativistic fields in (ii).
Section 15.3 gives the integral decomposition into phases. Sections 15.4 and 15.5 establish symmetry, reflection positivity and equality of the field spaces in every phase. Section 16.1 constructs the tempered boundary values. Theorem 16.3 reconstructs the local fields and proves the spectrum condition. Theorem 16.4 proves vacuum uniqueness in every phase, and Proposition 16.5 determines the weak commutant.
The stress tensor and local products in (iii).
Proposition 14.6 fixes the normalization of the conserved tensor by its Euclidean Ward identities. Section 16.6 continues them to the relativistic charge law (16.13). Theorem 18.2 gives the finite-order expansion and its remainder estimate. The distinct assertion about extension across the collision diagonals is Theorem 18.3.
A further conclusion in one selected phase.
Let \(\Theta\) be the trace of the Euclidean stress tensor minus its expectation in the phase selected in Section 15.6, and let \(G_{\Theta} (r)\) be its two-point function at separation \(r > 0\) in that phase. The additional estimates of Chapter 17 give
\(\displaystyle \lim_{r \downarrow 0} r^8 \log^2 (1 / r) \hspace{0.17em} G_{\Theta} (r) = \frac{3 \dim_{\mathbb{R}} \mathfrak{g}}{\pi^4} .\)

Theorem 17.5 proves the limit for arbitrary radii tending to zero. Section 17.9 specifies the finite class of mass-shell correlation functions excluded by this asymptotic.

About this draft

This book is being written from three supplied accounts of a proposed construction of four-dimensional Yang–Mills theory, together with the detailed outline accompanying Scott's account. The aim is a readable mathematical exposition with definitions at their first use, consistent notation, and precise separation of standard arguments from estimates specific to the construction.

The present draft contains the nineteen planned chapters, including the construction, reconstruction, quantitative trace law and finite-order operator products. The accompanying blueprint gives bounded source packets and a common notation registry; the audits record the detailed hypothesis matching and corrections made while writing. This is a working exposition, not an independent certification of the supplied manuscripts or a substitute for further mathematical review.

The intended construction first removes the lattice spacing at each fixed physical volume and then extracts an infinite-volume subsequence. Its limiting correlations describe gauge-invariant local observables. Reconstruction, the stress tensor and operator products are developed after the continuum field spaces. No positive mass gap is asserted. Nor does the plan assert uniqueness of the infinite-volume limit or independence of all choices in the blocking procedure.

The reader is assumed to know basic probability, analysis, compact Lie groups and the general aims of constructive quantum field theory. Standard results are cited where used; their application is explained without reproducing textbook proofs. Source-specific claims whose hypotheses have not yet been matched are not treated as proved merely because the source labels them theorems.

Reading guide

The construction has two limiting operations. First the lattice spacing is removed while the physical volume is held fixed. Then the volume increases along a subsequence. The first operation requires a stability argument, not just compactness. The second uses compactness only after bounds uniform in volume have been established. Keeping these operations separate also fixes the meaning of the local observables throughout the book.

A lattice expression with the correct classical limit is not yet a normalized quantum field. Integration can mix it with other expressions of the same or lower degree, including the constant. We therefore begin with finite-dimensional spaces of polynomial labels, construct their local lattice representatives, and later determine their normalization by transporting all coefficients together. Fields are finally defined by equality of all separated correlation responses. This prevents a formal equation of motion from being identified prematurely with a vanishing interacting field.

The main analytic work is the block step. Its quadratic part determines a Gaussian fluctuation field and a stationary background. The nonlinear integral is separated into regions of small curvature, where Taylor and Gaussian estimates apply, and complementary regions, where a positive action cost is retained. Local terms are reorganized as polymer activities: functions associated with connected supports, controlled by weighted norms. The support weights must accommodate the full dependence of an activity, including the cutoffs and local gauge choices used to define it. This distinction matters when several insertions interact.

Once the block estimates are available, one scale schedule must satisfy all fixed finite requirements. It is chosen slowly enough that each stage's constants can be controlled before the next set of requirements is introduced. A common trajectory then determines the couplings, activities and observable normalizations for the entire Wilson family. Observable insertions are tracked by commuting auxiliary variables whose squares are zero. Coefficients involving several variables retain the terms generated when insertions merge under blocking.

The continuum part uses this information in a different order from a formal perturbative calculation. Finite families of free-field correlation functionals separate the polynomial labels and control the dimension of the interacting field spaces. Coordinate reflection positivity constructs Hilbert spaces and bounded slab operators before continuous rotations are known. A conserved tensor, local flux estimates and positivity then establish rotational Ward identities and fix the stress normalization. Only then are phases separated and relativistic fields reconstructed.

Chapters Role in the argument
1–3 Lattice conventions, local labels and exact microscopic equations; free Maxwell correlations that separate polynomial labels.
4–9 Exact blocking, Gaussian kernels, large-field estimates, local matching, polymer expansions and the nonlinear step.
10–12 One common trajectory, observable insertions and the full ultraviolet limit at fixed physical volume.
13–16 Infinite-volume correlations, field quotients, Ward identities, phases and relativistic reconstruction.
17 The quantitative trace coefficient and its all-radius consequence.
18–19 Finite operator products, specified contact extensions, and assembly with the exact scope of the conclusions.

The final two analytic refinements have different purposes. The trace law needs a rate beyond convergence to the traceless free tensor; an error that tends to zero may still be too large after multiplication by the running coupling. An operator product expansion needs a signed replacement estimate for a whole cluster, uniform under repeated copies. Neither follows from the existence of separated correlations alone. A conservative finite-order extension across collision diagonals, by contrast, follows from polynomial separated bounds and a specified Taylor subtraction.

The final assembly chapter collects the proved dependencies and distinguishes the conclusions from further questions not addressed here. The accompanying chapter audits provide source locations and review records; they are working documents rather than prerequisites for reading the mathematical exposition.
