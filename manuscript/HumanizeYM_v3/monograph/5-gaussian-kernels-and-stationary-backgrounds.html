Chapter 5
Gaussian kernels and stationary backgrounds

The quadratic approximation serves two purposes. It identifies the covariance of the fluctuations eliminated by a block transformation, and it supplies the inverse needed to construct a stationary background for the nonlinear action. These uses require different estimates. Exact matrix identities hold on each finite lattice; uniform locality requires bounds which survive changes of the lattice size and of the blocking history.

We first fix the normalization of the exact identities. We then isolate the additional hypotheses for uniform analytic continuation and for a stationary background. The general block transformation algebra is standard; see Bałaban–Feldman–Knörrer–Trubowitz [BFKT16b]. The construction of local propagators and backgrounds for lattice gauge theory originates in Bałaban's work [Bał84, Bał85]. Those references explain the method. Their results cannot be applied to a different averaging map without checking its hypotheses.

5.1Lattice differential forms and zero modes

In this chapter distances and Fourier variables are in lattice units. Let \(\Lambda_s = (\mathbb{Z}/ s\mathbb{Z})^4\). A lattice \(r\)-form assigns an element of \(\mathfrak{g}\) to each site and each increasing \(r\)-tuple of coordinate directions. The scalar product is the sum over sites and increasing tuples, using the Lie algebra metric of (1.1). In particular there is no volume normalization in this scalar product. Write \(\Omega^r (\Lambda_s ; \mathfrak{g})\) for this finite-dimensional space. The forward difference is \(\nabla_{\mu} u (x) = u (x + e_{\mu}) - u (x)\), and the exterior derivative is

\(\displaystyle (d_r u)_{\mu_0 \cdots \mu_r} = \sum_{i = 0}^r (- 1)^i \nabla_{\mu_i} u_{\mu_0 \cdots \widehat{\mu_i} \cdots \mu_r} .\)

Commutation of the differences gives \(d_{r + 1} d_r = 0\). An adjoint is always taken for the stated counting scalar products.

A linearized gauge transformation of a one-form \(a\) is \(a \mapsto a + d_0 \phi\). If \(H\) is a positive self-adjoint operator on two-forms, the quadratic action and its kinetic operator are

\(\displaystyle S_0 (a) = \tfrac{1}{2} \langle d_1 a, Hd_1 a \rangle, \qquad K = d_1^{\ast} Hd_1 . \) (5.1)

Thus \(K\) vanishes on gradients. It also vanishes on constant one-forms. On a periodic lattice the latter are harmonic modes and are not gradients of periodic scalar functions. Removing gauge directions alone therefore does not make \(K\) invertible.

For \(p \in (2 \pi / s) \mathbb{Z}^4 / (2 \pi \mathbb{Z})^4\) our Fourier convention is

\(\displaystyle \hat{u} (p) = \sum_{x \in \Lambda_s} e^{- ip \cdot x} u (x), \quad u (x) = s^{- 4} \sum_p e^{ip \cdot x} \hat{u} (p), \quad \|u\|_2^2 = s^{- 4} \sum_p | \hat{u} (p) |^2 . \) (5.2)

Set \(q_{\mu} (p) = e^{ip_{\mu}} - 1\) and \(\lambda (p) = \sum_{\mu} q_{\mu} (- p) q_{\mu} (p)\). On real momenta this is \(\sum_{\mu} |q_{\mu} (p) |^2\). The symbol of \(d_r\) is exterior multiplication by \(q\). In particular, on one-forms,

\(\displaystyle |q \wedge a|^2 + | \bar{q} \lrcorner a|^2 = \lambda (p) |a|^2 .\)

Here contraction denotes the adjoint of exterior multiplication. For \(p \ne 0\) this gives the orthogonal decomposition into a gradient and its orthogonal complement. At \(p = 0\) the whole one-form space is harmonic.

Whenever a Gaussian probability measure is used below, its precision is strictly positive on the specified coordinate space. One may work on a fixed slice with the harmonic modes removed, or add a positive regulator. A massless covariance at zero momentum is not defined by writing \(1 / \lambda (0)\). Conversely, an identity between quadratic forms can remain valid on a space where no normalized massless Gaussian exists.

5.2The exact Gaussian block transformation

Let \(E_f, E_c\) be real finite-dimensional inner-product spaces, \(Q : E_f \to E_c\) a linear block map, and \(b > 0\) the block penalty. Suppose initially that \(K_f > 0\) on \(E_f\). Define

\(\displaystyle A = K_f + bQ^{\ast} Q, \qquad W = A^{- 1} bQ^{\ast}, \qquad K_c = bI - b^2 QA^{- 1} Q^{\ast} . \) (5.3)

These formulas describe the normalized Gaussian problem. When the physical quadratic exponent is \(\beta (\langle A_f, K_f A_f \rangle + b\|QA_f - V\|^2) / 2\), the variables here are \(a = \sqrt{\beta} A_f\) and \(v = \sqrt{\beta} V\). In unscaled link logarithms the fluctuation covariance is \(\beta^{- 1} (K_f + bQ^{\ast} Q)^{- 1}\). Thus the block parameter remains \(b\) after normalization, although the original block kernel has penalty \(b \beta\).

The map \(W\) sends a coarse field to the stationary fine field. The fluctuation covariance is \(A^{- 1}\). The Schur-complement formulas give

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} K_c & = (b^{- 1} I + QK_f^{- 1} Q^{\ast})^{- 1}, \hspace{3cm} \text{(5.4)} \\ \langle a, K_f a \rangle + b \|Qa - v\|^2 & = \langle v, K_c v \rangle + \langle a - Wv, A (a - Wv) \rangle . \hspace{3cm} \text{(5.5)} \end{array}\)

These are the usual finite-dimensional Gaussian identities, or the real quadratic specialization of the block transformation identities in [BFKT16b]; no separate proof is needed. The accompanying normalization is

\(\displaystyle \int_{E_f} e^{- \langle a, K_f a \rangle / 2 - b \|Qa - v\|^2 / 2} \hspace{0.17em} da = (2 \pi)^{\dim E_f / 2} (\det A)^{- 1 / 2} e^{- \langle v, K_c v \rangle / 2} .\)

The Lebesgue measure here comes from the specified inner product. In a normalized block kernel there is also the factor \((b / 2 \pi)^{\dim E_c / 2}\).

There is an equivalent probabilistic statement. If \(a\) and \(\xi\) are independent centered Gaussians with covariances \(K_f^{- 1}\) and \(I\), then \(v = Qa + b^{- 1 / 2} \xi\) has covariance \(b^{- 1} I + QK_f^{- 1} Q^{\ast}\). Conditional on \(v\), the fine field has mean \(Wv\) and covariance \(A^{- 1}\).

The identities (5.3) and (5.5) also hold for \(K_f \geqslant 0\), provided \(A\) is invertible. Their derivation uses no inverse of \(K_f\). In this case (5.4) and the joint probability interpretation are unavailable unless the remaining null directions have been treated. In the gauge problem, every use of this extension must specify the slice or quotient on which \(A\) is invertible.

The useful consequences, with the same domains and adjoints, are

\(\displaystyle K_f W = Q^{\ast} K_c, \qquad QW = I - b^{- 1} K_c, \qquad W^{\ast} K_f W + b (QW - I)^{\ast} (QW - I) = K_c .\)

The last identity says that the stationary fine action together with its block penalty is exactly the coarse quadratic action.

5.3Averaging and the Fourier alias formula

To check the scaling factor in the covariance recursion, take \(\Lambda_{Ls}\) as fine lattice and \(\Lambda_s\) as coarse lattice, with odd \(L \geqslant 3\). Let \(w (z)\) be a finitely supported real averaging kernel of total mass one, periodized on the fine lattice, and use

\(\displaystyle (Ma)_{\mu} (y) = \sum_z w (z) \sum_{r = 0}^{L - 1} a_{\mu} (Ly + z + re_{\mu}) .\)

The inner sum is a sum along a link of length \(L\), not its average. Define the symbols

\(\displaystyle \hat{w} (\theta) = \sum_z w (z) e^{i \theta \cdot z}, \qquad r_L (t) = L^{- 1} \sum_{r = 0}^{L - 1} e^{irt} .\)

The sign in \(\hat{w}\) is chosen for this formula for \(M\). The aliases of a coarse momentum \(p\) are \(\theta_z = (p + 2 \pi z) / L\), with one representative for each \(z \in (\mathbb{Z}/ L\mathbb{Z})^4\).

Suppose an auxiliary, translation-invariant Gaussian one-form has diagonal covariance symbol \(\text{diag} (c_{\mu} (\theta))\) in the direction index. Color indices are suppressed and are unchanged by this calculation. The symbol of the covariance of \(Ma + b^{- 1 / 2} \xi\) is

\(\displaystyle c'_{\mu} (p) = b^{- 1} + L^{- 2} \sum_{z \text{mod} \hspace{0.17em} L} \hat{w} (\theta_z) \hat{w} (- \theta_z) r_L (\theta_{z, \mu}) r_L (- \theta_{z, \mu}) c_{\mu} (\theta_z) . \) (5.6)

This formula is exact for a positive regulated covariance. It can also be used for nonzero modes of a massless symbol with the zero mode handled separately.

For clarity, the normalization check is short. Substitution of (5.2) into \(Ma\) gives \(\widehat{Ma}_{\mu} (p) = L^{- 4} \sum_z L \hat{w} (\theta_z) r_L (\theta_{z, \mu}) \hat{a}_{\mu} (\theta_z)\). Fine Fourier covariances carry the volume \((Ls)^4\), whereas coarse ones carry \(s^4\). Consequently the covariance factor is \(L^{- 8} L^2 L^4 = L^{- 2}\). This is the factor used in (5.6).

The particular smoothing in the supplied construction uses

\(\displaystyle \hat{w}_L (\theta) = \prod_{\nu = 1}^4 \left( L^{- 1} \sum_{|r| \leqslant (L - 1) / 2} e^{ir \theta_{\nu}} \right), \qquad \hat{w} (\theta) = \hat{w}_L (\theta)^j,\)

where \(j\) is the integer smoothing order. Centering makes \(\hat{w}_L\) even on real and complex momenta. The recurrence then contains \(\hat{w}_L (\theta_z)^{2 j}\). This choice fixes an auxiliary covariance. Identification with the quadratic form of a particular gauge-equivariant block map additionally requires checking that its linearization differs from \(M\) only by an appropriate gradient, and that the chosen treatment of zero modes agrees.

5.4Gauge reduction and a regular curvature symbol

At a real nonzero momentum suppose \(c_{\mu} > 0\) and put \(w_{\mu} = c_{\mu}^{- 1}\). Minimizing over a scalar gauge parameter gives

\(\displaystyle \min_{\phi} \sum_{\mu} w_{\mu} |a_{\mu} + q_{\mu} \phi |^2 = \frac{\sum_{\mu < \nu} w_{\mu} w_{\nu} |q_{\mu} a_{\nu} - q_{\nu} a_{\mu} |^2}{\sum_{\mu} w_{\mu} |q_{\mu} |^2} . \) (5.7)

It is the weighted orthogonal projection formula. Thus a diagonal two-form metric representing the quotient form is

\(\displaystyle (H_0)_{\mu \nu} (p) = \left[ c_{\mu} (p) c_{\nu} (p) \sum_{\alpha} \frac{q_{\alpha} (- p) q_{\alpha} (p)}{c_{\alpha} (p)} \right]^{- 1} . \) (5.8)

Formula (5.8) is initially an identity away from zero. Its regular extension at zero is a separate issue.

Here is the cancellation used for that extension. Let \(M\) now denote the total dilation over several steps; it is a number, distinct from the averaging operator of the previous section. Put \(\lambda_M (p) = M^2 \lambda (p / M)\). Suppose the accumulated covariance has the representation

\(\displaystyle c_{\mu} (p) = \frac{A_{\mu} (p)}{\lambda_M (p)} + G_{\mu} (p), \quad A_{\mu} (p) = F (p) r_M (p_{\mu} / M) r_M (- p_{\mu} / M), \quad B_{\mu} = A_{\mu} + \lambda_M G_{\mu} .\)

The remainder \(G_{\mu}\) collects the nonprincipal aliases and the accumulated block noise. Assume that \(F, G_{\mu}, A_{\mu}, B_{\mu}\) are holomorphic on a common neighborhood and that \(F, A_{\mu}, B_{\mu}\) do not vanish there. The elementary identity \(q_{\mu} (p) r_M (p_{\mu} / M)^{- 1} = Mq_{\mu} (p / M)\), interpreted after multiplication to remove possible zeros, gives

\(\displaystyle q_{\mu} (p) q_{\mu} (- p) F = M^2 q_{\mu} (p / M) q_{\mu} (- p / M) A_{\mu} .\)

Consequently, if

\(\displaystyle h = F^{- 1} - \sum_{\mu} \frac{q_{\mu} (p) q_{\mu} (- p) G_{\mu}}{A_{\mu} B_{\mu}}, \qquad \lambda_M h = \sum_{\mu} \frac{q_{\mu} (p) q_{\mu} (- p)}{B_{\mu}}, \) (5.9)

then (5.8) becomes

\(\displaystyle (H_0)_{\mu \nu} = (hB_{\mu} B_{\nu})^{- 1} .\)

The second equality in (5.9) follows by subtracting \(1 / B_{\mu}\) from \(1 / A_{\mu}\); it is not an additional assumption. If \(h\) is also nonvanishing, the last formula proves holomorphic extension through the apparent massless pole. When \(F (0) = A_{\mu} (0) = 1\), it gives \(H_0 (0) = I\).

This calculation proves pole cancellation, not a uniform zero-free strip. For that stronger conclusion the nonprincipal aliases, accumulated noise, and reciprocal factors must be bounded uniformly over every admitted blocking history. These are precisely the estimates which cannot be replaced by finite-dimensional Gaussian algebra.

One can modify the metric away from exact two-forms without changing the action. For \(\zeta \geqslant 0\) define, at real momentum,

\(\displaystyle H^{- 1} = H_0^{- 1} + \zeta (d_2 H_0^{- 1})^{\ast} (d_2 H_0^{- 1}) . \) (5.10)

If \(H_0 > 0\), this defines a positive \(H\), and \(H^{- 1} H_0 d_1 = d_1\), because \(d_2 d_1 = 0\). Therefore \(Hd_1 = H_0 d_1\). Positivity on all two-forms and agreement on exact two-forms are different assertions; this modification is useful because later estimates need both.

5.5Uniform estimates over finite blocking histories

We now verify the uniform reciprocal bounds required in (5.9) for the actual averaging recurrence. A finite history consists of odd ratios \(L_1, \ldots, L_n \geqslant 3\) and smoothing orders \(j_0, \ldots, j_n \geqslant 4\), with \(j_{h - 1} - j_h \in \{0, 1\}\). Set \(M_h = L_1 \cdots L_h\), \(M_0 = 1\). The order used by the \(h\)th block map is its incoming order \(j_{h - 1}\). Denote the centered box symbol \(\hat{w}_L\) of Section 5.3 by \(w_L\) in the following formulas.

Proposition 5.1. (Uniform curvature metric) Fix the final smoothing order \(j = j_n \geqslant 4\) and the penalty \(b > 0\). Start with the nonzero-mode covariance \(c_{\mu} (p) = 1 / \lambda (p)\) and iterate (5.6) along any such finite history. The resulting curvature metric \(H_0\) has a holomorphic, periodic extension to a common strip about real momenta, with uniformly bounded entries and inverse entries. On real momenta \(H_0\) has uniform positive upper and lower bounds and \(H_0 (0) = I\). The constants depend on \(j, b\), but not on the history length, ratios or torus period.

Proof. The box composition identity \(w_A (B \theta) w_B (\theta) = w_{AB} (\theta)\) holds for odd \(A, B\), by decomposing a centered interval of length \(AB\) into centered intervals of length \(B\). Thus the accumulated filter is

\(\displaystyle f (\theta) = w_{M_n} (\theta)^j \prod_{\{h : j_{h - 1} = j_h + 1\}} w_{M_h} (\theta) . \) (5.11)

The directional filter is \(f (\theta) r_{M_n} (\theta_{\mu})\): the link factors compose by \(r_A (Bt) r_B (t) = r_{AB} (t)\).

Here and below momentum estimates are on an enlarged cube \(| \text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} p_{\mu} | < \pi + \eta\), \(| \text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} p_{\mu} | < \eta\), for a sufficiently small fixed \(\eta > 0\). Write \(M = M_n\) and choose centered alias representatives \(z_{\mu} \in [- (M - 1) / 2, (M - 1) / 2]\). The finite geometric sum gives

\(\displaystyle |r_M ((p_{\mu} + 2 \pi z_{\mu}) / M) |, \quad \left| M^{- 1} \sum_{|r| \leqslant (M - 1) / 2} e^{ir (p_{\mu} + 2 \pi z_{\mu}) / M} \right| \leqslant \frac{C}{1 + |z_{\mu} |} .\)

Indeed the numerator of the geometric quotient is bounded, and \(M |e^{i (p_{\mu} + 2 \pi z_{\mu}) / M} - 1|\) is bounded below by \(c (1 + |z_{\mu} |)\) for \(z_{\mu} \ne 0\); the principal case is bounded directly by the finite sum. At the principal real alias the centered factor has a positive lower bound.

The additional factors in (5.11) have a uniform product bound. A factor of side \(A \leqslant M\) has modulus at most \(\exp (C \eta A / M)\) on the cube evaluated at \(p / M\) or any other alias. The sides occurring in the product increase at least geometrically, so their sum divided by \(M\) is bounded. On the real principal alias the same factors have product bounded away from zero. To see this without multiplying a fixed lower bound infinitely often, use \(| \log w_A (p / M) | \leqslant CA / M\) on that real cube, with the value at a removable zero interpreted continuously. The bound follows from the geometric quotient near zero and compactness for \(A / M\) bounded below. Again \(\sum A / M\) is bounded. Consequently, with \(\Gamma (z) = \prod_{\mu} (1 + |z_{\mu} |)\),

\(\displaystyle |f ((p + 2 \pi z) / M) r_M ((p_{\mu} + 2 \pi z_{\mu}) / M) | \leqslant C_j \Gamma (z)^{- j}, \qquad |f (p / M) | \geqslant c_j > 0\)

in the first estimate on a complex cube and in the second on its real slice.

There is also an exact description of the accumulated noise. The independent noise introduced at step \(h\) is propagated only through the suffix \(h + 1, \ldots, n\). If that suffix has total ratio \(D = M_n / M_h\), its contribution, before multiplication by \(b^{- 1}\), is

\(\displaystyle D^{- 2} \sum_{z \text{mod} \hspace{0.17em} D} f_{\text{suffix}} (\theta_z) f_{\text{suffix}} (- \theta_z) r_D (\theta_{z, \mu}) r_D (- \theta_{z, \mu}) .\)

This includes \(1\) when \(h = n\) and \(D = 1\). To verify the formula, apply (5.6) to one constant covariance and split each residue modulo a product of ratios into its successive residues. The filter composition identities above identify the summands and the factors multiply to \(D^{- 2}\). Summing over the noises gives precisely the noise part of the iterated recurrence. Each suffix contribution is bounded by \(C_j D^{- 2}\) because

\(\displaystyle \sum_z \Gamma (z)^{- 2 j} \leqslant \left( \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} (1 + |k|)^{- 2} \right)^4 < 81.\)

As \(D \geqslant 3^{n - h}\), the total noise is at most \(C_j \sum_{r \geqslant 0} 3^{- 2 r}\), uniformly in \(n\).

Separate the principal alias of the propagated initial covariance from all other aliases. The former gives \(A_{\mu} / \lambda_M\) with the notation of (5.9); the latter are

\(\displaystyle \sum_{z \ne 0} \frac{f (\theta_z) f (- \theta_z) r_M (\theta_{z, \mu}) r_M (- \theta_{z, \mu})}{M^2 \lambda (\theta_z)} .\)

For a nonprincipal real alias the denominator is bounded below by \(c (1 + \max_{\mu} |z_{\mu} |)^2\). Its change on a complex strip of width \(\eta\) is at most \(C \eta (1 + \max_{\mu} |z_{\mu} |) + C \eta^2\). Decreasing \(\eta\) gives the same lower bound in modulus. The preceding summability therefore bounds this sum holomorphically. Together with the suffix noise estimate it proves a uniform holomorphic bound for \(G_{\mu}\), which is nonnegative on the real slice.

Recall that \(F = f (p / M) f (- p / M)\), \(A_{\mu} = Fr_M (p_{\mu} / M) r_M (- p_{\mu} / M)\) and \(B_{\mu} = A_{\mu} + \lambda_M G_{\mu}\). The principal filter bounds give positive real lower bounds for \(F, A_{\mu}\), and hence for \(B_{\mu}\). All three have uniform complex upper bounds. Cauchy estimates on a slightly larger cube therefore give uniformly bounded reciprocals on a smaller one. The identity in (5.9) now supplies the real lower bound for \(h\): since \(B_{\mu} \leqslant C_B\),

\(\displaystyle h (p) = \frac{\sum_{\mu} |q_{\mu} (p) |^2 / B_{\mu} (p)}{\lambda_M (p)} \geqslant c / C_B \quad (p \ne 0) .\)

We used uniform comparison of both \(\lambda (p)\) and \(\lambda_M (p)\) with \(|p|^2\) on the real cube. At zero \(h = 1\). Its defining formula gives a holomorphic upper bound using the reciprocals already obtained, and another Cauchy estimate gives a uniformly bounded reciprocal for \(h\). Thus \((H_0)_{\mu \nu} = (hB_{\mu} B_{\nu})^{- 1}\) has all the asserted bounds. The order of this argument is important: the lower bound for \(h\) uses \(B_{\mu}\), not an assumed inverse bound for \(h\). Periodicity holds first on real overlaps by permutation of the aliases in the original covariance recurrence. Analytic continuation then joins the cubes to a strip. The same Fourier symbol works on every finite-period grid.\(\Box\)

This proves uniform bounds for arbitrary finite histories. Convergence for a prescribed sequence of growing histories is stronger and is not a consequence of this proposition alone.

5.6Exact transports and the curvature defect

Recall that \(H_0\) is the two-form metric in (5.8); \(H\) denotes its positive completion from (5.10), agreeing with \(H_0\) on exact two-forms. Fine and coarse subscripts below specify the scale of each metric.

We describe the algebra at one real nonzero coarse momentum. This also specifies which transport must be estimated. The use of aliases and block-periodic Fourier coordinates is standard; see [BFKT16a]. Write \(\theta_l = (p + 2 \pi l) / L\), \(\delta_r = Ld_r (\theta_l)\) for the rescaled fine exterior derivative, and \(d_r = d_r (p)\) for the coarse one. Let

\(\displaystyle D_l = L^{- 2} \text{diag} (c_{f, \mu} (\theta_l)), \quad D = \text{diag} (c_{c, \mu} (p)), \quad x = d_0, \quad x_l = \delta_0 .\)

For this paragraph \(H, H_l\) denote the unmodified curvature metrics. Set

\(\displaystyle R = d_1^{\ast} Hd_1, \quad R_l = \delta_1^{\ast} H_l \delta_1, \quad \rho = x^{\ast} D^{- 1} x, \quad \pi = \rho^{- 1} x^{\ast} D^{- 1} .\)

The gauge quotient identity gives

\(\displaystyle R = D^{- 1} - \rho^{- 1} D^{- 1} xx^{\ast} D^{- 1}, \qquad DR = I - x \pi .\)

The fine formulas have subscript \(l\). Define

\(\displaystyle m_l = w_L (\theta_l)^j, \qquad F_{r, l} = m_l \bigwedge^r \text{diag} (r_L (\theta_{l, \mu})) .\)

The link identity and the covariance recurrence say, respectively,

\(\displaystyle F_{r + 1, l} \delta_r = d_r F_{r, l}, \qquad \sum_l F_{1, l} D_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} = D - b^{- 1} I. \) (5.12)

Introduce the positive scalar \(\alpha = \sum_l |m_l |^2 \text{tr} \hspace{0.17em} D_l\), and put \(e_l = \bar{m}_l \text{tr} \hspace{0.17em} D_l / \alpha\), \(y_l = |m_l |^2 \rho / \rho_l\), and \(C = Hd_1 Dd_1^{\ast}\). The rescaled one-form and two-form transports are

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \mathcal{A}_l & = D_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} R + e_l x_l \pi, \hspace{3cm} \text{(5.13)}\\ \mathcal{T}_l & = H_l \delta_1 D_l F_{1, l}^{\ast} d_1^{\ast} + y_l F_{2, l}^{\ast} (I - C) . \hspace{3cm} \text{(5.14)} \end{array}\)

Their defining identities are

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlllll} R_l \mathcal{A}_l & = F_{1, l}^{\ast} R, & \delta_1^{\ast} \mathcal{T}_l & = F_{1, l}^{\ast} d_1^{\ast}, & \mathcal{T}_l Hd_1 & = H_l \delta_1 \mathcal{A}_l, \hspace{3cm} \text{(5.15)}\\ \sum_l F_{1, l} \mathcal{A}_l & = I - b^{- 1} R. & & & & \hspace{3cm} \text{(5.16)} \end{array}\)

These follow by substituting the quotient formula for \(R\) and using (5.12); in the last equality the gradient contribution is \(x \pi \sum_l m_l e_l = x \pi\). In particular the term \(I - C\) cannot simply be omitted: it is annihilated by \(d_1^{\ast}\) and annihilates \(Hd_1\), but controls the transport on the complementary two-forms.

The physical kernels from a coarse site to a fine site \(Ly + r\) are obtained from the alias sums

\(\displaystyle L^{- 1} \sum_l e^{i \theta_l \cdot r} \mathcal{A}_l (p), \qquad L^{- 2} \sum_l e^{i \theta_l \cdot r} \mathcal{T}_l (p)\)

by coarse Fourier inversion. Denote the resulting operators by \(W\) and \(T\). Restoring counting adjoints gives

\(\displaystyle T^{\ast} d_{1, f} = d_{1, c} Q, \qquad TH_c d_{1, c} = H_f d_{1, f} W, \qquad QW = I - b^{- 1} d_{1, c}^{\ast} H_c d_{1, c} . \) (5.17)

Here \(Q\) is the straight averaging operator in this construction. The same statements for a block average containing a gradient correction require its corresponding choice of the gradient part of \(W\).

The apparent poles in (5.14) can be removed using the principal covariance split. We record the crucial cancellation to avoid concealing a uniformity assumption. Write \(s = \lambda_{M_c} (p)\), \(D = s^{- 1} \text{diag} (B_{\mu})\), \(D_0 = s^{- 1} \text{diag} (B_{0, \mu})\), and

\(\displaystyle D = F_{1, 0} D_0 F_{1, 0}^{\ast} + U, \qquad U = \text{diag} (u_{\mu}) .\)

The tail \(U\) consists of nonprincipal aliases and the current noise; it is uniformly holomorphic by the estimates just proved. Let \(z_{\mu} = u_{\mu} / B_{\mu}\). Then

\(\displaystyle \frac{|m_0 r_L (\theta_{0, \mu}) |^2 B_{0, \mu}}{B_{\mu}} = 1 - sz_{\mu} .\)

With \(J = F_{2, 0}^{- 1}\), define the bounded analytic matrix

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{B}= H_0 \left[ \text{diag}_{\mu < \nu} (z_{\mu} + z_{\nu}) - s \bigwedge^2 \text{diag} (z_{\mu}) \right] .\)

Here \(H_0\) means the fine metric at alias zero, not a new modification of the metric. Entrywise multiplication gives \(s\mathcal{B}= H_0 - y_0 F_{2, 0}^{\ast} HF_{2, 0}\), and hence

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{T}_0 = y_0 F_{2, 0}^{\ast} + (\mathcal{B}Jd_1 \text{diag} (B_{\mu}) - H_0 Jd_1 U) d_1^{\ast} .\)

All factors are regular at zero. For \(\mathcal{A}_0\), the corresponding principal denominator is \(s \alpha = |m_0 |^2 \sum_{\mu} B_{0, \mu} + s \alpha_{\ne 0}\), which is bounded below on the real cube. Its reciprocal is therefore uniformly holomorphic. Subtracting the leading diagonal term in \(\mathcal{A}_0\) leaves the regular factor

\(\displaystyle m_0^{- 1} (u_{\mu} / B_{\mu} - \alpha_{\ne 0} / (s \alpha)) .\)

For nonprincipal aliases the denominators have the lower bounds used in Proposition 5.1. These formulas give bounded principal columns and bounds \(C \Gamma (l)^{- j}\) for both nonprincipal columns on a smaller cube. Adjoints in these analytic formulas mean transpose at opposite momentum, with \(l\) replaced by \(- l\).

Each fine difference in the alias sum contributes \(e^{i \theta_{l, \mu}} - 1\), bounded by \(CL^{- 1} (1 + \max_{\nu} |l_{\nu} |)\). The summability estimate for \(\Gamma\) therefore gives, for \(r \leqslant j - 2\), exponentially weighted coarse Fourier coefficient bounds of order \(L^{- 1 - r}\) for \(\nabla_f^r W\) and \(L^{- 2 - r}\) for \(\nabla_f^r T\). Fourier contour displacement is the usual conversion of strip analyticity to exponential coefficient decay. It is performed after summing the aliases, whose permutation under \(p \mapsto p + 2 \pi n\) gives periodicity. When a fine difference crosses a block boundary, its coarse argument is shifted by one block; this is included by the phase \(e^{i \theta_l \cdot r}\) and must not be dropped. At zero momentum the principal columns are \(I\) and the other columns vanish, so the identities extend there without choosing a massless Gaussian normalization.

5.6.1A positive factorization of the defect

The next argument uses exterior-complex exactness together with the bounds just obtained. Exactness for a regular sequence is standard Koszul algebra [The, Lemma 15.31.2]. The quantitative version needed here preserves exponential coefficient bounds: if an exponentially summable matrix row \(A\) on positive-degree forms satisfies \(Ad_r = 0\), it can be written \(A = Vd_{r + 1}\) on a smaller strip, with a fixed norm cost. One obtains this version by successively dividing \(A (p) - A (p) |_{p_a = 0}\) by \(e^{ip_a} - 1\) and contracting the \(a\)th exterior index. The elementary quotient

\(\displaystyle \frac{e^{inp_a} - 1}{e^{ip_a} - 1} = \sum_{h = 0}^{n - 1} e^{ihp_a} \quad (n > 0)\)

and the corresponding negative sum for \(n < 0\) show that a division costs at most \(C / \eta\) when width \(\eta\) is reduced to \(\eta / 2\). There are only four coordinates. This is the usual coordinate proof of Koszul exactness, with the coefficient norm retained. Applying it on both sides shows that a symmetric two-form row \(E\) with \(Ed_1 = d_1^{\ast} E = 0\) has \(E = d_2^{\ast} C_0 d_2\), with \(C_0\) symmetric and exponentially summable. For completeness, first choose a left lift \(d_2^{\ast} W_0 = E\). Since \(d_2^{\ast} W_0 d_1 = 0\), exactness gives \(W_0 d_1 = d_3^{\ast} V_0\). The top differential \(d_3^{\ast}\) has trivial kernel on analytic four-form coefficients, so \(V_0 d_0 = 0\). A right lift gives \(V_0 = X_0 d_1\). Thus \(W_1 = W_0 - d_3^{\ast} X_0\) satisfies both \(W_1 d_1 = 0\) and \(d_2^{\ast} W_1 = E\). Lift once more to write \(W_1 = C_0 d_2\), and replace \(C_0\) by \((C_0 + C_0^{\ast}) / 2\). All these lifts act on the infinite lattice coefficients before periodization; finite-period harmonic modes do not supply the analytic exactness used here.

Put \(B_c = H_c^{- 1}\), \(B_f = H_f^{- 1}\) for the unmodified metrics, and

\(\displaystyle Z_0 = B_c - T^{\ast} B_f T - b^{- 1} d_{1, c} d_{1, c}^{\ast} .\)

Equation (5.17) gives \(Z_0 H_c d_{1, c} = 0\). Applying the two-sided lift to \(H_c Z_0 H_c\) and the one-sided lift to \(d_{2, f} B_f TH_c\) produces

\(\displaystyle Z_0 = (d_{2, c} B_c)^{\ast} C_0 (d_{2, c} B_c), \qquad Sd_{2, c} B_c = d_{2, f} B_f T. \) (5.18)

The estimates are \(\|C_0 \| \leqslant C_0'\) and \(\|S^{\ast} S\| \leqslant C_1 L^{- 2}\). Indeed a fixed fine-residue entry of \(T\) or \(B_f T\) has weighted coefficient bound \(CL^{- 2}\) and one fine difference has bound \(CL^{- 3}\). A Gram product sums over \(L^4\) residues. Its bounds are thus \(L^4 (CL^{- 2})^2 = O (1)\) and \(L^4 (CL^{- 3})^2 = O (L^{- 2})\), respectively. The lift acts on coarse exterior indices separately for every fine residue, so it introduces no further residue sum.

Modify the inverse metrics as in (5.10), with coefficients \(\zeta_c, \zeta_f\). Write the modified inverse metrics as \(\tilde{B}_c, \tilde{B}_f\), and \(\tilde{H}_c = \tilde{B}_c^{- 1}\). Set

\(\displaystyle D_c = (I + \zeta_c d_{2, c} B_c d_{2, c}^{\ast})^{- 1}, \qquad R_0 = C_0 + \zeta_c I - \zeta_f S^{\ast} S.\)

Expansion of (5.18) gives the exact identity

\(\displaystyle \tilde{H}_c (\tilde{B}_c - T^{\ast} \tilde{B}_f T - b^{- 1} d_{1, c} d_{1, c}^{\ast}) \tilde{H}_c = d_{2, c}^{\ast} D_c^{\ast} R_0 D_c d_{2, c} . \) (5.19)

If \(\zeta_c \geqslant C_0' + 2\) and \(L^2 \geqslant \zeta_f C_1\), then \(R_0 \geqslant I\). Since \(D_c^{- 1}\) has a uniform upper bound for fixed \(\zeta_c\), the three-form metric \(D_c^{\ast} R_0 D_c\) is uniformly strictly positive. Its inverse and positive square root have exponential coefficient bounds on a smaller strip by the usual analytic functional calculus. This proves positivity on the whole three-form space.

For each adjacent pair of stages choose the coarse modification large enough for its finitely many incoming possibilities. Fix these coefficients before choosing the block ratios. Then the condition \(L^2 \geqslant \zeta_f C_1\) is a finite lower bound on each permitted ratio. No constant uniform over all smoothing orders is asserted: the estimates are uniform over histories ending at a fixed order.

5.7Convergence at a fixed scale

The curvature metrics and transport operators in this section are those of Sections 5.4 and 5.6; their histories now vary while the observation scale stays fixed.

The preceding uniform bounds also give convergence for the particular nested histories used in the construction. The nesting is essential: fix the ratios, smoothing orders and modification coefficients at every scale, and increase only the terminal cutoff. At a fixed scale \(m\), the history with terminal scale \(N + 1\) extends the history with terminal scale \(N\) at its fine end. An arbitrary sequence of bounded histories need not converge.

We first record how to identify limits on the infinite lattice. Write a translation-invariant row as \(K_{ab} (z)\), where \(z \in \mathbb{Z}^4\) and \(a, b\) range over fixed finite coordinate sets. For an adjacent-scale row, include the finitely many fine residues in these coordinate sets. Suppose

\(\displaystyle \sum_z e^{\sigma |z|_1} |K_{N, ab} (z) | \leqslant C \qquad (\sigma > 0)\)

uniformly in \(N\). Every subsequence has a further subsequence converging in each coefficient, by diagonal extraction. Its limit has the same bound, by finite partial sums. For every \(0 \leqslant \tau < \sigma\), coefficient convergence implies

\(\displaystyle \sum_z e^{\tau |z|_1} |K_{N, ab} (z) - K_{ab} (z) | \longrightarrow 0. \) (5.20)

Indeed each summand is bounded by \(2 Ce^{- (\sigma - \tau) |z|_1}\), a summable sequence. If the symbols have a specified limit on a dense set of real momenta, any two cluster symbols agree there, hence everywhere by continuity. Fourier inversion then identifies every coefficient. Alternatively, agreement of cluster matrices on an unbounded sequence of torus sides suffices: for fixed \(z\), the periodized entry is \(\sum_h K (z + nh)\), and its terms with \(h \ne 0\) tend to zero as \(n \to \infty\). Either criterion proves uniqueness of the cluster limit and therefore convergence of the full sequence. This argument identifies the lattice-cover coefficients, not merely a matrix on one fixed torus.

Proposition 5.2. (Convergence of the selected Gaussian rows) Fix a scale and the nested schedule just described. The unmodified curvature metrics, their inverses, the adjacent one- and two-form transports, and the modified metrics of (5.10) converge in the sense of (5.20), at every width strictly smaller than a common positive width available at that scale. The same conclusion holds for the specified coordinate exterior lifts, their amended three-form metric in (5.19), and their positive square roots. It also holds for a positive square root of \(\tilde{H} - cI\) whenever the fixed direct coefficient \(c\) is chosen strictly below the uniform lower bound of \(\tilde{H}\). All limits are for the actual chosen rows on the cover and their periodizations. No choice of a subsequence, matrix eigenbasis or square-root signs is made.

Proof. Let \(d = N - m\) be the history depth and \(M_{d, m}\) its total dilation. Let \(u_{d, m, \mu} (p)\) be the squared modulus of its accumulated directional filter, evaluated at \(p / M_{d, m}\). Thus \(u_{0, m, \mu} = 1\); the filter is the product of the centered-box and link factors used in (5.11). Write \(g_{m, \mu} (\theta)\) for the single-step directional filter at scale \(m\), including the link average \(r_{L_m} (\theta_{\mu})\). Composition gives

\(\displaystyle u_{d + 1, m, \mu} (p) = |g_{m, \mu} (p / L_m) |^2 u_{d, m + 1, \mu} (p / L_m) .\)

Every factor is an average of unit complex numbers on real momenta, possibly multiplied by a unit phase. Its modulus is at most one. Induction, with the schedule fixed at each scale, gives \(0 \leqslant u_{d + 1, m, \mu} (p) \leqslant u_{d, m, \mu} (p) \leqslant 1\). Thus the directional filter squares converge at every real \(p\), including any fixed reciprocal translate.

The accumulated noise is also convergent. Extending the history adds one independent noise contribution propagated through all the previously fixed coarser steps. The old contributions are unchanged, and every new contribution is nonnegative at real momenta. The accumulated noise therefore increases with depth. The suffix estimate in Proposition 5.1 bounds it uniformly, and the noise at the last blocking step contributes one. Its limit is finite and at least one. This argument concerns the actual nested noise list; it is not a monotonicity assertion for arbitrary histories of the same length.

For the initial covariance contribution, extend the finite nonprincipal alias sum to all \(z \in \mathbb{Z}^4 \setminus \{0\}\) by zero outside the centered representatives modulo \(M = M_{d, m}\). Its summand at an eventually included fixed \(z\) is

\(\displaystyle \frac{u_{d, m, \mu} (p + 2 \pi z)}{M^2 \lambda ((p + 2 \pi z) / M)} .\)

The denominator tends to \(|p + 2 \pi z|^2\), which is positive for nonzero \(z\) and \(p\) in the base cube. The alias estimates above dominate these summands by \(C \prod_{\nu} (1 + |z_{\nu} |)^{- 2}\), independently of depth. Dominated convergence proves convergence of the sum. At \(p \ne 0\) the principal summand converges by the same argument without an alias sum. Consequently each directional covariance \(c_{N, m, \mu} (p)\) has a finite limit at real \(p \ne 0\), bounded below by \(b^{- 1}\).

The curvature metric is the rational expression (5.8) in these covariances and the exterior derivatives. Its scalar quotient denominator is \(\sum_{\mu} |q_{\mu} (p) |^2 / c_{N, m, \mu} (p)\); its limit is positive away from zero. Thus its symbol converges there. The uniform analytic bounds already proved and the cluster-limit criterion identify one limit on the cover, including at zero. Inverses have the same conclusion. This avoids assigning a finite massless covariance to the zero mode.

For adjacent transports the ratio \(L = L_m\) is fixed as \(N\) increases. At \(p \ne 0\), every one of its finitely many aliases \(\theta_l = (p + 2 \pi l) / L\) is a nonzero real momentum. All covariance factors in (5.14) therefore converge. The denominators \(\rho, \rho_l\) have positive limits, and so does

\(\displaystyle \alpha = \sum_l |m_l |^2 \text{tr} \hspace{0.17em} D_l :\)

the principal centered-box factor is nonzero on the real base cube and \(D_l\) has a positive diagonal limit. Hence the finite alias formulas and their Fourier reconstruction converge. The previously proved uniform exponential bounds identify unique cover limits for \(W, T\). At exceptional zero momenta uniqueness follows from continuity of cluster symbols, rather than division by a vanishing expression.

Products preserve this convergence by the weighted convolution inequality, with the fixed finite coordinate multiplicity included. For uniformly bounded inverse rows, cluster limits satisfy the inverse identity on each torus; uniqueness of the finite-dimensional inverse and recovery from unbounded periods identify their cover limit. The coordinate exterior lifts used in (5.18) are also continuous after a width decrease. Each elementary operation is coordinate evaluation or a divided difference

\(\displaystyle \frac{\hat{K}_N (p) - \hat{K}_N (p|_{p_a = 0})}{e^{ip_a} - 1} .\)

Its numerator converges, its denominator is nonzero on a dense set, and its smaller-width bound was proved in the exterior construction. The cluster criterion consequently applies through the finitely many coordinate operations. This requires the fixed coordinate lift of that construction, not a fresh arbitrary solution of the exterior identity.

It follows that \(C_0, S, D_c\) and \(D_c^{\ast} R_0 D_c\) in (5.19) converge, as do the modified metrics and inverses. Finally consider one of the stated positive square roots. Its uniform analytic bound gives cluster rows. On each finite torus their matrices are positive semidefinite and have the same square, namely the limit of the underlying positive matrix. The positive semidefinite square root is unique by the finite-dimensional spectral theorem. Thus the cluster matrices agree on every torus, and period recovery identifies the cover row. This proves convergence for the chosen roots themselves, not merely for their squares.\(\Box\)

In particular all canonical path-row coefficients obtained from these rows converge, with a strictly positive gap between the available width and the width used to sum paths. Finite products, fixed exterior operations and the fixed positive factorizations preserve this conclusion. The actual block average correction introduced below is a fixed finite-range row at the selected scale, so its application to the convergent straight transport also preserves convergence. These are statements about the quadratic data. They do not imply cutoff continuity of the nonlinear activity arrays or of marked integral maps; those require their own stability arguments.

5.8Uniform inverse bounds: the hypotheses that matter

A spectral gap in Euclidean norm is not, by itself, a bound for an inverse in supremum norm independent of volume. One sufficient framework is an exponentially local matrix with a uniform positive gap. To state its quantitative content, assign each coordinate \(i\) a lattice site \(a_i\), with uniformly bounded coordinate multiplicity per site, and define

\(\displaystyle \|T\|_{\eta} = \max \left\{ \sup_i \sum_j e^{\eta d (a_i, a_j)} |T_{ij} |, \sup_j \sum_i e^{\eta d (a_i, a_j)} |T_{ij} | \right\} .\)

Here \(d\) is periodic lattice distance. Suppose \(H = H^{\ast} \geqslant mI\) and \(\|H\|_{\eta_0} \leqslant C\) uniformly in the volume. For some \(0 < \eta < \eta_0\), depending only on \(m, C, \eta_0\) and the coordinate multiplicity, \(H^{- 1}\) has uniformly bounded exponentially weighted row and column sums, after reducing the exponent once more if necessary. This is the usual weighted resolvent estimate behind exponentially local lattice propagators; compare [Bał84]. It follows by conjugating with \(e^{\eta d (a_i, a_{i_0})}\) and applying the resolvent identity to the resulting small perturbation. In particular \(\|H^{- 1} \|_{\infty \to \infty}\) is bounded independently of volume.

For analytic momentum-dependent matrices, an analogous perturbation argument must use holomorphic coordinates. Suppose \(K (p)\) is holomorphic in a strip of width \(\sigma\) and, in fixed coordinates, \(\|K (p)\| \leqslant A\) there, while at real \(x\) its least singular value is at least \(a > 0\). Cauchy estimates and the inverse perturbation lemma give a smaller strip, depending only on \(a, A, \sigma\), on which \(K^{- 1}\) is holomorphic and uniformly bounded. This statement is dimension independent.

In weighted Fourier spaces the weights often involve \(|q (\text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} p) |\) and are not holomorphic. The preceding argument is justified if, at every real anchor \(x\), the matrices defining the weighted norms have holomorphic replacements in a fixed neighborhood of \(x\), uniformly equivalent to the original norms, and coinciding with them on the vertical slice \(\text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} p = x\). One applies Cauchy's estimate in these replacement coordinates. A bound in nonholomorphic coordinates alone does not justify differentiating the weighted matrix. This distinction is essential when the weight degenerates with a scale parameter.

5.9The actual block average and the forest coordinates

We now identify the nonlinear coordinates behind the Gaussian calculation. Fix \(j, L\), a coarse period \(s \geqslant 100 (j + 8)\), and in each centered block \(Ly +\mathbb{B}_{(L - 1) / 2}\) a spanning tree rooted at \(Ly\). Trees contain only internal block edges. Denote by \(\iota_F\) extension from free-edge coordinates to all fine edges, with value zero on tree edges. For coarse links \(V\), their replica is

\(\displaystyle (\text{rep} \hspace{0.17em} V)_e = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1, & \text{parent} (e_-) = \text{parent} (e_+),\\ V_{y, \mu}, & \text{parent} (e_-) = y, \text{parent} (e_+) = y + e_{\mu} . \end{array} \right.\)

The centered-block convention and odd \(L\) make these the only possibilities for a positive fine edge in direction \(\mu\). In particular the replica is one on every tree edge. The actual forest chart is

\(\displaystyle U (v, \psi)_e = \exp ((\iota_F \psi)_e) (\text{rep} \hspace{0.17em} \exp v)_e . \) (5.21)

It is a chart on the tree slice, not a choice of independent variables on tree edges. Its derivative at the identity is exactly

\(\displaystyle DU (0, 0) [h, z] = \text{rep} \hspace{0.17em} h + \iota_F z, \qquad DU (0, 0) [0, z] = \iota_F z. \) (5.22)

All color coordinates are orthonormal for the trace metric. Thus \(\| \iota_F z\|_2 = \|z\|_2\) in counting norms. This identifies the vertical coordinates used by the Hessian with the arrays in the tree inequality below; no volume-dependent coordinate conversion occurs.

5.9.1A common analytic branch for all local labels

The minimizing frame and block average of Chapter 4 are real analytic near the identity. For the frame this follows directly from its chart equation: its quadratic objective at zero is \(\tfrac{1}{2} \text{av}_{r, r'} |h_r - h_{r'} |^2\), positive definite after \(h_0 = 0\) is imposed. For the block average, its Hessian in the group variable is the identity. Complexify these finite-dimensional real analytic chart equations and apply the analytic implicit-function theorem [Leb, Theorem 1.3.8]. The resulting critical points are the unique minimizers already identified in Lemma 4.1, after reducing the neighborhood if necessary.

At fixed \(j, L\) every such equation reads a translate of one fixed finite patch. Choose a radius in the supremum norm of all its input logarithms on which these analytic extensions and their derivatives are bounded. There are finitely many directions and local tree shapes at fixed \(L\); take their common radius and bounds. Periodic pullback to a patch has supremum norm at most one, so these bounds are independent of \(s\). Decrease this radius until every loop test and minimum-cost test is strictly on its minimizing branch. Every local label then selects the same frame and block average as a function of the full fine field on the whole common ball. Redundant uniform branch masses are constant there. This is equality of the local action functions before stationarity is imposed, not merely equality along a stationary graph. It concerns the uncut action; shrinking smooth cutoff plateaux are treated separately.

Let \(a\) be an infinitesimal fine field. Write \(I_a (r \to r' ; o)\) for its signed sum along a coordinate path in the frame patch at \(Ly\). The linearized minimizing-frame equation gives

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} \tau_y (r) & = - \text{av}_{r', o} \hspace{0.17em} I_a (Ly + r \to Ly + r' ; o), & & \\ H_{y, r} (a) & = \tau_y (r) - \tau_y (0), & Ra (y) & = - \sum_r w_r^{\text{av}} H_{y, r} (a) . \hspace{3cm} \text{(5.23)} \end{array}\)

Indeed its equation is \(H_r - \text{av}_{r'} \hspace{0.17em} H_{r'} = - \text{av}_{r', o} \hspace{0.17em} I_a (r \to r' ; o)\), and \(H_0 = 0\) fixes the additive constant. Differentiating the actual connector and then its block average therefore yields

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{Q}a = Ma + d_{0, c} Ra, \qquad \mathcal{Q}d_{0, f} \phi = d_{0, c} (\phi |_{L \Lambda_s}) . \) (5.24)

Here \(M\) is exactly the straight length-\(L\) average with the convolution weights used in (5.6). The second identity follows also by differentiating gauge covariance at block roots. Each route has length \(O_j (L)\); hence \(R\) has finite coarse range and absolute row sum at most \(C_j L\). These statements include the root normalization. Dropping it would change \(\mathcal{Q}\).

5.9.2The tree comparison and the actual Hessian

For a fine edge \(e\), join its endpoints to their block roots along the chosen trees. In the tree slice the resulting walk has transport \(U_e\). It lies in one or two adjacent blocks and has length at most \(2 L^4 - 1\). In the macro axial gauge of Section 4.3, its distance from the identity is bounded by this length times the sum of bond distances from the identity in the macro-box. The first-power frame estimate controls the two root-to-frame discrepancies; the block average estimate controls the coarse root-to-root transport. Thus

\(\displaystyle \chi \left( U_e (\text{rep} \hspace{0.17em} V)_e^{- 1} \right) \leqslant C_{j, L} \mathcal{P}_y (U) +\boldsymbol{1}_{\{e \text{ crosses blocks} \}} \chi (V_{y, \mu} Q_{y, \mu} (U)^{- 1}) . \) (5.25)

For an internal edge the walk closes at its root and only the first term is needed. For a crossing edge, insert the block average between the two root frames and use the triangle inequality for the normalized Hilbert–Schmidt distance. The endpoint errors are bounded by the sum in (4.13), so this step requires no pointwise frame estimate not already proved. The constant is allowed to depend on \(L\); the stronger pre-\(L\) factor in (4.12) is not required for this use.

Apply (5.25) to \(U = \exp (\epsilon a)\) with \(a\) zero on tree edges, and \(V = 1\). Divide by \(| \epsilon |\) and let \(\epsilon \to 0\). The first-order variation of the distance from the identity is the color norm, so

\(\displaystyle |a_e | \leqslant C_{j, L} \sum_{p \text{ in the macro-patch at } y} | (d_{1, f} a)_p | +\boldsymbol{1}_{\{e \text{ crosses blocks} \}} | (\mathcal{Q}a)_{y, \mu} | .\)

Square, use the finite macro-patch cardinality, and sum over edges. A fine plaquette belongs to a bounded number of macro-patches, and each coarse edge occurs for at most a fixed number of fine edges at fixed \(L\). Consequently

\(\displaystyle \|a\|_2^2 \leqslant B_{j, L} \|d_{1, f} a\|_2^2 + C_{j, L} \|\mathcal{Q}a\|_2^2, \quad a = \iota_F z. \) (5.26)

Both constants are uniform in the period and in the allowed trees. The use of periodic lifts preserves the bounded multiplicity.

Let \(\mathcal{A}_f\) be the uncut fine action, without the common inverse coupling or inherited loop action, and set

\(\displaystyle f (v, \psi) =\mathcal{A}_f (U (v, \psi)) + b \sum_{e \text{ coarse}} w ((\exp v)_e Q_e (U (v, \psi))^{- 1}) . \) (5.27)

Assume its actual quadratic Taylor coefficient is \(K_f = d_{1, f}^{\ast} H_f d_{1, f}\) with \(H_f \geqslant \varepsilon I > 0\). This coefficient is read from the same path rows in \(\mathcal{A}_f\): each quadratic path square contributes the square of its linearized curvature row, and atoms of curvature degree at least three contribute nothing at second order. Identifying their sum with a chosen metric \(H_f\) is an exact coefficient condition on the incoming path action.

The first derivatives of both terms of (5.27) vanish at the identity. Therefore the second derivative of the forest chart does not enter its Hessian. By (5.22), for \(a = \text{rep} \hspace{0.17em} h + \iota_F z\) and \(a' = \text{rep} \hspace{0.17em} h' + \iota_F z'\), it is precisely

\(\displaystyle D^2 f (0) [(h, z), (h', z')] = \langle d_{1, f} a, H_f d_{1, f} a' \rangle + b \langle h -\mathcal{Q}a, h' -\mathcal{Q}a' \rangle . \) (5.28)

No derivative of the nonlinear block average beyond its actual first derivative is missing here: its residual vanishes at zero. In particular,

\(\displaystyle D_{\psi}^2 f (0, 0) \geqslant \frac{\min (\varepsilon, b)}{1 + B_{j, L} + C_{j, L}} I. \) (5.29)

This proves the vertical gap for the actual forest chart, rather than assuming it from the flat Fourier calculation.

5.9.3A smooth representative of the stationary tangent

Let \(W_s\) denote the straight-average transport already constructed in Section 5.6. Its identities in physical coordinates are

\(\displaystyle K_f W_s = M^{\ast} K_c, \qquad MW_s = I - b^{- 1} K_c .\)

These hold for the same incoming metric and penalty as (5.28). Since \(K_c d_{0, c} = 0\), replacing \(M\) by \(\mathcal{Q}= M + d_{0, c} R\) does not change the right side of the first identity. The second identity does require a gradient correction.

Choose a smooth compactly supported cardinal partition of unity \(\varphi\) on \(\mathbb{R}^4\): \(\varphi (n) =\boldsymbol{1}_{n = 0}\) for \(n \in \mathbb{Z}^4\) and \(\sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}^4} \varphi (x - n) = 1\). For example, in one dimension divide a smooth bump supported in \((- 1, 1)\) and positive there by the sum of its integer translates, and take the fourfold product. Define a periodic extension of coarse site fields to fine sites by

\(\displaystyle (Eh) (x) = \sum_{y \in \Lambda_s} \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}^4} \varphi (x / L - y - sk) h (y) .\)

It equals \(h (y)\) at \(x = Ly\), is supported within one coarse block of its input, and has fine differences bounded by \(\| \nabla_f^r E\|_{\infty \to \infty} \leqslant C_r L^{- r}\). Now put

\(\displaystyle W_a = W_s - d_{0, f} ERW_s . \) (5.30)

By (5.24),

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{Q}W_a = MW_s = I - b^{- 1} K_c, \qquad K_f W_a =\mathcal{Q}^{\ast} K_c, \qquad d_{1, f} W_a = d_{1, f} W_s . \) (5.31)

Thus \(W_a\) minimizes the actual quadratic energy and has the same reduced quadratic form \(K_c\). This holds also on constant modes: \(E\) extends constants to constants, gradients have zero curl, and no inverse of a zero-momentum Laplacian has been introduced.

The correction preserves the needed smooth interpolation bounds. The finite-range row \(R\) has size \(C_j L\) and the already proved kernel of \(W_s\) has size \(C_j L^{- 1}\) with exponential coarse decay. Their composition \(RW_s\) therefore has an \(O_j (1)\) exponentially decaying coarse kernel. Applying \(r + 1\) fine differences to \(E\) gives

\(\displaystyle | \nabla_f^r W_a (Ly + x, y') | \leqslant C_{j, r} L^{- 1 - r} e^{- c_j d (y, y')}, \quad r \leqslant j - 2, \) (5.32)

with the same permitted orders as for \(W_s\). The extension adds only bounded coarse range, so it preserves exponential summability. This argument uses finite-difference bounds of a cardinal extension, not an assumption that a discontinuous block-constant extension is smooth.

Finally adjust \(W_a v\) by the unique gradient \(d_{0, f} \phi\) whose site potential is zero at every block root and makes all tree edges vanish. It is obtained successively along each tree. Such a gradient leaves \(\mathcal{Q}W_a v\) unchanged by (5.24), and leaves its curl unchanged. Write the resulting representative as \(W_F v\). Coercivity (5.29) makes it the unique minimizer in the forest slice. Once a stationary graph \(\psi = \rho (v)\) has been constructed, differentiating its equation and using (5.28) proves

\(\displaystyle \text{rep} \hspace{0.17em} v + \iota_F D \rho (0) v = W_F v. \) (5.33)

The smooth representative \(W_a\) and the forest representative \(W_F\) are distinct. Estimate (5.32) is asserted for \(W_a\), not for tree coordinates. Gauge-invariant quadratic insertions see the same curl in either representative.

5.10A stationary background on a uniform neighborhood

We first state the quantitative implicit-function estimate needed for the nonlinear action. Let \(x\) be the coarse coordinates and \(y\) the free fine coordinates after gauge fixing. Both carry the supremum norm. Write \(f (x, y)\) for the action including the block penalty, and \(F (x, y) = D_y f (x, y)\). All norms below are operator norms induced by these supremum norms.

Proposition 5.3. (Uniform stationary graph) Suppose \(F\) is holomorphic on a product neighborhood \(\|x\|_{\infty} < r_0\), \(\|y\|_{\infty} < r_0\), with \(F (0, 0) = 0\). Let \(H_{\ast} = D_y F (0, 0)\) be invertible and assume, uniformly in the number of coordinates,

\(\displaystyle \|H_{\ast}^{- 1} \| \leqslant J, \qquad \|D_y F (x, y) - H_{\ast} \| \leqslant C \max (\|x\|_{\infty}, \|y\|_{\infty}), \qquad \|D_x F (x, y)\| \leqslant B.\)

Choose \(0 < r_y < r_0\) and \(0 < r_x \leqslant r_y\) so that \(JCr_y \leqslant 1 / 4\) and \(JBr_x \leqslant r_y / 4\). Then for \(\|x\|_{\infty} < r_x\) there is a unique solution \(y = \rho (x)\) of \(F (x, y) = 0\) in \(\|y\|_{\infty} \leqslant r_y\). It is holomorphic, satisfies \(\rho (0) = 0\) and \(\| \rho (x)\|_{\infty} \leqslant r_y / 2\), and obeys

\(\displaystyle D \rho (x) = - [D_y F (x, \rho (x))]^{- 1} D_x F (x, \rho (x)), \qquad \|D \rho (x)\| \leqslant \tfrac{4}{3} JB.\)

This is the quantitative contraction form of the implicit-function theorem: apply it to \(y - H_{\ast}^{- 1} F (x, y)\) on the closed \(r_y\)-ball. The two displayed smallness conditions give contraction constant \(1 / 4\) and displacement of the origin at most \(r_y / 4\). We use the standard theorem rather than repeat its proof; holomorphic regularity is the analytic implicit-function theorem [Leb, Theorem 1.3.8]. The real smooth version is also a direct application of the implicit-function theorem [Rud76, Theorem 9.28]. For a merely \(C^N\) real action the conclusion is \(C^{N - 1}\) for \(\rho\), not holomorphy.

In applications the operator bounds in this proposition should be deduced from local derivative estimates before any dimension dependent conversion of norms. For example, suppose each coarse or fine coordinate has an anchor \(a_i\), the total number of coordinates anchored at any site is bounded uniformly in the volume, and, for \(2 \leqslant q \leqslant N\), every rooted derivative satisfies

\(\displaystyle | \partial_{i_1} \cdots \partial_{i_q} f (w) | \leqslant C \exp \left( - \eta \sum_{h = 1}^q d (a_{i_t}, a_{i_h}) \right) \quad \text{for every } t.\)

The lattice shell sum \(S_{\eta} = \sup_v \sum_i e^{- \eta d (v, a_i)}\) is bounded uniformly in the torus size. Summing the second derivative gives \(B \leqslant CS_{\eta}\); summing the third gives the Hessian Lipschitz bound \(CS_{\eta}^2\). A real Hessian gap together with exponential locality supplies the required \(J\) by the previous section. These implications yield a uniform real neighborhood. A uniform complex neighborhood requires the corresponding complex estimates, which do not follow from a fixed finite number of real derivatives.

At fixed derivative order, higher derivatives of the background and of its Hessian can then be controlled by repeated implicit differentiation. If only polynomial dependence on an auxiliary volume is needed, this conversion can be postponed until after the uniform neighborhood and the first derivative have been obtained. It must not be used to choose the neighborhood itself. For a positive real Hessian \(H (x)\), the determinant contribution is differentiated by the standard identity \(D \log \det H [h] = \text{tr} (H^{- 1} DH [h])\).

5.10.1Supplying the local derivative estimates

We retain the distinction from Section 5.9.3: \(W_a\) is the smooth representative of the actual stationary tangent, whereas \(W_F\) is its representative in the forest coordinates. They have the same curvature, but the fine-difference bounds apply to \(W_a\).

The derivative hypotheses have a direct origin in the exponentially weighted path expansion. We spell it out because a bound for the action on each finite torus would not supply a uniform neighborhood.

Suppose the uncut classical action is a sum of local analytic terms indexed by rooted finite path collections. Let \(\ell\) be the total path length plus one. Require that each collection have connected support containing its root, and that

\(\displaystyle \sup_z \sum_{\gamma : \text{root} (\gamma) = z} |c_{\gamma} | e^{\sigma \ell (\gamma)} \leqslant P. \) (5.34)

A bounded number of paths per term is sufficient; products of their matrix entries, normalized traces and fixed invariant contractions are allowed. The constants \(\sigma, P\) and this number are independent of the torus. Choose local coordinate maps with uniformly bounded analytic extensions and fixed support radius at the chosen block ratio.

A complex coordinate of modulus at most \(r\) changes each matrix factor's norm by at most \(e^{Cr}\). Therefore a term of total length \(\ell\) has complex modulus at most \(Ce^{Cr \ell}\). Choose \(r\) so that \(Cr < \sigma / 4\). Differentiating \(q\) times selects at most \(C_q (1 + \ell)^q\) positions; the same estimates hold on the smaller complex ball. If the differentiated coordinates have prescribed anchors \(a_{i_1}, \ldots, a_{i_q}\), connectedness implies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{h = 1}^q d (a_{i_t}, a_{i_h}) \leqslant C_R q (1 + \ell) \quad \text{for every } t.\)

Here \(C_R\) allows for the finite support radius of the coordinate maps, fixed at this block ratio. A fraction of the exponential weight in (5.34) controls this distance, and a further fraction absorbs the polynomial \((1 + \ell)^q\). The remaining exponential weight bounds the sum over possible roots of a term visiting a fixed anchor: the number of roots within distance \(C_R (1 + \ell)\) grows only polynomially in \(\ell\). It follows, for every fixed \(N\), that

\(\displaystyle | \partial_{i_1} \cdots \partial_{i_q} f (w) | \leqslant C_N \exp \left( - \eta_N \sum_h d (a_{i_t}, a_{i_h}) \right), \qquad 2 \leqslant q \leqslant N,\)

on a common complex ball. Block penalties and local coordinate terms add finite-range contributions satisfying the same estimate. Smooth cutoffs are not being analytically continued: the action here is the uncut action whose germ at zero defines the background.

For the actual forest chart (5.21), the deterministic tree comparison and the Hessian identification have already been proved in (5.26)–(5.29). The chosen block average is analytic on a common fixed-patch ball, and all its redundant local labels agree there as functions of the full field. The derivative bounds above give exponential locality of that Hessian. Section 5.8 now supplies a supremum-norm bound for its inverse independent of volume; a sufficiently small complex perturbation preserves this bound. Proposition 5.3 therefore applies on a uniform neighborhood.

This argument proves the uniform stationary construction for uncut path actions satisfying (5.34), the actual incoming quadratic coefficient condition in (5.28). The actual block average and forest coordinates supply the local analytic-coordinate hypotheses and the deterministic comparison (5.26). It does not require a later matching theorem. If two local descriptions agree as functions on the same coordinate neighborhood, uniqueness makes their stationary graphs agree there. Equation (5.33) identifies the tangent with the forest representative of the actual corrected transport \(W_a\). The stationary chain rule and (5.31) give the coarse quadratic form as the Hessian of the reduced action. Equality of actions only on a previously selected graph would not suffice for this argument.

The constants are uniform in the period after the block ratio, smoothing order and path bound have been fixed. Uniformity in an arbitrary block ratio is a separate weighted-norm estimate; the Euclidean tree gap above is allowed to depend on \(L\).

Proposition 5.4. (Uniform actual stationary chart) Fix the group, \(j, L, b\), an exponential path bound (5.34), and the actual incoming quadratic metric with \(H_f \geqslant \varepsilon I > 0\). For the uncut action (5.27) there are radii \(r_V, r_{\psi} > 0\) and constants \(c, J, C_{\rho} > 0\), independent of the allowed period and block trees, such that its unique stationary graph \(\rho\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \| \rho (v)\|_{\infty} < r_{\psi} / 2, \qquad \|D \rho (v)\|_{\infty \to \infty} \leqslant C_{\rho} \quad (\|v\|_{\infty} < r_V) .\)

On the corresponding smaller real product chart the vertical Hessian is at least \(cI\) and its inverse has supremum operator norm at most \(J\). All local tag descriptions define the same action and stationary graph there. For every prescribed finite order \(N\), ordinary derivatives of \(f\) through order \(N\), of \(\rho\) through order \(N - 1\), and of the centered log determinant of its vertical Hessian through order \(N - 2\) are bounded by fixed polynomials in the total coordinate number \(n\). The radii and first-derivative bound do not acquire a factor depending on \(n\).

To check the last assertions, the third rooted derivative bound gives \(\|H (w) - H (0)\|_{2 \to 2} \leqslant C \|w\|_{\infty}\) by row and column sums. Reduce the common ball until this is at most half the gap in (5.29). The weighted inverse argument then applies uniformly throughout that real ball. Proposition 5.3 gives the graph and its volume-independent Lipschitz constant. Repeated implicit differentiation isolates \(HD^k \rho\) at order \(k\); every other term involves lower derivatives of \(\rho\) and derivatives of \(f\) of order at most \(k + 1\). Converting bounded coordinate entries to ordinary multilinear norms costs only a fixed power of \(n\) at fixed order. Induction therefore proves the asserted polynomial bounds. The identities \(DH^{- 1} = - H^{- 1} (DH) H^{- 1}\) and \(D \log \det H = \text{tr} (H^{- 1} DH)\) give the determinant assertion, with one additional factor \(n\) for the trace. These are standard implicit and Gaussian determinant differentiation rules; no polynomial-in-volume bound is used to select the radii. For an application requiring \(k + 4\) derivatives, take the path/coordinate derivative order at least \(k + 6\) so that the stated determinant margin is retained.

5.11Scope of the completed Gaussian construction

For each fixed smoothing order, the finite-history construction now provides uniformly analytic positive curvature metrics, the specified one- and two-form transports, and a positive three-form factor for the amended curvature defect. Its parameter choices precede the choice of the scale sequence. The nonlinear stationary graph is supplied for each incoming path action with the explicit local bound and actual quadratic coefficient condition just stated; its tree comparison and block average branch compatibility are now proved for the chosen block kernel.

For the nested selected schedule, Proposition 5.2 now also proves fixed-scale convergence of the actual quadratic rows at every smaller exponential width. For the actual nonlinear step, the chosen path representation and block average must be checked against (5.34) and the actual quadratic coefficient condition (5.28). The common minimizing branch and equality of its local descriptions have been checked above. These are precise interface conditions, not consequences of Gaussian algebra. These conditions will be imposed on the admissible path action and retained by the nonlinear step; a Gaussian identity alone would not justify dropping them.
