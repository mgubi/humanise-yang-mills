Chapter 10
A common trajectory and fixed-scale stability

The one-step transformation must be used with a single sequence of scales and a consistent choice of coefficient arrays. There are two separate problems. First one must tune each finite Wilson theory to the same coarse inverse coupling. Then one must compare theories with different ultraviolet cutoffs. Continuity of a finite step solves the first problem, but does not by itself solve the second.

We first construct the numerical schedule and the tuned finite histories from Theorem 9.2. We then describe the additional quantitative comparison needed for convergence of these histories. Its abstract consequences are proved here. The distinction between that comparison and ordinary continuity remains explicit; in particular we do not assert analytic dependence on the tuned endpoint. The elementary contraction arguments are applications of Banach's principle [Ban22]; we give the estimates specific to the oppositely directed inverse coupling and activity recursions.

10.1What is fixed before choosing the scales

A stage \(j \geqslant 1\) specifies finitely many derivative orders, support exponents, path widths and coefficient bounds. These are numerical bounds for the representations of Chapter 4, not a new collection of physical theories. Stages are nested: the conditions of stage \(j + 1\) include those already required at stage \(j\). At a fixed stage there are only finitely many permitted adjacent pairs. Choose constants for all these pairs simultaneously.

The order of choices is important. Fix the quadratic metric bounds, path widths and classical bounds first. Choose the odd block ratio large enough for the classical contraction (7.15), inherited-loop contraction (7.16), and the support reserves in the local integral. Only afterwards choose inverse coupling thresholds and minimum auxiliary periods. Enlarging an inverse coupling threshold does not change the block kernel or the coefficient construction. The Wilson initialization can be made smaller than any prescribed fixed fraction of its activity bounds by this enlargement, as follows from the estimates in Section 9.8.

Let \(J (m + 1)\) denote the stage of the step from scale \(m + 1\) to scale \(m\). Let \(C_j^{\text{drift}}\) be a stage bound for the error in the downward inverse coupling step:

\(\displaystyle | \beta_{m + 1} - \beta_m - \kappa_G \log L_m | \leqslant C^{\text{drift}}_{J (m + 1)} .\) (10.1)

Here \(\kappa_G = 11 C_{\mathfrak{g}} / (24 \pi^2) > 0\). The normalization of the field-independent term is included in \(C_j^{\text{drift}}\). Chapter 7 fixes this coefficient with the same normalization of the Lie algebra metric as the block integral. Its error constant is fixed before the block ratio. Choose nondecreasing odd integers \(\mathscr{L}_j \geqslant 3\) above every stage floor, and also require

\(\displaystyle \frac{C_j^{\text{drift}}}{\log \mathscr{L}_j} \leqslant \min \{\kappa_G / 4, j^{- 2} \} .\) (10.2)

Any further fixed logarithmic size bound \(K_j\) can similarly be required to satisfy \(K_j / \log \mathscr{L}_j \leqslant j^{- 2}\). Costs depending on the chosen \(\mathscr{L}_j\) are covered by later thresholds for the starting scales, not by redefining the constants used in (10.2).

Future applications may prescribe countably many finite numerical requests, provided their analytic estimates have already been established for a fixed stage. At stage \(j\) take the maximum of the first \(j\) such requests. This diagonal choice absorbs finitely many constants; it does not establish an estimate merely because it has been listed. In particular the comparison hypotheses below must be proved for the same coefficient and activity construction before they can be used.

One particular later request can already be made explicit. Fix the equation-adapted sections of Theorem 2.6 once and for all. For \(d \leqslant j\), let \(a_{dj}\) be their derivative constants at the orders and spatial weights of stage \(j\), and let \(q_d\) bound their union stencil with \(P_d^{\text{loc}}\). These constants depend only on fixed cover paths, finite polynomials and the stage orders; Proposition 2.5 makes them independent of the block ratio, period and cutoff. Fix the stage column bound \(C_j\), support exponent \(\sigma_j\), and finite symmetry norm \(\mathcal{O}_d\) before choosing the ratio. With

\(\displaystyle Q_{dj} = 2 + e^{81 \sigma_j} (a_{dj} + C_j + 2 q_d), \qquad \delta_d = (2\mathcal{O}_d^2)^{- 1},\)

include the additional floor

\(\displaystyle \mathscr{L}_j^{1 / 8} \geqslant \max_{d \leqslant j} 2 (1 + C_j) Q_{dj} \delta_d^{- 1} .\) (10.3)

For the bounded coarse polynomial trials also include \(\mathscr{L}_j^{1 / 8} \geqslant 8 (1 + C_j) Q^{\text{coarse}}_{dj}\) for \(d \leqslant j\), where \(Q^{\text{coarse}}_{dj}\) is their ordinary chart bound, fixed before the ratio. On that chart the relaxed small-field smooth cutoff is one. Its complementary global value is controlled by the same later square-root large-field bound condition described below. Here \(C_j\) is the prescribed bound, not a norm recomputed after choosing \(\mathscr{L}_j\). No terminal radius enters this floor: the raw stencil is fixed; the terminal requirement that its cover be at most \(81\) is imposed later by increasing the inverse coupling threshold and hence the available localization radius. The remaining terminal value norms contain inverse powers of the small-field threshold. The delays require their product with the square root of the terminal large-field bound to be at most one, as well as the fixed-stencil support and occupancy conditions. They are absorbed by choosing sufficiently large inverse-coupling thresholds, not included in \(Q_{dj}\). Those delays also impose \(\hat{\beta}_N \geqslant a_{d, J (N)}\) and all terminal reserve inequalities for \(d \leqslant J (N)\). Since only finitely many degrees are tested at a stage, this is a noncircular addition to the schedule. Its use is the near-identity comparison in Section 11.11.

10.2A slowly increasing stage schedule

We give an explicit construction, including the initial scales. Let \(b_j, c_j\) be nonnegative integers specifying respectively the minimum starting scale and a requested number of consecutive scales at stage \(j\). We call the interval on which the stage is constant a plateau. If \(\jmath_j\) is the largest smoothing order used in stage \(j\) or a permitted adjacent pair, include \(b_j \log 3 \geqslant 2 \log (\jmath_j + 7)\) for \(j > 1\). This ensures that the full dependence radius of a probability-kernel pullback shrinks in physical units, including its frame-choice variables. Starting with \(A_1 = 0\), define

\(\displaystyle k_j = c_j + 2 A_j + 1, \qquad A_{j + 1} = A_j + b_{j + 1} + 2 k_j + 1, \qquad J (m) = j \quad (A_j \leqslant m < A_{j + 1}) .\)

Then \(J\) is nondecreasing, tends to infinity, and increases by at most one in a single step. If \(J (m + 1) = j > 1\), then \(m \geqslant b_j\). Moreover the interval \([k_j - \lfloor k_j / 2 \rfloor, k_j + \lfloor k_j / 2 \rfloor]\) lies in the \(j\)th plateau: its lower endpoint is at least \(A_j\), and its upper endpoint is less than \(A_{j + 1}\).

Set

\(\displaystyle L_m = \mathscr{L}_{J (m + 1)}, \qquad \tau_0 = 0, \quad \tau_{m + 1} = \tau_m + \log L_m, \qquad a_m = e^{- \tau_m} .\) (10.4)

Thus \(a_m\) is the lattice spacing, and \(a_m^{- 1}\) is an integer. Monotonicity gives

\(\displaystyle m \log 3 \leqslant \tau_m \leqslant m \log \mathscr{L}_{J (m)} .\)

Choose a coarse target \(\bar{\beta} > 0\) and reference inverse couplings

\(\displaystyle \hat{\beta}_m = \bar{\beta} + \kappa_G \tau_m .\) (10.5)

The first-stage threshold is satisfied by \(\bar{\beta}\). Every subsequent stage threshold \(d_j\) is satisfied by increasing its starting scale until \(\hat{\beta}_m \geqslant d_j\). Let \(V_j (s)\) bound the required lattice side at stage \(j\) when the logarithm of the inverse mesh is \(s\). For a fixed polynomial \(V_j\) we may simultaneously require

\(\displaystyle \log \mathscr{L}_j \leqslant \tau_m / j, \qquad V_j (\tau_m) \leqslant e^{\tau_m}, \quad J (m + 1) = j > 1.\) (10.6)

Also require, for the original support radius and its per-site normalization cost, \(B_{m + 1}^2 \leqslant 2 B_m^2\) and \((L_m B_m^2)^4 q_{m + 1}^{\text{site}} / \tau_{m + 1} \leqslant 1 / j\) at stage \(j\). Here \(q_{m + 1}^{\text{site}}\) is bounded by \(C_j (1 + \log \hat{\beta}_{m + 1})\) using the Haar-ball normalization argument. At a fixed stage the numerator is a fixed polynomial in \(\log (2 + \tau_m)\), so this is a finite choice of a sufficiently large starting scale. It will keep replication costs subpower at a nearby sparse stopping scale. For the later grouping of finitely many local sources, also require \((\sigma_{m + 1} + 1) h_m / \tau_m \leqslant 1 / j\), where \(h_m = 16 [1 + L_m (B_m + B_{m + 1} + 1)^2]^4\) and \(\sigma_{m + 1}\) is the prescribed support exponent. This is again a fixed polynomial in \(\log (2 + \tau_m)\) at each stage. It ensures that moving a finite number of source anchors has only a subpower cost. The bound on \(V_j\) in (10.6) also holds eventually for any fixed subexponential \(V_j\). Choose \(b_j\) so these inequalities hold whenever \(\tau_m \geqslant b_j \log 3\). They then hold at every step of stage \(j\), not just at its first occurrence.

For comparison estimates requiring a window \(w_j (s) = o (s)\) around one level of stage \(j\), increase \(c_j\) so that \(w_j (s) \leqslant \lfloor k / 2 \rfloor\) whenever \(k \geqslant c_j\) and \(k \log 3 \leqslant s \leqslant k \log \mathscr{L}_j\). At \(k = k_j\) the whole requested window stays on the same plateau. This is useful when a fixed finite calculation must be isolated from changes in derivative order or support exponent.

Only finitely many steps belong to the first plateau. Choose an integer \(M\) large enough that the lattice side \(R_p / a_m\) meets the required lower bound at every step of this plateau for all \(p \geqslant 0\), where

\(\displaystyle R_p = 4 (M + p + 1), \qquad p \geqslant 0.\) (10.7)

On later plateaux (10.6) ensures the required lattice side. These periods also contain arbitrarily late multiples of every positive integer: for a given integer \(c\), take \(p = c (M + z + 1) - (M + 1)\) with \(z\) arbitrarily large. Finally

\(\displaystyle \frac{\log L_m}{\tau_m} \longrightarrow 0.\)

This follows from (10.6) outside the first plateau. No limit involving \(\tau_0^{- 1}\) is intended.

10.3Tuning a finite history to the coarse endpoint

The admissible inverse coupling band at level \(m\) is \([\hat{\beta}_m / 2, 2 \hat{\beta}_m]\). Define inner barriers

\(\displaystyle \ell_m = \bar{\beta} + \tfrac{1}{2} \kappa_G \tau_m, \qquad u_m = \bar{\beta} + 2 \kappa_G \tau_m .\)

Both lie in the band. If a step starts at \(\ell_{m + 1}\), its output is at most \(\ell_m - \kappa_G \log L_m / 4\) by (10.1)–(10.2). Starting at \(u_{m + 1}\) gives an output at least \(u_m + 3 \kappa_G \log L_m / 4\).

We use only the intermediate value theorem. If a continuous function \(f\) on \([x, y]\) satisfies \(f (x) \leqslant d < u \leqslant f (y)\), take its first point \(w\) with value \(u\) and its last point \(z \leqslant w\) with value \(d\). Then \(f ([z, w]) \subset [d, u]\). An excursion below \(d\) would force a later crossing of \(d\); an excursion above \(u\) an earlier crossing of \(u\). When \(d = u\), choose a single point of that level set. In particular no monotonicity of the output inverse coupling is required.

Theorem 10.1. (Tuned finite histories) Fix the common numerical choices above and the compatible finite quadratic and path constructions of Chapters 5 and 7. For every cutoff \(N\) and period \(R_p\) there is a finite history starting at a Wilson density and ending with \(\beta_{N, p, 0} = \bar{\beta}\). It has the admissible activity bounds and exact block identities at every level \(0 \leqslant m < N\). Its inverse couplings stay between \(\ell_m\) and \(u_m\).

Proof. At level \(N\) use the Wilson curve with bare inverse coupling in \([\ell_N, u_N]\). Apply the continuous step of Theorem 9.2. The barrier inequalities and the interval argument give a compact subinterval on which the output stays between \(\ell_{N - 1}\) and \(u_{N - 1}\) and attains these endpoints. Restrict the entire previously constructed history to that interval. Restriction compatibility preserves the labels, activity bounds and exact identities. Repeat downwards. At level zero the barriers coincide at \(\bar{\beta}\). For \(N = 0\) use the Wilson family directly. Every input to an applied step lies in its band; no continuation through a forbidden inverse coupling interval has been used.\(\Box\)

Choose one such history for each \(N, p\), retaining its path arrays, forests, local integral choices and output representations. The choice need not be unique. The paths fixed before the period are kept identical when the period changes. The exact identity is

\(\displaystyle \text{dens} (\rho_{N, p, m}) =\mathcal{B}^{(L_m)}_{\beta_{N, p, m + 1}} \text{dens} (\rho_{N, p, m + 1}) .\)

It is an identity of the actual densities, including the additive scalar normalization. That scalar is not asserted to converge.

The same construction reaches any endpoint \(|b - \bar{\beta} | \leqslant h\), where \(h = \min \{ \bar{\beta} / 2, \kappa_G \log L_0 / 4\}\). Descend to level one and use the last barrier crossing to reach \(b\). This proves existence in a common endpoint interval, but neither uniqueness of the shooting parameter nor continuity of its selection.

10.4Uniform logarithmic running

Telescoping from the fixed endpoint gives

\(\displaystyle | \beta_{N, p, m} - \hat{\beta}_m | \leqslant \sum_{i < m} C^{\text{drift}}_{J (i + 1)}, \qquad N \geqslant m.\) (10.8)

For each \(\epsilon > 0\), choose \(r\) so that \(J (i + 1)^{- 2} \leqslant \epsilon / 2\) for \(i \geqslant r\). The right side is at most

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i < r} C^{\text{drift}}_{J (i + 1)} + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2} (\tau_m - \tau_r) .\)

The finite initial sum is eventually at most \(\epsilon \tau_m / 2\). Consequently

\(\displaystyle \beta_{N, p, m} = \bar{\beta} + \kappa_G \tau_m + o (\tau_m)\) (10.9)

uniformly in \(N \geqslant m\) and \(p\). In particular the bare Wilson inverse coupling obeys \(\beta_{N, p, N} / \log (a_N^{- 1}) \to \kappa_G\) uniformly in \(p\). This conclusion does not use convergence of histories at a fixed scale. A bounded stage error is not summable by itself; the small ratio in (10.2) is essential.

By using a smaller fixed constant than \(\kappa_G / 4\) in that floor, the ratios \(\beta_{N, p, m} / \hat{\beta}_m\) can all be kept in an arbitrarily prescribed fixed neighborhood of one. We henceforth choose it small enough that

\(\displaystyle 17 / 20 \leqslant \sqrt{\beta_{N, p, m} / \hat{\beta}_m} \leqslant 23 / 20.\)

For example an error at most \(\kappa_G \log L_m / 10\) at each step implies a relative error at most \(1 / 10\) after summation. This also covers the initial indices, including \(m = 0\).

10.5The coefficient comparison before the activity comparison

A representation by transported curvature terms is not unique. For comparisons we fix the linear curl-division and path-placement operations in Chapter 7 once on the common coefficient dictionaries. An alternative is the orthogonal representative modulo the kernel of all periodic Taylor coefficients; then the orthogonal complement must be taken inside the admissible subspace, so that its invariance and moment constraints are preserved. Equality of the action on one torus is insufficient to identify its coefficient array.

At a fixed stage enlarge each incoming classical or loop coefficient bound by a factor \(64\), one family at a time, and choose the ratio floor for that enlarged bound. The bounds in (7.15) and (7.16) then retain ordinary output bounds. This enlargement does not concern the positive quadratic metric, which is kept fixed. The fixed-order stationary calculation is valid after reducing its local analytic neighborhood if necessary.

With quadratic data fixed, the outgoing cubic coefficient is affine in the incoming cubic coefficient. With the cubic coefficient also fixed, the same holds for the quartic coefficient. Finally the quadratic loop coefficient is affine in the incoming loop. The stationary formula (7.14) proves the first two assertions: its exchange term is fixed in the quartic comparison. The inherited Hessian and determinant formula prove the third. Fixed linear coefficient functionals preserve these identities.

Normalize each coefficient norm by its stage bound. If an affine map \(T\) sends the admissible ball of radius \(64\) into the output ball of radius one, then for \(\|H\|, \|K\| \leqslant 1\), writing \(\delta = \|H - K\| > 0\), the admissible extrapolation \(K_{\ast} = K + 32 (H - K) / \delta\) has norm at most \(33\) and gives

\(\displaystyle \|T (H) - T (K)\| \leqslant \delta / 16.\) (10.10)

The extrapolation preserves all linear symmetry and moment conditions. This is an affine estimate, not an assertion that the full nonlinear RG transformation is affine.

Proposition 5.2 gives convergence of the actual quadratic rows and free transports at every fixed level, with a strictly stronger exponential width than the required width. Choose the direct row coefficient strictly below its uniform positive metric gap, as required there. Their fixed-window inverses converge by the resolvent identity and the common inverse bound. In each successive coefficient comparison the change in these preceding data contributes an error \(e_{N, N', i} \to 0\) at fixed \(i\). The current coefficient discrepancy therefore satisfies

\(\displaystyle \delta_i \leqslant \tfrac{1}{4} \delta_{i + 1} + e_{N, N', i} .\)

For \(k > m\) and \(N, N' > k\), iteration gives

\(\displaystyle \delta_m \leqslant 2 (1 / 4)^{k - m} + \sum_{i = m}^{k - 1} (1 / 4)^{i - m} e_{N, N', i} .\) (10.11)

First send \(N, N' \to \infty\) at fixed \(k\), then \(k \to \infty\). This proves the Cauchy property in succession for cubic, quartic and loop coefficients, for the actual nested quadratic history and the fixed common coefficient functionals.

There is a useful precise version of the width reserve. If coefficients \(c_N (\gamma)\) on a common dictionary converge coordinatewise and \(\sum_{\gamma} |c_N (\gamma) | e^{w' \ell (\gamma)} \leqslant C\) with \(w' > w\), then their tails at width \(w\) and length greater than \(r\) are bounded by \(Ce^{- (w' - w) r}\). If only finitely many dictionary entries have bounded length, finite-coordinate convergence and this tail bound prove convergence at width \(w\). For a Hilbert encoding instead, one must verify summability of the encoding weights on that dictionary; a bound on individual coefficients alone does not bound the number of paths of a given length.

10.6The stronger local comparison required for cutoff limits

Fix a physical period \(R_p\). At level \(m\) let \(\mathcal{X}_{m, p}\) be the completion of the actual activity arrays in their normalized weighted derivative and value norm. Zero seminorm arrays are identified. Equality of their evaluated partition functions is not this equivalence relation. Dictionaries, event indicators, supports and prescribed-site rules are fixed at this level independently of \(N\). We pad absent labels by zero.

To state the comparison, set

\(\displaystyle \chi_m = \hat{\beta}_m^{- 1 / 32}, \qquad \mathfrak{b}_m = \hat{\beta}_m^{31 / 32}, \qquad \lambda_m = \chi_m^{- 1} \sqrt{\beta_m / \hat{\beta}_m}, \qquad b_m (\lambda) = \hat{\beta}_m (\chi_m \lambda)^2 .\)

The symbol \(b_m (\lambda)\) in this section is the inverse coupling coordinate map; it is unrelated to the block penalty \(b\). On the narrow band \(17 / 20 \leqslant \chi_m \lambda \leqslant 23 / 20\) we have

\(\displaystyle \mathfrak{b}_m | \lambda - \lambda' | \leqslant |b_m (\lambda) - b_m (\lambda') | .\) (10.12)

The extra factor in the right side is \(\chi_m (\lambda + \lambda') \geqslant 17 / 10\).

Write the actual step maps at cutoff \(N\) as

\(\displaystyle x_m = F_{m, N} (\lambda_{m + 1}, x_{m + 1}), \qquad q_m = Q_{m, N} (\lambda_{m + 1}, x_{m + 1}), \quad \beta_m - \beta_{m + 1} - \alpha_{N, m} = q_m .\)

Here \(x_m\) is the activity array and \(q_m\) is the residual scalar inverse coupling correction. It is not the vacuum normalization. The path coefficient \(\alpha_{N, m}\) is the one already used in the exact step.

The following strengthens the continuous finite-step statement of Theorem 9.2. Its proof uses the additional numerical reserves already allowed in the scale choices.

Proposition 10.2. (Quantitative comparison on the common data) The actual maps are defined for every prescribed narrow-band inverse coupling sequence and every allowed activity array of norm at most \(1 / 8\), including zero terminal arrays. They preserve that ball, satisfy \(|Q_{m, N} | \leqslant s_m / 2\), and, on the same cutoff, obey

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \|F_{m, N} (z) - F_{m, N} (z')\| & \leqslant \tfrac{1}{16} \|z - z' \|, \hspace{3cm} \text{(10.13)} \\ |Q_{m, N} (z) - Q_{m, N} (z') | & \leqslant 4 s_m \|z - z' \|, \end{array}\)

where \(\|z - z' \| = \max \{| \lambda - \lambda' |, \|x - x' \|\}\). The strengthened Wilson terminal arrays have norm at most \(1 / 8\). The numerical choices satisfy \(\sum_{i < m} s_i \leqslant \mathfrak{b}_m / 128\). The paths and \(\alpha_{N, m}\) converge at each fixed scale with the strict width reserve above. Finally, for two sequences of admissible inputs at different cutoffs, common limits of their scalar coordinates and Cauchy activity arrays with difference tending to zero imply output differences tending to zero, in both the activity norm and the scalar correction. This last assertion applies also to every zero-terminal-array history with prescribed inverse couplings.

The last condition compares changing maps \(F_{m, N}\), not just changing inputs to one fixed map. Uniform contractions by themselves do not imply it. For example \(F_N (x) = (- 1)^N / 10\) has Lipschitz constant zero and no cutoff limit. Nor does smallness of the output imply the small Lipschitz constant in (10.13).

Proof of Proposition 10.2. We derive the contraction from finite parameter evaluations, bound those evaluations using the local expansion, sum the errors over scales, and finally prove continuity as the cutoff is removed.

    Step 1.

    Contraction from finite parameter evaluations. One sufficient quantitative estimate uses only real parameter lines. For a line \(z + tv\) of admissible inputs, \(|t| \leqslant 1\), suppose every finite choice of weights \(a_i\) and points \(t_i\) satisfies

    \(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \left| \sum_i a_i F (z + t_i v) \right| & \leqslant d \sup_{n \geqslant 0} \left| \sum_i a_i (t_i / r)^n \right|, \hspace{3cm} \text{(10.14)} \\ \left| \sum_i a_i Q (z + t_i v) \right| & \leqslant f_m \sup_{n \geqslant 0} \left| \sum_i a_i (t_i / (2 r))^n \right|, \qquad r \geqslant 2. \end{array}\)

    These are estimates of the actual arrays in their derivative norm, not just of the value of a partition function. They imply an absolutely convergent one-variable expansion. Indeed the map \(((t / r)^n)_{n \geqslant 0} \mapsto F (z + tv)\) extends linearly and boundedly from its span in \(c_0\) to its closure. That closure contains every coordinate vector: subtract the first \(n\) coordinates, divide by \((t / r)^n\), and let \(t \downarrow 0\). Applying the bounded map to the geometric expansion gives coefficients of norm at most \(d\). Consequently

    \(\displaystyle \|F (z + tv) - F (z)\| \leqslant d |t| / (r - |t|) .\)

    The identical argument applies to \(Q\). This short bounded-operator argument records the precise information in the parameter evaluations; it is not an inference of analyticity from continuity.

    For two inputs at distance \(0 < h \leqslant 1 / 4\), use the line with direction \((z' - z) / (4 h)\) and evaluate at \(t = 4 h\). Activity norm along this line is at most \(1 / 8 + 1 / 4 < 1\). An inverse coupling reserve from the narrow band to \(33 / 40 \leqslant \chi_m \lambda \leqslant 47 / 40\) makes the whole line admissible when \(\chi_m \leqslant 1 / 128\). The resulting bounds are at most \(4 dh\) and \(4 f_m h\). If \(h > 1 / 4\), use the separate output bounds; if the distance is zero, use the same estimate with a vanishing seminorm direction and let the parameter tend to zero. Thus \(d \leqslant 1 / 256\) and \(s_m = 2 f_m\) imply (10.13) with room to spare. The finite-evaluation inequalities require the analytic estimates on the local expansion established in the next step.

    Step 2.

    Parameter estimates from the local expansion.

    Here is the parameter estimate for the actual local factors. It uses the protected change of variables and real Gaussian majorants of Chapter 6, retaining their fixed derivative order and support reserves. The incoming array is affine along the parameter line. The path, event indicators, held fine reads and coefficient projection are fixed. In particular the finite low-order collection in Chapter 9 is chosen on the compact inverse coupling band independently of incoming activity arrays. The estimates below therefore compare the same output construction on different lines. For the estimates one uses the explicit local expansion on the larger affine activity domain. Its polynomial products and convergent logarithmic series define the array and local scalar outputs there even when an extrapolated array has not been identified with a positive density. Positivity is required for actual histories, not for this algebraic extension of their local comparison formulas.

    Put \(y = \hat{\beta}_{m + 1}\), \(R = y^{1 / 256}\) and \(\delta = y^{- 1 / 128}\). The inverse coupling is a square of an affine function of the line parameter. The relative change on \(|t| \leqslant 6 R\) is \(O (Ry^{- 1 / 32}) = O (y^{- 7 / 256}) = o (\delta)\). Choose a slightly smaller reference inverse coupling \(\beta_0\) so that

    \(\displaystyle \beta (t) = \beta_0 \ell (t)^2, \quad \ell (t) = a + ct, \qquad 1 \leqslant a - 6 R |c| \leqslant a + 6 R |c| \leqslant 1 + \delta .\)

    All these real inverse couplings remain in the reserved band. Compensation keeps each fine field read fixed: the Gaussian coordinate is rescaled by \(\ell (t)\) while its fluctuation coefficient is divided by \(\ell (t)\). The cutoff-failure indicators and measurable incoming values are consequently not differentiated in this parameter.

    For a parameter series use the norm \(|f|_R = \sum_{n \geqslant 0} |f_n | R^n\). Its product estimate is the ordinary Cauchy-product inequality. A real local energy factor, including its coordinate prefactor, has the form

    \(\displaystyle J (t, U) = A (U) \ell (t)^n \exp ((1 - \ell (t)^2) \Gamma (U)) .\)

    The energy \(\Gamma\) can have either sign. It cannot be replaced by its absolute value before estimating the exponential. For \(q = \Gamma (U)\) the exponential series norm is at most \(e^{H (q)}\), where

    \(\displaystyle H (q) = (1 - a^2) q + 2 aR |cq| + R^2 c^2 |q| .\)

    Choose the sign of \(\xi\) so that \(qc \xi \leqslant 0\) and take \(3 \leqslant | \xi | \leqslant 4\). Since \(a \geqslant 1 + 6 R |c|\), direct expansion gives \(H (q) \leqslant (1 - \ell (R \xi)^2) q\). After \(j\) coarse-field derivatives, factoring out \(\ell (t)^n e^{(1 - \ell (t)^2) q}\) leaves a polynomial of degree at most \(2 j\). Interpolate that polynomial at \(2 j + 1\) distinct such real nodes. Their scaled Lagrange polynomials have a fixed coefficient norm. Thus real jet bounds \(Cd^i\) at those nodes imply

    \(\displaystyle |D^i J (\cdot, U) |_R \leqslant K_j (1 + \delta)^n Cd^i, \qquad i \leqslant j.\) (10.15)

    The node sign is chosen only for a pointwise bound and is never differentiated. The same argument applied to the Gaussian density ratio in \(D\) local real coordinates yields

    \(\displaystyle | \ell (t)^D e^{(1 - \ell (t)^2) Q (z)} |_R \leqslant (1 + \delta)^D, \qquad Q (z) \geqslant 0.\)

    Only coordinates read by the local term enter \(D\).

    Small logarithmic factors require a different estimate. Their parameter dependence is polynomial of degree at most two; real values and derivatives of their exponential minus one have bound \(vd^i\) at \(- 1, 0, 1\). For \(v \leqslant 1 / 2\), taking the real logarithm at these three nodes preserves a bound \(C_j vd^i\). Polynomial interpolation costs \(C_j R^2\), and exponentiating in the coefficient algebra gives a bound \(C_j R^2 vd^i\) when \(C_j R^2 v \leqslant 1\). Affine activities constrained to cover prescribed large-field sites have the same polynomial interpolation bound without taking a logarithm. These steps require no analyticity of the incoming activity as a function of the gauge field beyond the already imposed finite derivative order.

    For the primitive bound \(v = y^{- 7 / 16 + o (1)}\), this radius cost changes its exponent to \(- 7 / 16 + 2 / 256 = - 55 / 128 < - 2 / 5\). The local factor estimate (10.15) retains its negative large-field energy: its additional \((1 + \delta)^n\) is absorbed into the fixed local factor cost. A local read set in support \(S\) has at most \(C_L (1 + B)^4 C_B (S)\) coordinates. Since \(B\) is polynomial in \(\log y\), its Gaussian prefactor satisfies, for any fixed \(\eta > 0\) and sufficiently large \(y\),

    \(\displaystyle (1 + \delta)^{C_L (1 + B)^4 C_B (S)} \leqslant e^{\eta C_B (S)} .\)

    This is absorbed by a support-exponent reserve, independently of the volume. The joint cutoff-failure probability and Gaussian score moments are those already used for the common majorant; Cauchy–Schwarz retains their cutoff-failure gain without assuming independent cutoff failures.

    We can therefore run the connected expansions of Chapter 8 in this coefficient norm. A derivative of order at most \(j\) of a product with \(k\) factors costs at most \(k^j \leqslant 2^{jk}\), absorbed by inflating each vertex bound by \(2^j\). Polynomial support moments are absorbed by another fixed support reserve. The first connected expansion has bound \(y^{- 2 / 5 + o (1)}\); the logarithmic expansion and spatial derivative weights consequently have bound \(y^{- 3 / 8}\) at radius \(R / 2\), after increasing the threshold for the starting scale. The low-order projection uses finitely many auxiliary volumes of side \(O (B)\) and supports coverable by a fixed number of \(B\)-balls. Its polynomial factors have exponent zero. Hence the actual raw projected regular family has finite-evaluation bound

    \(\displaystyle \mathcal{N} \left( \sum_i a_i H (t_i) \right) \leqslant y^{- 1 / 4} \sup_{n \geqslant 0} \left| \sum_i a_i (2 t_i / R)^n \right| .\) (10.16)

    Here \(\mathcal{N}\) is the full unnormalized regular derivative bound. The margin between \(- 3 / 8\) and \(- 1 / 4\) absorbs the projection factors. A cover number merely growing subpolynomially would not suffice inside an exponential weight; its uniform bound is used here.

    For completeness we explain the sharpening which makes this useful. At each real point, the stronger one-step remainder estimate gives

    \(\displaystyle \mathcal{N} (H (t)) \leqslant 3 \varepsilon C_{\text{st}} x^{- 11 / 10} + O (x^{- 23 / 20 + o (1)}), \qquad x = \hat{\beta}_m,\)

    where the fixed response bound \(\varepsilon\) can be made as small as desired before choosing the inverse coupling threshold. This is the small remainder estimate of Chapter 9 applied to this same collection. Let a finite parameter evaluation have moment norm \(M_q\) at a fixed radius \(q\). Interpolation at \(k + 1\) fixed real nodes separates its moments through degree \(k\) from the remainder. The interpolating evaluation functional has total variation at most \(U_{k, q} M_q\); the remaining evaluation functional has moment norm at radius \(v \geqslant q\) at most \((1 + U_{k, q}) (q / v)^{k + 1} M_q\). Applying the pointwise bound to the first part and (10.16) to the second yields

    \(\displaystyle M_q (U_{k, q} \sup_t \mathcal{N}(H (t)) + (1 + U_{k, q}) (2 q / R)^{k + 1} y^{- 1 / 4}) .\)

    Take \(k = 300\). The last power is \(y^{- 365 / 256}\), strictly smaller than \(y^{- 23 / 20}\). First choose \(\varepsilon\) to absorb the fixed interpolation constant, then increase the inverse coupling threshold. The result is an arbitrarily small multiple of \(x^{- 11 / 10} M_q\). The scalar projection costs the squared inverse field threshold; thus its bound has the form

    \(\displaystyle f_m = C \varepsilon x^{- 11 / 10} / t (x)^2, \qquad t (x) = x^{- 1 / 2} e^{(\log \log x)^2} .\)

    The reset is linear on finite parameter evaluations, and its fixed path contribution costs \(O (| \alpha |t (x)^3) = o (x^{- 11 / 10})\).

    Arrays constrained to cover prescribed large-field sites retain one compulsory large-field factor throughout fusion. Work with their coefficientwise majorants, not separately chosen value bounds. A raw hard bound \(a_h \leqslant Ce^{- cP (y)^2}\), where \(P (y) = e^{(\log \log y)^2}\), is negligible compared with any fixed fraction \(\phi\) of \(e^{- P (x)^{1 / 5}}\). The soft correction has series bound \(a_s \leqslant y^{- 1 / 8}\). Put \(p = y^{1 / 16}\) and \(\zeta = - \log (\phi p) \geqslant 0\). Then \(I = e^{\zeta} a_h + 2 a_s \leqslant (8 p)^{- 1} + 2 a_s\) eventually, the support ball count times \(I\) tends to zero, and

    \(\displaystyle e^{- \zeta} (e^I - 1) \leqslant \phi (1 / 4 + 4 pa_s) \leqslant \phi .\)

    This is exactly the connected fusion estimate with the hard factor retained once. Radius reduction by a fixed factor converts the finite-evaluation coefficient bounds into one summable majorant at every support. Eligibility indicators are parameter independent and preserve it. The result proves both inequalities (10.14), with arbitrarily small fixed \(d\), for the protected local construction. The inverse coupling bound above gives \(s_m = 2 f_m \leqslant C_j x^{- 1 / 10}\) needed for cumulative control.

    This argument uses the real-node bounds for the same protected local sectors on the reserved inverse coupling band. Those bounds follow by applying the real estimates of Chapter 6 with their constants fixed on that band; the rescaling keeps all fine reads and event indicators fixed. It adds a parameter estimate to that construction, not a differentiation of measurable cutoff indicators.

    Step 3.

    Summation over scales.

    The cumulative scalar bound is compatible with the schedule. Suppose at a fixed stage its bound has the proved form \(s (x) \leqslant C_j x^{- 1 / 10}\), where \(x\) is the reference inverse coupling. For the stage increment \(a = \kappa_G \log \mathscr{L}_j > 0\) and \(x \geqslant a\),

    \(\displaystyle (x + a)^{31 / 32} - x^{31 / 32} \geqslant \tfrac{31}{32} 2^{- 1 / 32} ax^{- 1 / 32} .\)

    Since \(x^{- 1 / 10} = o (x^{- 1 / 32})\), a threshold for the starting scale ensures \(s (x) \leqslant ((x + a)^{31 / 32} - x^{31 / 32}) / 128\). Summation gives the required bound by telescoping, including all initial scales once \(\bar{\beta}\) exceeds the first threshold. The decay estimate for \(s (x)\) is required before this numerical argument; an arbitrary stage constant would not suffice.

    Step 4.

    Continuity for a common input.

    Use now the quadratic and path coefficient limits with the strict width reserve established in Section 10.5. We verify the cutoff-comparison part of Proposition 10.2 on the actual allowed inputs. All assertions in this paragraph are at one fixed scale and period; constants need not be uniform in the scale.

    For a finite collection of elementary labels and bounded path lengths, the fine-coordinate dimension is finite. Write each integral in the same physical coordinates at the two cutoffs. Convergence of the path coefficients with a width reserve gives convergence of its finitely many required derivatives, uniformly on the smaller local chart. The inverse identity \(H_N^{- 1} - H^{- 1} = H_N^{- 1} (H - H_N) H^{- 1}\), the common positive Hessian bound and stationary uniqueness give convergence of means, precisions and stationary jets. Differentiated Gaussian densities are their ordinary polynomial scores. On a sufficiently late tail their precisions have a common smaller positive lower bound, so a fixed Gaussian exponential times a polynomial dominates them. The protected change of variables leaves measurable fine reads fixed. Thus the same input activity discrepancy is bounded in its prescribed norm; no derivative of a measurable indicator is introduced.

    Use the physical fine coordinates also for the event indicators. At a fixed level their threshold is determined by the fixed reference inverse coupling, and the cutoff-failure catalogue, support dictionary and clipping rule are independent of the terminal index. Thus the actual event indicator is the same measurable function in both integrals. Although its expression in a centered Gaussian variable would move with the mean or precision, no such moving expression is compared here. The local change of density includes its Jacobian and leaves that physical event indicator fixed. The coarse small-field event indicators are likewise identical. This avoids any uniform assertion about the measure of a moving threshold surface.

    To obtain the suprema in the output derivative bound, choose the protected Gaussian and chart estimates through one order beyond the outgoing order. This is a numerical derivative reserve for the integration kernels, and does not require one additional derivative of an incoming activity. Indeed in each differentiated integral the protected coordinate change holds its entire fine activity read fixed. An additional coarse increment differentiates the smooth density, chart and Jacobian scores, while the old measurable factors remain unchanged. The bounds of Chapter 6 apply at every fixed requested score order; increase that order before selecting the stage constants. Direct path terms and the finite local projection are smooth and satisfy the same extra order by their exponential coefficient bounds. Thus this reserve is available also on a plateau with equal incoming and outgoing activity derivative orders. On each common small-field chart it gives equicontinuity of the finite collection of output derivatives. A finite net on the compact coarse configuration domain and dominated convergence then give uniform convergence there. The estimates extend to its closure from the protected neighborhood; multiplication by the same small-field event indicator preserves the resulting supremum bound.

    Next remove the finite-label restriction. Long paths have uniformly small tails by the strict exponential width reserve. Large connected supports and high cluster multiplicities have uniformly small tails by the support and attachment reserves of the polymer estimates. Remaining multiplicities inherited from the input arrays are treated by the Cauchy tail argument at the end of this chapter: a fixed late array has a summable value tail, and the activity-norm discrepancy bounds the tail of every later array. In a protected derivative the input factors are kept at fixed fine reads, so these are value tails; the differentiations fall on the controlled Gaussian and chart factors. If an input derivative is explicitly present, its difference is already controlled by the corresponding component of the input norm. First truncate with these uniform errors, then compare the finite integrals. This proves convergence in the output norm.

    The projected scalar is a finite linear functional of the same converging local derivatives and therefore also converges. Local subtraction preserves this conclusion by linearity; fusion preserves it by its convergent product expansion with a strict attachment margin. The finite auxiliary projection uses the same fields and periods at both cutoffs. Thus this comparison concerns the actual returned arrays and \(Q_{m, N}\), not some other representation of their densities.

    The same proof applies when the terminal activity is zero and the inverse couplings below it are prescribed in advance. At the initial auxiliary level the density is the positive bare path exponential, and all activity bounds are zero. After each step its activity array has the small bound proved above. Replacing the returned inverse coupling by the next prescribed inverse coupling multiplies the represented density by a strictly positive path exponential; its activity gas is unchanged. Therefore positivity is retained, and the next step is an allowed input to Theorem 9.2. All stationary, support and cutoff conditions depend on the common path and reference band, which have not changed. In particular this induction supplies precisely the histories with prescribed inverse couplings used in the convergence proof; it does not assume that every abstract element of a completed norm ball represents a positive density.

    The quadratic limit of Proposition 5.2, the affine coefficient comparison and the preceding local-integral argument give the common-input continuity required here, including all zero-array histories with prescribed inverse couplings. Together with the finite-evaluation estimates and cumulative bound, this proves Proposition 10.2.

\(\Box\)

10.7Convergence of the coupled histories

Recall the schedule from Section 10.2: \(J (m)\) is the stage at scale \(m\), \(L_m = \mathscr{L}_{J (m + 1)}\) is the block ratio, and \(\tau_m = \log a_m^{- 1}\). The reference inverse coupling \(\hat{\beta}_m = \bar{\beta} + \kappa_G \tau_m\) is distinct from the tuned actual inverse coupling \(\beta_m\).

Theorem 10.3. (Fixed-scale convergence) Use the common construction above with its strengthened comparison reserves and fixed coefficient functionals. If the coarse endpoints converge to \(b\), then for every fixed \(m, p\) their inverse couplings and activity arrays converge along the entire cutoff sequence. Their scalar corrections \(q_m\) converge as well. The limits depend on \(b\) and the fixed common construction, and not on the choices made in shooting. No convergence of additive vacuum normalizations is asserted.

Proof. First prescribe two inverse coupling sequences having the same limit at each fixed level. At a level \(k > m\) replace the activity array by zero and run the same maps downwards using the prescribed inverse coupling sequence, without imposing the scalar recursion on this auxiliary history. The hypothesis on its domain is needed here. Repeated use of (10.13) gives

\(\displaystyle \|x_m - x_m^{[k]} \| \leqslant (1 / 16)^{k - m} .\) (10.17)

Figure 10.1 shows the common interval of levels on which the two truncated arrays are compared.

Figure 10.1. Activity comparison at a fixed period \(R_p\). The two solid rows reach the same tuned inverse coupling \(\bar{\beta}\) at level zero. In each dashed run the activity is reset to zero at \(k\), retaining that row's inverse couplings and block maps down to \(m\). At \(m\) the error from this replacement is at most \(16^{- (k - m)}\) in each row, uniformly in its cutoff.

For fixed \(k\), the truncated arrays converge by finite downward induction and the common-input cutoff comparison. This statement is Cauchy convergence: apply that comparison to arbitrary pairs of subsequences. Then choose \(k\) so the two errors in (10.17) are small. Completeness gives a common activity limit for both prescribed inverse coupling sequences, and common scalar correction limits follow from the same local comparison.

At each fixed scale the inverse coupling coordinate lies in a compact real interval. A diagonal subsequence therefore makes all inverse coupling coordinates converge; the preceding paragraph supplies activity limits without compactness of their norm balls. Compare two such limiting histories. The finite scalar recursion, summed from their common bottom endpoint, gives

\(\displaystyle b_m (\lambda_m) - b_m (\lambda'_m) = - \sum_{i < m} (q_i - q'_i) .\) (10.18)

The limits of the common path corrections \(\alpha_{N, i}\) cancel. There is no infinite sum or ultraviolet boundary term.

The estimates (10.13) pass to the two limits. To justify this without extending a map arbitrarily to the completed space, at each finite cutoff run the second limiting inverse coupling sequence with a zero terminal array. The first paragraph identifies its limit with the second history. Compare it with the first history at the same cutoff and pass to the limit. Put \(d_m = \max \{| \lambda_m - \lambda'_m |, \|x_m - x'_m \|\}\). The activity discrepancy is at most \(1 / 4\). By (10.12), (10.18), and the individual scalar bounds, its inverse coupling discrepancy is at most \(\mathfrak{b}_m^{- 1} \sum_{i < m} s_i \leqslant 1 / 128\). Hence \(D = \sup_m d_m \leqslant 1 / 4\). The sharper scalar comparison now gives

\(\displaystyle | \lambda_m - \lambda'_m | \leqslant 4\mathfrak{b}_m^{- 1} \sum_{i < m} s_i D \leqslant D / 32, \qquad \|x_m - x'_m \| \leqslant D / 16.\)

Thus \(D \leqslant D / 16\) and \(D = 0\). Every subsequence has a further subsequence with these same limits. A subsequence staying a positive distance away at one fixed level would contradict this fact, so the whole sequence converges. Finally \(b_m\) is continuous, giving the asserted convergence of the physical inverse couplings.\(\Box\)

10.8Identifying the analytic comparison on the selected family

For clarity we record what the additional input requires of the local integral, so it can be checked independently of the preceding abstract argument. The finite path comparisons must use the same fixed coefficient functionals and rooted estimates as the actual history. Replacing a terminal Wilson normalization must leave every fixed lower-scale construction unchanged eventually, or have an explicitly vanishing comparison error. A new selection of equally bounded coefficients at each occurrence would not suffice.

For a finite set of activity labels, write both local integrals in common physical coordinates. Converging means and positive precisions, with their uniform inverse and derivative bounds, give convergence against a common Gaussian majorant. The protected change of variables keeps the physical fine event indicators fixed, including their reference thresholds, and retains its common Jacobian. The common majorant of Chapter 6 concerns the actual source and event indicator; it cannot be replaced by a bound for an unrelated flat Gaussian. The projected scalar coefficient also needs convergence in its local derivative norm. Convergence of the gas alone does not identify it.

The infinite dictionaries require a separate tail argument. For an input array \(f\), let \(b_f (i)\) be the supremum of the absolute actual activity, including its event indicator. On a fixed finite torus the activity bounds give \(\sum_i b_f (i) \leqslant C_m \|f\|\). A Cauchy sequence has uniformly small value tails: compare with one late fixed member, truncate that member's summable tail, and use \(b_f (i) \leqslant b_{f - g} (i) + b_g (i)\). This does not assert density of finite arrays in every derivative norm. Output derivative tails require the stronger support reserve in the connected expansion. The local projection is finite and the fusion sum has a strict convergence margin, so these estimates, when proved on the common inputs, survive both operations.

The auxiliary zero-array histories used in the proof require the same checks. Their incoming activity bounds vanish, but their path, stationary branch, cutoff geometry and differentiated Gaussian factors do not vanish. One must use the same block bridge, clipping data and stationary graph. Uniform implicit-function uniqueness identifies a auxiliary graph built from the same repeated block steps with the selected one; merely invoking the existence of some admissible stationary graph is insufficient.

The schedule, finite-history tuning and logarithmic running follow from the finite-step theorem. The fixed-scale conclusion is Theorem 10.3. Steps 2 and 4 in the proof of Proposition 10.2 establish the stronger local comparisons from the protected expansion and converging background coefficients. Proposition 5.2 supplies the fixed-scale quadratic limit for the actual nested history. This completes the fixed-scale construction, which remains distinct from subsequent convergence of all continuum correlations.
