Chapter 3
The free Maxwell comparison theory

The reference theory for short distances consists of independent Maxwell fields, one for each direction in the Lie algebra. Its role is more precise than a formal approximation to the action. It supplies finite-dimensional spaces of local fields, correlation functionals that distinguish those fields, and a normalization for the stress tensor. The interacting construction must eventually reproduce these data in an appropriate short-distance comparison. No interacting limit is assumed in this chapter.

We fix the positive inner product on the real Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{g}\) and write \(d_{\mathfrak{g}} = \dim_{\mathbb{R}} \mathfrak{g}\). Use the orthonormal basis of Section 1.1, \((e_{\alpha})_{\alpha = 1}^{d_{\mathfrak{g}}}\). Greek letters \(\mu, \nu, \rho, \sigma\) from the middle of the alphabet denote coordinate indices in \(\{1, 2, 3, 4\}\); \(\alpha, \beta\) denote indices in this basis. Time is the fourth coordinate. The superscript \(\text{Max}\) always denotes the free reference theory. We first define its correlations at distinct points; this requires no multiplication of distributions at the same point.

3.1Curvature covariance

Let

\(\displaystyle \Gamma (x) = \frac{1}{4 \pi^2 |x|^2}, \qquad x \ne 0.\) (3.1)

Its locally integrable extension is the fundamental solution of \(- \Delta\) in four dimensions. A convenient way to specify the curvature covariance is to start from independent Gaussian vector-potential components with covariance \(\delta_{\alpha \beta} \delta_{\mu \nu} \Gamma (x - y)\), and take a curl in each argument. The vector potential in this description is a device for computing the covariance; it is not one of the gauge-invariant local observables used in the construction.

Write \(F^{\alpha}_{\mu \nu} = - F^{\alpha}_{\nu \mu}\) for the curvature components in this basis. The covariance is the product of the inner product on \(\mathfrak{g}\) and a spacetime kernel \(C\): pairing the two fields with \(u, v \in \mathfrak{g}\) gives \(\langle u, v \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}} C_{\mu \nu, \rho \sigma} (x - y)\). In coordinates,

\(\displaystyle S^{\text{Max}}_2 (F^{\alpha}_{\mu \nu}, F^{\beta}_{\rho \sigma} ; x, y) = \delta_{\alpha \beta} C_{\mu \nu, \rho \sigma} (x - y),\) (3.2)

where, with \(h_{\mu \nu} = \partial_{\mu} \partial_{\nu} \Gamma\),

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} C_{\mu \nu, \rho \sigma} & = - \delta_{\nu \sigma} h_{\mu \rho} + \delta_{\nu \rho} h_{\mu \sigma} + \delta_{\mu \sigma} h_{\nu \rho} - \delta_{\mu \rho} h_{\nu \sigma}, \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.3)} \\ h_{\mu \nu} (z) & = \frac{8 z_{\mu} z_{\nu} - 2 \delta_{\mu \nu} |z|^2}{4 \pi^2 |z|^6} . \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.4)} \end{array}\)

The minus signs in the first and last terms arise because a derivative in \(y\) is minus a derivative in \(z = x - y\). This convention matters both for reflection positivity and for the stress normalization.

The covariance is translation invariant and transforms as a two-form in each pair of indices under \(O (4)\). At nonzero separation it satisfies

\(\displaystyle \partial_{\lambda} C_{\mu \nu, \rho \sigma} + \partial_{\mu} C_{\nu \lambda, \rho \sigma} + \partial_{\nu} C_{\lambda \mu, \rho \sigma} = 0, \qquad \sum_{\mu} \partial_{\mu} C_{\mu \nu, \rho \sigma} = 0.\) (3.5)

The first identity is the Bianchi identity; the second is the source-free Maxwell equation. They hold off the origin. A distributional derivative can have a contact term at the origin, so these identities must not be used to infer a choice of contact extension.

For a multi-index \(a = (a_1, \ldots, a_4)\), put \(|a| = a_1 + \cdots + a_4\) and \(\partial^a = \partial_1^{a_1} \cdots \partial_4^{a_4}\). Derivatives of the fields have covariance

\(\displaystyle S^{\text{Max}}_2 (\partial^a F^{\alpha}_{\mu \nu}, \partial^c F^{\beta}_{\rho \sigma} ; x, y) = \delta_{\alpha \beta} (- 1)^{|c|} \partial^{a + c} C_{\mu \nu, \rho \sigma} (x - y) .\) (3.6)

In particular, for each fixed derivative order, this kernel is smooth away from zero and homogeneous of degree \(- 4 - |a| - |c|\). Thus its size on \(|x - y| \geqslant r\), \(0 < r \leqslant 1\), has a polynomial bound in \(r^{- 1}\). This elementary estimate is the free counterpart of the much harder uniform bounds required from the lattice construction.

3.2Local polynomials and Wick correlations

A local polynomial is a polynomial in finitely many curvature derivatives at one point. Its scaling dimension is computed by assigning degree \(2\) to \(F\) and degree \(1\) to each derivative. For example, \(F^{\alpha}_{\mu \nu} F^{\beta}_{\rho \sigma}\) has degree \(4\), whereas \(\partial_{\lambda} F^{\alpha}_{\mu \nu}\) has degree \(3\). Scaling dimension is an algebraic grading of the reference model; it is not an assertion about anomalous dimensions of the interacting fields.

The polynomial attached to one insertion is Wick ordered. We use the usual Gaussian pairing rule, whose operator formulation is due to Wick [Wic50]. Here it can be taken as the definition of the separated correlation functions. Expand each polynomial into monomials, regard each factor as a distinguishable occurrence, and sum over pairings of all occurrences subject to the condition that paired occurrences belong to different insertions. For each pair use (3.6). The empty pairing has value one. The resulting finite sum is denoted by

\(\displaystyle S_n^{\text{Max}} (P_1, \ldots, P_n ; x_1, \ldots, x_n), \qquad x_i \ne x_j \quad (i \ne j) .\)

There is no covariance at zero separation in this definition.

For instance, if \(X_1, X_2, Y_1, Y_2\) are linear curvature derivatives,

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} S_2^{\text{Max}} (: X_1 X_2 :, : Y_1 Y_2 : ; x, y) & = C (X_1, Y_1 ; x - y) C (X_2, Y_2 ; x - y)\\ & \quad + C (X_1, Y_2 ; x - y) C (X_2, Y_1 ; x - y) . \end{array}\)

Here \(C (X, Y ; z)\) denotes the bilinear covariance obtained from (3.6). By contrast, in a word containing several insertions in the same time half-space, contractions between those different insertions are included. Wick ordering removes self-contractions at each insertion, not all contractions inside a word.

The pairing rule gives complex multilinearity, symmetry under simultaneous permutation of labels and positions, reality, and the unit identity

\(\displaystyle S_{n + 1}^{\text{Max}} (1, P_1, \ldots, P_n ; x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n) = S_n^{\text{Max}} (P_1, \ldots, P_n ; x_1, \ldots, x_n) .\)

Differentiating an insertion differentiates its incident covariances. The product rule therefore agrees with ordinary differentiation of the correlation function in that insertion position.

If the labels are homogeneous of degrees \(d_1, \ldots, d_n\), then

\(\displaystyle S_n^{\text{Max}} (P_1, \ldots, P_n ; \lambda x_1, \ldots, \lambda x_n) = \lambda^{- \sum_i d_i} S_n^{\text{Max}} (P_1, \ldots, P_n ; x_1, \ldots, x_n) \quad (\lambda > 0) .\) (3.7)

For general labels of bounded degree, decompose into finitely many homogeneous pieces. Equations (3.6) and (3.7) give bounds on all derivatives on separated configuration spaces. In particular, smearing against a smooth compactly supported function whose support avoids collisions is well defined. This statement is weaker than specifying the correlations on test functions whose supports meet collision diagonals.

3.3The on-shell polynomial space

The Maxwell identities impose linear relations on the jet variables. They should be imposed before discussing strict positivity or the number of independent fields. At each integer \(j \geqslant 0\), let \(\mathcal{S}_j\) be the real vector space of homogeneous degree-\(j\) polynomial two-forms on \(\mathbb{R}^4\), with values in \(\mathfrak{g}\), satisfying

\(\displaystyle dF = 0, \qquad \delta F = 0.\)

Here \(\delta F = 0\) means \(\sum_{\mu} \partial_{\mu} F_{\mu \nu} = 0\) for every \(\nu\); the harmless choice of sign for the codifferential does not affect its kernel. These are finite-dimensional spaces. They encode the possible homogeneous Taylor coefficients of a source-free Maxwell curvature. A Taylor coefficient of order \(j\) is normalized as \(D^j F (0) / j\)!.

The phrase on shell will mean restriction to these solution jets. It does not refer to restricting a point of Euclidean spacetime. A linear coordinate on \(\mathcal{S}_j\) has scaling dimension \(j + 2\). As in Definition 2.2, we also call ‘weight' the scaling dimension of a homogeneous jet polynomial. Consequently polynomials of weight at most \(d\) use only the spaces \(\mathcal{S}_j\) with \(j \leqslant d - 2\). Constants are retained at every degree. The action on \(\mathfrak{g}\) is the adjoint action of \(G\); invariant polynomials are the gauge-invariant local observables of the free reference theory.

To compare this definition with the radial-potential labels \(V_d\), associate to a closed homogeneous curvature \(F^{(j)}\) the radial potential

\(\displaystyle A^{(j + 1)}_{\mu} (x) = \int_0^1 tx^{\nu} F^{(j)}_{\nu \mu} (tx) \hspace{0.17em} dt = \frac{1}{j + 2} \sum_{\nu} x^{\nu} F^{(j)}_{\nu \mu} (x) .\) (3.8)

There is a sum over \(\nu\) in the integral as well. This is the usual radial homotopy formula: \(x^{\mu} A_{\mu} = 0\) and \(dA = F\) when \(dF = 0\). The connection here is the linear Maxwell potential; no nonabelian commutator is inserted into this formula. Restricting a label in \(V_d\) to these radial potentials gives a map

\(\displaystyle \omega_d : V_d \longrightarrow \{ \text{complex polynomials on Maxwell solution jets of weight at most } d\} .\)

It preserves the unit, conjugation and the geometric actions. Define

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{M}_d^{\text{Max}} = \text{im} \hspace{0.17em} \omega_d .\) (3.9)

Equivalently, this is \(V_d / \ker \omega_d\) as a vector space. The image formulation is useful: it does not assume a separate surjectivity theorem onto a larger invariant-polynomial space.

The covariance identities (3.5) make the Wick correlations well defined on these on-shell polynomial images. Products of a Maxwell equation with another jet polynomial vanish in every separated Wick correlation. Conversely, proving that all correlation-null polynomials vanish on shell requires a nondegeneracy argument; it does not follow from writing down the equations.

The free spaces form a filtration. The inclusion \(\mathcal{M}_d^{\text{Max}} \to \mathcal{M}_e^{\text{Max}}\) for \(d \leqslant e\) retains the same polynomial, while differentiation raises weight by one. For a simple nonabelian Lie algebra there is no nonzero invariant linear form on \(\mathfrak{g}\). Therefore every gauge-invariant field of degree less than four in this polynomial image is a constant. This low-degree statement follows from the weights: a nonconstant monomial below degree four would have just one curvature factor or one of its first derivatives.

3.4Reflection and the meaning of nondegeneracy

Let \(\vartheta (\boldsymbol{x}, t) = (\boldsymbol{x}, - t)\). Reflection acts on a two-form by pullback, so a component with one time index changes sign and a purely spatial component does not. On a local polynomial, \(\theta\) combines this action with complex conjugation. On a word it also reverses the order and reflects every insertion point. This is the antilinear reflection used in the Osterwalder–Schrader form, not merely conjugation of scalar coefficients.

For words \(w_i\) supported at distinct points in the positive half-space, the free field has reflection positivity:

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i, j} \overline{c_i} c_j S^{\text{Max}} (\theta w_i \hspace{0.17em} w_j) \geqslant 0.\) (3.10)

The same inequality holds for smooth joint test functions supported on separated positive-time configurations. This is the standard Gaussian reflection-positive construction underlying Osterwalder–Schrader reconstruction [OS73, OS75], applied to the curvature covariance rather than to an unconstrained vector-potential observable. Its hypotheses can be checked at the one-particle level: spatial Fourier transformation of (3.1) gives

\(\displaystyle \frac{e^{- |\boldsymbol{p}| (t + s)}}{2 |\boldsymbol{p}|} \qquad (t, s > 0),\)

and taking the two curls with the reflected two-form indices gives the positive transverse-polarization Gram form. Derivative jets act on the same factors. The Gaussian Fock construction then gives (3.10); contractions within each positive-time word are included in its Fock vector. On compact separated supports the kernels are smooth, so the corresponding form on joint test functions follows by integration. We use this standard argument by citation rather than repeating the Fock-space construction.

A simple sign check is informative. At separation \(z = re_4\), the six unordered curvature components have diagonal covariance. Three purely spatial components have value \(1 / (\pi^2 r^4)\); the three components \(F_{i 4}\) have value \(- 1 / (\pi^2 r^4)\). Reflection changes the sign of \(F_{i 4}\) in the first slot. The reflected one-jet form is therefore positive in all six directions. Without the tensorial reflection, the same displayed covariance would give the wrong positivity statement.

Positive semidefiniteness of (3.10) alone does not identify its null space. For later rank arguments one needs the stronger statement that a nonzero on-shell polynomial has a strictly positive reflected two-point pairing at two suitable points. Derivatives of different orders may mix in that pairing. Checking one homogeneous order at a time does not establish the statement for their sum.

3.5Strict positivity of finite jet families

We now identify the null relations in the free polynomial theory. Recall from Section 3.3 that \(\mathcal{S}_j\) is the space of homogeneous degree-\(j\) Maxwell curvature solutions. Write a member as

\(\displaystyle P_{ab} (v) = \sum_{i_1, \ldots, i_j} P_{ab ; i_1 \cdots i_j} v_{i_1} \cdots v_{i_j} .\)

The derivative-index coefficients are symmetric. On this coefficient space use the Frobenius inner product, summing both orders of the antisymmetric field indices, all ordered derivative indices and components in the basis of Section 1.1. In particular, no factor \(1 / 2\) is inserted for the field indices. The linear jet probe represented by \(P\) is

\(\displaystyle \ell_P (F) = \frac{1}{j!} \sum_{a, b, i_1, \ldots, i_j, \alpha} P^{\alpha}_{ab ; i_1 \cdots i_j} \partial_{i_1} \cdots \partial_{i_j} F^{\alpha}_{ab} .\) (3.11)

The factor \(1 / j\)! matches our Taylor convention. Every linear functional on the order-\(j\) solution jets has this form.

Put \(I (z) = z / |z|^2\) and \(R_z = 1 - 2 zz^{\textsf{T}} / |z|^2\). For a two-form, \(R_z^{(2)}\) means applying \(R_z\) to both form indices. The full reflection on a polynomial solution is \((R_z \cdot P) (v) = R_z^{(2)} P (R_z v)\): the derivative indices are reflected as well. For real probes define

\(\displaystyle B_z (P, Q) = S_2^{\text{Max}} (\ell_P, \ell_{R_z \cdot Q} ; z, 0) .\)

Extend this bilinearly across different derivative orders. Covariance and exchange of the two insertions show that \(B_z\) is symmetric.

Proposition 3.1. (Finite-jet nondegeneracy) For every \(z \ne 0\) and integer \(m \geqslant 1\), the form \(B_z\) is positive definite on \(\bigoplus_{j < m} \mathcal{S}_j\).

The scalar differentiation identity needed in the proof is the harmonic case of Hobson's differentiation formula [Hob92]. In our normalization, if \(P \in \mathcal{S}_j\), it says, componentwise,

\(\displaystyle \frac{1}{j!} P (\partial) \Gamma (y) = \frac{(- 2)^j}{4 \pi^2} |y|^{- 2 j - 2} P (y), \qquad y \ne 0.\) (3.12)

Here \(P (\partial)\) means the ordered coefficient contraction without the factor \(1 / j\)!. Maxwell solutions are harmonic, as follows by applying a divergence to the Bianchi identity. Thus the harmonic hypothesis of the scalar formula holds in each component. We use that classical differentiation result; the following finite-dimensional argument explains its application to the mixed jet orders needed here.

Proof. Let \(\mathcal{R} (P) (y)\) be the curvature response obtained by contracting \(\partial_y^j C (y) / j\)! with the coefficients of \(P\). Taking the two curls in (3.12) gives

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R} (P) (y) = c_j |y|^{- 4} R_y^{(2)} P (I (y)), \qquad c_j = \frac{(j + 1) (j + 2) (- 2)^j}{\pi^2} .\) (3.13)

For clarity, the identities determining the coefficient are as follows. Set \(V_i (y) = \sum_a y_a P_{ai} (y)\) and \(b_j = (- 2)^j / (4 \pi^2)\). The divergence of the differentiated Green potential is \(D_i = - 2 (j + 1) b_j |y|^{- 2 j - 4} V_i\). The Maxwell equations and homogeneity give \(\partial_i V_m - \partial_m V_i = (j + 2) P_{im}\). The response is \(2 (\partial_m D_i - \partial_i D_m)\), so its coefficient is \(4 (j + 1) (j + 2) b_j = c_j\) and its two-form factor is

\(\displaystyle P_{im} (y) - 2 |y|^{- 2} (y_i V_m (y) - y_m V_i (y)) = (R_y^{(2)} P (y))_{im} .\)

This verifies (3.13), including its sign, off the origin.

Fix the separation \(z\). For \(P \in \mathcal{S}_j\) define a finite tower \(\Sigma_z P\) by

\(\displaystyle (\Sigma_z P)_k = \frac{c_j}{c_k} \text{Taylor}_k [P (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - I (z))], \qquad 0 \leqslant k \leqslant j.\) (3.14)

The operator \(\text{Taylor}_k\) extracts the homogeneous term of degree \(k\) at zero. Translation preserves the Maxwell equations, and each homogeneous term separately satisfies them. Thus all components in (3.14) lie in their declared solution spaces. The degree-\(j\) component is \(P\) itself.

By the finite Taylor formula, the response of this tower is

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R} (\Sigma_z P) (y) = c_j |y|^{- 4} R_y^{(2)} P (I (y) - I (z)) .\)

Since \(DI (z) = |z|^{- 2} R_z\), its Taylor coefficients at \(y = z\) vanish through order \(j - 1\), while its order-\(j\) coefficient is \(c_j |z|^{- 2 j - 4} (R_z \cdot P)\). For \(Q \in \mathcal{S}_k\), differentiation in the second insertion gives

\(\displaystyle B_z (\Sigma_z P, Q) = (- 1)^k \langle R_z \cdot Q, \text{Taylor}_k [\mathcal{R}(\Sigma_z P)] (z) \rangle_F .\)

It follows that this expression vanishes if \(k < j\), and at \(k = j\) equals \((- 1)^j c_j |z|^{- 2 j - 4} \langle P, Q \rangle_F\). Transforming the second argument introduces only lower orders in addition to its unchanged top order. Symmetry therefore gives, on the full tower,

\(\displaystyle B_z (\Sigma_z P, \Sigma_z Q) = \sum_{j < m} \frac{(j + 1) (j + 2) 2^j}{\pi^2 |z|^{2 j + 4}} \langle P_j, Q_j \rangle_F .\) (3.15)

Every coefficient is positive. Finally \(\Sigma_z\) is invertible: it is the identity plus a strictly degree-lowering map, hence its inverse is a finite geometric sum. This proves positivity on the whole tower, including mixtures of derivative orders.\(\Box\)

Corollary 3.2. (Polynomial positivity and finite separating families) Every nonzero element of \(\mathcal{M}_d^{\text{Max}}\) has strictly positive reflected two-point pairing at a fixed separated pair. For any basis \((P_1, \ldots, P_s)\) of this space and fixed distinct points \(x, y\), the linear map

\(\displaystyle P \longmapsto (S_2^{\text{Max}} (P, P_j ; x, y))_{j = 1}^s\)

is injective. Consequently a label in \(V_d\) vanishes in every separated correlation if and only if its on-shell polynomial \(\omega_d\) vanishes.

Proof. This is the standard symmetric-Fock-space consequence of the one-particle Gram form and Wick's rule [Wic50]. Explicitly, choose coordinates \(X_1, \ldots, X_M\) diagonalizing the positive form on a tower containing all variables of the polynomial, with positive squared lengths \(b_i\). The reflected pairing of Wick monomials is

\(\displaystyle \langle X^a, X^c \rangle_{\text{ref}} =\boldsymbol{1}_{a = c} \hspace{0.17em} a! \prod_i b_i^{a_i} .\)

Thus the squared norm of a polynomial is the sum of the squared absolute values of its coefficients with these positive weights; the constant coefficient has weight one. Restricting to the gauge-invariant image preserves positivity. If all basis-column pairings vanish, the pairing with the reflected conjugate of the polynomial vanishes too, forcing that polynomial to be zero. This also identifies the correlation-null labels. On any fixed finite-dimensional degree space, the map of correlation pairings has a positive minimum norm on the unit sphere. Small perturbations of its matrix remain injective; applying this fact to interacting correlations still requires a quantitative comparison estimate.\(\Box\)

3.6Quadratic fields and normalization checks

We return to the free curvature covariance (3.2); in the basis of Section 1.1, each component has the same covariance and orthogonal components have zero cross-covariance. Define

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} Q_{\mu \nu} & = \sum_{\rho = 1}^4 : \langle F_{\mu \rho}, F_{\nu \rho} \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}} :, & Q_{\text{tot}} & = \sum_{\mu} Q_{\mu \mu}, \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.16)} \\ \mathcal{E}^{\text{Max}} & = \tfrac{1}{4} Q_{\text{tot}}, & T^{\text{Max}}_{\mu \nu} & = - Q_{\mu \nu} + \tfrac{1}{4} \delta_{\mu \nu} Q_{\text{tot}} . \hspace{3cm} \text{(3.17)} \end{array}\)

The sign of \(T^{\text{Max}}\) is chosen to match the shell-flux convention in the supplied construction. A complete stress-normalization argument must check that convention against the translation Ward identity; conservation alone does not determine the sign.

The tensor in (3.17) is symmetric and traceless. Its divergence vanishes as an on-shell Wick polynomial. These are the ordinary algebraic Maxwell stress identities, obtained from the Bianchi and source-free equations; the same identities hold in separated correlations. The scalar \(\mathcal{E}^{\text{Max}}\) is nevertheless nonzero. It is the curvature-square operator, not the trace of the free stress tensor.

With the present normalization, Wick's rule gives

\(\displaystyle S_2^{\text{Max}} (\mathcal{E}^{\text{Max}}, \mathcal{E}^{\text{Max}} ; x, y) = \frac{3 d_{\mathfrak{g}}}{\pi^4 |x - y|^8}, \qquad x \ne y.\) (3.18)

For checking constants, at separation \(re_4\) one has \(\mathcal{E}^{\text{Max}} = \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{\mu < \nu} : \|F_{\mu \nu} \|_{\mathfrak{g}}^2\):. The two cross-pairings of each square, the six unordered components, and the prefactor \(1 / 4\) give \(\tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 \cdot 6 d_{\mathfrak{g}} = 3 d_{\mathfrak{g}}\). Orthogonal covariance makes the answer independent of the direction of \(x - y\). The calculation uses only separated Wick contractions.

This normalization will be needed when comparing a renormalized scalar field with the free curvature square. It does not itself identify the interacting trace, its coefficient, or its logarithmic asymptotic. Those conclusions require the interacting stress Ward identity and quantitative estimates on moving comparison fields.

3.7The degree-four conserved tensor

The finite-dimensional classification needed for the stress tensor is small enough to state explicitly. In this section centered means that the constant Wick coefficient is zero. By the Wick prescription it is also the one-point expectation. We consider real symmetric tensor fields \(J_{ab}\) of degree at most four, invariant under the adjoint action and transforming as a rank-two tensor under the signed coordinate permutations \(H_4\). Conservation is an identity in the on-shell polynomial space, or equivalently in every separated free correlation by Corollary 3.2.

Proposition 3.3. (Conserved tensor line) The space of such centered tensors has dimension three. Its conserved subspace is the line \(\mathbb{R}T^{\text{Max}}\), with \(T^{\text{Max}}\) as in (3.17).

Proof. Simplicity of \(\mathfrak{g}\) excludes invariant linear forms on \(\mathfrak{g}\) and makes an invariant symmetric bilinear form on \(\mathfrak{g}\) a multiple of the fixed metric. Through degree four a centered invariant polynomial therefore contains two undifferentiated curvature factors. Indeed, a derivative on one of two factors would raise the degree to at least five, while a single differentiated factor still has no invariant linear functional on \(\mathfrak{g}\).

Recall the quadratic fields \(Q_{ab}\) and \(Q_{\text{tot}}\) from (3.16). A basis of the tensor candidates is

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} Z^{(1)}_{ab} & = \delta_{ab} Q_{aa},\\ Z^{(2)}_{ab} & = \delta_{ab} (Q_{\text{tot}} - 2 Q_{aa}),\\ Z^{(3)}_{ab} & = (1 - \delta_{ab}) Q_{ab} . \end{array}\)

There is no sum over \(a\) on the right sides of the first two formulas. To see that these span, consider sign reversal of each coordinate. A diagonal component can contain only squares of curvature components. Permutations fixing its distinguished axis divide them into two classes: those containing that axis, and those containing neither instance of it. An off-diagonal component with indices \(a, b\) must be odd in axes \(a, b\) and even in the others. Its possible contractions are \(: \langle F_{ac}, F_{bc} \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\): with \(c \not\in \{a, b\}\); permutations identify their coefficients. This gives exactly the three candidates displayed above.

For an explicit rank check, let \(Q_{ij, ij} = : \|F_{ij} \|_{\mathfrak{g}}^2\):. Evaluate the first component of the divergence of each \(Z^{(l)}\) at \(e_1\), against \(Q_{12, 12} (0)\) and \(Q_{23, 23} (0)\). The two rows of these evaluations, after division by \(d_{\mathfrak{g}} / (4 \pi^2)^2\), are

\(\displaystyle \left( \begin{array}{ccc} - 256 & 0 & 128\\ 0 & - 512 & - 128 \end{array} \right) .\) (3.19)

These numbers come directly from the curvature covariance (3.3) and the two cross-pairings in a quadratic Wick correlation. For example, \(S_2^{\text{Max}} (Q_{11}, Q_{12, 12} ; te_1, 0) = 2 d_{\mathfrak{g}} / (\pi^4 t^8)\); its derivative at \(t = 1\) gives the first entry after the stated division. The first two columns are independent. Also \(Z^{(1)}_{12} = Z^{(2)}_{12} = 0\), whereas the correlation of \(Z^{(3)}_{12} (e_1)\) with \(: \langle F_{13}, F_{23} \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}} : (0)\) equals \(- 16 d_{\mathfrak{g}} / (4 \pi^2)^2\). Thus the candidate tensors are themselves independent, and the kernel of divergence has dimension at most one.

The Maxwell stress is conserved and

\(\displaystyle - T^{\text{Max}} = \tfrac{1}{2} Z^{(1)} - \tfrac{1}{4} Z^{(2)} + Z^{(3)} .\)

It is nonzero by the preceding off-diagonal correlation. Hence it spans that kernel.\(\Box\)

This result fixes only a line of conserved tensors. Multiplying the stress by a scalar preserves all the properties used in the proposition. We next fix its normalization by a local Ward flux and then classify linear responses on fields of arbitrary bounded degree.

3.8Local stress fluxes

The sign in (3.17) is fixed by a local identity, not by conservation. Let \(x_0, \ldots, x_n\) be distinct points and choose a real \(\chi \in C_c^{\infty} (\mathbb{R}^4)\) equal to one near \(x_0\), with support avoiding \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\). For a vector field \(v\) define the flux

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{F}_{\chi, v} (P_0 ; P_1, \ldots, P_n) = - \sum_{a, b} \int \partial_a \chi (y) v_b (y) S_{n + 2}^{\text{Max}} (T^{\text{Max}}_{ab}, P_0, \ldots, P_n ; y, x_0, \ldots, x_n) \hspace{0.17em} dy.\) (3.20)

The integrand is supported on \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \nabla \chi\), a compact set separated from all the insertions. It is therefore an ordinary integral of a smooth function; no extension of a product through \(x_0\) is involved.

For a constant translation \(v = e_b\), let \(\delta_v = \partial_b\) act on local polynomial labels. For \(v (y) = K (y - x_0)\), \(K^{\textsf{T}} = - K\), let \(\delta_v =\mathcal{L}_K\) be the infinitesimal action induced on jets by the pullback of two-forms under \(y \mapsto x_0 + e^{tK} (y - x_0)\). Equivalently, on a smooth closed two-form \(f\) its variation is

\(\displaystyle (\mathcal{L}_v f)_{ij} = v_b \partial_b f_{ij} + (\partial_i v_b) f_{bj} + (\partial_j v_b) f_{ib} = (d \iota_v f)_{ij},\) (3.21)

with sums over \(b\). On polynomial labels the action is the induced Leibniz derivation, including the derivative indices of each jet. For the dilation \(v (y) = y - x_0\), this action at the center is the weight operator

\(\displaystyle \mathscr{E} = \sum_{j, \lambda} (j + 2) X_{j, \lambda} \frac{\partial}{\partial X_{j, \lambda}},\) (3.22)

where \(X_{j, \lambda}\) are any linear coordinates on order-\(j\) solution jets, including the \(\mathfrak{g}\) components. The sum is finite on each polynomial.

Proposition 3.4. (Normalized free Ward identities) For translations, centered rotations and centered dilation,

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{F}_{\chi, v} (P_0 ; P_1, \ldots, P_n) = S_{n + 1}^{\text{Max}} (\delta_v P_0, P_1, \ldots, P_n ; x_0, \ldots, x_n) .\) (3.23)

This holds for arbitrary finite Wick polynomials and arbitrary finite spectator lists. The common coefficient is \(+ 1\) for the stress (3.17) and the outward-flux convention (3.20).

The Ward mechanism is the standard free-field action of the stress current. We include the normalization and the treatment of two contractions at the enclosed insertion, since neither is supplied by a formal integration by parts through a singularity. Wick's theorem is used throughout in its usual form [Wic50].

Proof. Polarize our stress between closed two-forms \(f, g\):

\(\displaystyle t_{ab} (f, g) = - \sum_c (\langle f_{ac}, g_{bc} \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}} + \langle g_{ac}, f_{bc} \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}) + \tfrac{1}{2} \delta_{ab} \sum_{c, d} \langle f_{cd}, g_{cd} \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}} .\)

The Bianchi identity gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_a \partial_a t_{ab} = - \sum_c \left( \langle (\text{div} \hspace{0.17em} f)_c, g_{bc} \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}} + \langle f_{bc}, (\text{div} \hspace{0.17em} g)_c \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}} \right) .\)

The tensor is symmetric and traceless. Thus contracting with a constant, skew-linear or scalar-linear \(v\) produces no additional divergence term from \(Dv\).

Suppose \(f\) is a smooth Maxwell wave near \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \chi\), and \(g\) is the covariance pulse from \(F_{ij} (x_0)\). Regularize its Green function by convolution with a smooth compactly supported unit-mass approximate identity \(\rho_{\epsilon}\), so \(- \Delta \Gamma_{\epsilon} = \rho_{\epsilon}\). The resulting closed pulse has divergence

\(\displaystyle (\text{div} \hspace{0.17em} g_{\epsilon})_c = \delta_{cj} \partial_i \rho_{\epsilon} (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - x_0) - \delta_{ci} \partial_j \rho_{\epsilon} (\hspace{0.17em} \cdot - x_0) .\)

Integrate the displayed divergence identity against \(\chi v\). An integration by parts against \(\rho_{\epsilon}\) shows that the flux converges to \(\partial_i (v_b f_{bj}) (x_0) - \partial_j (v_b f_{bi}) (x_0) = (\mathcal{L}_v f)_{ij} (x_0)\). Here \(\chi = 1\) near the center. On \(\text{supp} \hspace{0.17em} \nabla \chi\), the regularized covariance \(g_{\epsilon}\) and its derivatives of each fixed order converge uniformly to \(g\) and the corresponding derivatives as \(\epsilon \to 0\). Differentiating in the pulse center, while holding \(v, \chi\) fixed, gives the same identity for every jet probe. For dilation, (3.21) becomes \((y - x_0) \cdot \nabla f + 2 f\); its order-\(j\) jet at \(x_0\) is \((j + 2)\) times the original jet. This gives (3.22).

In a Wick correlation, extract the two factors at the stress insertion. The remaining sum has three types. If both partners are outside \(x_0\), the two waves are smooth throughout the cutoff support and conservation gives zero flux. If exactly one partner is at \(x_0\), the preceding calculation differentiates that occurrence. Summing its possible partners reconstructs Wick's rule, and summing occurrences gives the Leibniz derivation \(\delta_v\).

If both partners are at \(x_0\), their jet orders \(j, l\) give pulse homogeneities \(- 4 - j\) and \(- 4 - l\). Conservation away from \(x_0\) makes the flux independent of the choice of \(\chi\). Rescaling a cutoff by \(t\) about \(x_0\) multiplies this same flux by \(t^{e - 5 - j - l}\), where \(e = 0\) for translation and \(e = 1\) for rotation or dilation. Taking \(t = 2\) forces the flux to vanish. The flux integral involves the two pulses only away from their common singularity at \(x_0\). The polarization counts each ordered pair twice, canceling its factor \(1 / 2\). Combining the three types proves (3.23).\(\Box\)

3.9Separating fields by insertions of bounded scaling dimension

The polynomial labels and their scaling dimensions are those of Section 2.1; throughout this section they are evaluated in the free curvature field defined in Section 3.1.

Corollary 3.2 detects a field of degree \(d\) by another field of degree \(d\). A different statement is needed when a charge can raise degree: its response must be tested by spectators whose degree remains fixed. Their number may be arbitrary.

We first make explicit the polynomial space used here. The free image \(\mathcal{M}_d^{\text{Max}}\) in (3.9) is the full space of invariant polynomials of weight at most \(d\) on the solution jets. Indeed, the radial map identifies each order of solution jets with a linear subspace of the corresponding radial-potential space. Choose an invariant inner product and its orthogonal projection onto this subspace, separately in each weight. Composing a polynomial with these projections extends it to the radial jets without changing its weight or invariance. This is the usual compact-group averaging argument; no equality with a larger space of unrestricted formal jets is being assumed.

Let \(\mathfrak{P}\) be the polynomial algebra on all solution jets and \(\mathfrak{P}^G\) its invariant subalgebra. For \(D \geqslant 0\), define

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{N}_D = \{P \in \mathfrak{P}: S^{\text{Max}} (P (x_0), P_1 (x_1), \ldots, P_n (x_n)) = 0\},\) (3.24)

where the equality is required for every separated word with \(P_i \in \mathcal{M}_D^{\text{Max}}\). There is no restriction on the degree of \(P\) or the length \(n\).

Proposition 3.5. (A separating family of insertions of bounded scaling dimension) There is a finite \(D_{\ast}\) such that \(\mathcal{N}_{D_{\ast}} \cap \mathfrak{P}^G = \{0\}\).

Proof. First restrict all jet orders to at most \(j\). Hilbert's finite-generation argument [Hil90], using compact-group averaging, gives finitely many invariant generators homogeneous in ordinary polynomial degree. Let \(b_j\) bound their weights. To apply that standard theorem, the jet space is finite-dimensional, the polynomial ring is Noetherian, and averaging preserves its invariant ideal and homogeneous pieces. Thus an invariant polynomial of ordinary degree \(k\) is a sum of products of generators of total ordinary degree \(k\), each of weight at most \(b_j\).

If a polynomial in \(\mathcal{N}_{b_j}\) is nonzero, take its lowest nonzero ordinary-degree part \(P_k\). Express its reflected conjugate as such a sum of products, and put the factors at distinct spectator points approaching a common point different from \(x_0\). There are precisely \(k\) spectator legs. Higher-degree parts of \(P\) cannot pair with them, and for \(P_k\) every spectator leg pairs with a leg at \(x_0\). No singular spectator–spectator contraction survives. The limit is the strictly positive two-point pairing of \(P_k\) with its reflected conjugate, contradicting (3.24). This proves detection at bounded jet order.

To obtain a uniform bound, we use a collision that lowers the highest jet order. For a linear functional \(p\) on scalar order-\(j\) jets, let \(X_j (p) \in \mathfrak{g}\) be its evaluation on the spacetime indices of the jet. Write \(X_{j, \alpha} (p) = \langle e_{\alpha}, X_j (p) \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\) in the basis of Section 1.1. Fix a polynomial \(P\) with highest jet order \(j\) and a quadratic spectator \(: \langle X_k (p), X_l (q) \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\):, where \(l < k\). Contracting just its order-\(k\) leg with the top-order variables of \(P\) leaves the polynomial

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R}_{p, q, j} (z) P = \sum_{\alpha, \lambda} X_{l, \alpha} (q) C_{k, j} (p, e_{\lambda} ; z) \frac{\partial P}{\partial X_{j, \alpha} (e_{\lambda})} .\) (3.25)

The covariance \(C_{k, j}\) is in a single unit direction in \(\mathfrak{g}\). We claim that

\(\displaystyle P \in \mathcal{N}_D, \quad k + l + 4 \leqslant D \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mathcal{R}_{p, q, j} (z) P \in \mathcal{N}_D \quad (z \ne 0) .\) (3.26)

Here is the necessary separation of singularities. A differentiated curvature covariance of orders \(k, j\) is harmonic, homogeneous of degree \(- r\), \(r = 4 + k + j\), and becomes smooth after multiplication by \(|z|^{2 (r - 1)}\). A nonzero such harmonic function cannot become smooth after multiplication by \(|z|^{2 (r - 2)}\). Indeed, for a harmonic homogeneous \(H\) of degree \(- r\),

\(\displaystyle \Delta (|z|^{2 s} H) = 4 s (s + 1 - r) |z|^{2 s - 2} H.\)

Starting with a smooth extension at \(s = r - 2\) and iterating would extend \(H\) smoothly to zero, contradicting its negative homogeneity unless it vanishes. A product of two harmonic poles of orders \(r_1, r_2\), on the other hand, is cleared by \(|z|^{2 (r_1 + r_2 - 2)}\).

Insert the quadratic spectator at \(x_0 + tz\) and keep all other spectators fixed. Its two contractions with \(P\) give finitely many homogeneous product poles. Subtract them before multiplying by \(t^r\). The limit of the remaining expression is the correlation with (3.25): the lower single-contraction orders vanish, and contractions with remote spectators are smooth. The original full correlation is zero by (3.24). Successively removing the highest powers of \(t^{- 1}\) shows that the surviving harmonic pole is minus a product-pole coefficient of the same degree, or is zero. The extra radial cancellation just proved forces it to be zero in either case. This proves (3.26); in particular we have not assumed existence of the unmodified rescaled collision limit.

Put \(g = d_{\mathfrak{g}}\) and choose \(D_{\ast} \geqslant \max (b_g, 2 g + 5)\). Use \(k = g + 1\) and \(l = 0, \ldots, g - 1\). For each \(l\) choose a nonzero probe \(q_l\) and a solution jet on which it has value one. Such jets exist in every order: for example, the curl of \(e_3 \text{Re} (x_1 + ix_2)^{l + 1}\) is a nonzero homogeneous Maxwell solution of degree \(l\). If the highest order \(j\) of \(P\) exceeds \(g\), each polynomial (3.25) has smaller weight than \(P\). Strong induction on weight and (3.26) therefore make it zero.

The matrix of polynomial entries \((X_{l, \alpha} (q_l))_{l, \alpha}\) has nonzero determinant: choose the jets so that \(X_l (q_l) = e_{l + 1}\) for each \(l\). At these jets the matrix is the identity. Multiplication by its adjugate, in the polynomial integral domain, shows that every componentwise top-order contraction derivative in (3.25) vanishes. As \(p, z\) vary, the functionals \(C_{k, j} (p, \cdot ; z)\) span the dual of the order-\(j\) solution space. To verify this last assertion, a vector annihilated by all of them has a response wave whose order-\(k\) jets vanish everywhere off zero. It is then a polynomial of degree below \(k\) there; its negative homogeneity makes it zero. Formula (3.13) makes the original vector zero. Hence all top-order partial derivatives of \(P\) vanish. Descending removes every jet order above \(g\), and the fixed-order argument with \(b_g\) concludes the induction.\(\Box\)

3.10Finite correlation criteria for infinitesimal symmetries

We now give the two finite-dimensional consequences used later to normalize translations and remove an internal rotation defect. Throughout this section the Lie algebra is simple and nonabelian. Recall that \(\mathcal{M}_d^{\text{Max}}\) consists of invariant polynomials of weight at most \(d\) on the solution jets \(\mathcal{S}_j\); its elements are local Wick fields, not distributions defined on collision sets. For maps \(A\) on this space, a Ward sum means

\(\displaystyle W_A (P_1, \ldots, P_n ; x) = \sum_{i = 1}^n S_n^{\text{Max}} (P_1, \ldots, AP_i, \ldots, P_n ; x) .\)

All points in this definition are distinct. The word length is arbitrary.

Theorem 3.6. (Characterization of infinitesimal translations) Let \(d \geqslant \max (4, D_{\ast})\). Consider real-preserving complex-linear maps \(A_b : \mathcal{M}_d^{\text{Max}} \to \mathcal{M}_{d + 1}^{\text{Max}}\) which kill the unit, send \(\mathcal{M}_e^{\text{Max}}\) into \(\mathcal{M}_{e + 1}^{\text{Max}}\) for \(e \leqslant d\), and transform as a vector under signed coordinate permutations. Their Ward sums vanish identically if and only if \(A_b = c \partial_b\) for a common real scalar \(c\). There are finitely many evaluations of configuration derivatives of these Ward sums, at collinear configurations with line axis different from \(b\), whose common kernel is precisely this translation line.

Proof. Translation invariance proves one direction. We prove the converse, starting at weight four. Let \(\mathcal{S}_j^{\text{sc}}\) be the scalar-valued solution jets, so \(\mathcal{S}_j =\mathfrak{g} \otimes \mathcal{S}_j^{\text{sc}}\), and write \(U_j = (\mathcal{S}_j^{\text{sc}})^{\ast}\) for the scalar order-\(j\) probes and \(Q (p, q) = \langle X_j (p), X_k (q) \rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\) for the corresponding quadratic polynomial. The invariant metric on a simple Lie algebra is unique up to scalar. Hence \(\mathcal{M}_4^{\text{Max}}\) is spanned by the unit and \(Q (p, q)\) with \(p, q \in U_0\); weight five adds exactly the mixed quadratics with \(p \in U_0, q \in U_1\). Inversion of all coordinates acts by \((- 1)^e\) at weight \(e\). The filtration and vector parity therefore imply that \(A_b Q (p, p)\) is a mixed quadratic. It has a unique representation by a linear map \(D_p : \mathcal{S}_0^{\text{sc}} \to U_1\):

\(\displaystyle A_b Q (p, p) = \sum_{\lambda} Q (e^{\lambda}, D_p (e_{\lambda})) .\)

Uniqueness follows by assigning independent zero- and first-order solution jets in a single unit direction in \(\mathfrak{g}\). Here and until the coefficient comparison, \(b\) is fixed.

Denote the scalar covariance of probes by \(C (p, q ; z)\). The three-quadratic Wick rule is

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} & S_3^{\text{Max}} (Q (a, v), Q (p, p), Q (q, q) ; x, y, z)\\ & \quad = 4 d_{\mathfrak{g}} [C (a, p ; x - y) C (v, q ; x - z) + C (v, p ; x - y) C (a, q ; x - z)] C (p, q ; y - z) . \end{array}\)

In the Ward sum for three quadratics at \(tu, 0, z\), multiply by \(t^5\) and let \(t \downarrow 0\). The third response has no fifth-order pole. The one-jet covariance is odd, so the other two terms have opposite signs. The zero-order covariance is invertible, allowing the probe at \(z\) to be chosen so its contraction against any \(q\) is \(q (X)\), for any prescribed \(X \in \mathcal{S}_0^{\text{sc}}\). Consequently

\(\displaystyle C (D_p (X), q ; u) q (X) = C (D_q (X), p ; u) p (X) .\) (3.27)

If \(p (X) = 0\), varying \(q\) with \(q (X) \ne 0\) shows that \(D_p (X) = 0\). The separation used here follows from (3.13): a probe pairing to zero with all zero-order probes at every displacement has zero response wave, hence is zero. Thus \(D_p (X) = p (X) f_b (p)\) for some \(f_b (p) \in U_1\). Cancellation in (3.27) gives

\(\displaystyle C (f_b (p), q ; u) = C (f_b (q), p ; u) .\)

This identity and separation prove additivity and real homogeneity of \(f_b\); the original signed-coordinate covariance passes to \(f_b\).

For completeness the remaining coefficient determination is very small. Write \(c_{ij}\) for the probe \(F_{ij}\) and \(\partial_a c_{ij}\) for its first derivative. Parity allows only \(\partial_1 c_{12}, \partial_3 c_{23}, \partial_4 c_{24}\) in \(f_1 (c_{12})\). The swap of axes \(3, 4\) and the divergence equation reduce their span to \(\partial_1 c_{12}\). Similarly the candidates for \(f_1 (c_{23})\) are \(\partial_1 c_{23}, \partial_2 c_{13}, \partial_3 c_{12}\); the swap of axes \(2, 3\) and the Bianchi equation reduce them to \(\partial_1 c_{23}\). The two coefficients agree by reciprocity: differentiation of the covariance gives \(C (\partial_1 c_{12}, c_{23} ; e_1 + e_3) = - 1 / (2 \pi^2) \ne 0\). Signed permutations then give \(f_b = c_0 \partial_b\) on every probe. Since \(\partial_b Q (p, p) = 2 Q (p, \partial_b p)\), we have \(A_b = c \partial_b\) on weight four, with \(c = c_0 / 2\).

Set \(B_b = A_b - c \partial_b\). It kills the stress and still has zero Ward sums. Write its Ward sum for a word with an additional stress insertion. Apply the flux formula (3.20) to this insertion with the dilation vector field \(v (y) = y - x_0\). The term acting on the stress is zero. The free Ward identity then gives the Ward sum with \(\mathscr{E} B_b P\) in the distinguished slot and \(\mathscr{E} P\) in the other terms. Subtract the ordinary Ward sum for the word starting with \(\mathscr{E} P\). It follows that \([\mathscr{E}, B_b] P \in \mathcal{N}_d\). Proposition 3.5 makes this commutator zero, even though its weight can be \(d + 1\). Thus \(B_b\) preserves each weight eigenspace. But vector covariance makes it anticommute with coordinate inversion, which is the scalar \((- 1)^e\) on that eigenspace. Therefore \(B_b = 0\).

Finally, if every configuration derivative of a Ward sum vanishes at all strict collinear configurations along one axis other than \(b\), real analyticity implies that it vanishes everywhere. One can connect any separated configuration to such a collinear configuration without collisions: order its projections on that axis, break ties by tiny ordered perturbations, and interpolate to the resulting line points. The projected differences keep a strict sign until the separated endpoint. Analytic continuation along the segment proves the assertion. The space of permitted maps is finite-dimensional over \(\mathbb{R}\). An intersection of kernels of linear functionals on it is already the intersection of a finite subfamily (take a basis of their span in the dual). Real and imaginary parts of the evaluations give the required finite family.\(\Box\)

Theorem 3.7. (A vanishing criterion for an internal rotation defect) For \(d \geqslant 4\), let \(\Delta : \mathfrak{so} (4) \times \mathfrak{so} (4) \to \text{End}_{\mathbb{C}} (\mathcal{M}_d^{\text{Max}})\) be real-bilinear and alternating. Assume its values preserve reality and the weight filtration and kill the unit, and that

\(\displaystyle \Delta (sKs^{- 1}, sLs^{- 1}) = \rho (s) \Delta (K, L) \rho (s)^{- 1}\)

for orientation-preserving signed coordinate permutations \(s\). If every value of \(\Delta\) has zero Ward sums, then \(\Delta = 0\). It suffices to check that finitely many of these Ward sums vanish.

Proof. Fix \(K, L\) and put \(U = \Delta (K, L)\). At weight four write \(B_x (P, Q) = S_2^{\text{Max}} (P, Q ; x, 0)\). Recall \(R_x = I - 2 xx^{\textsf{T}} / |x|^2\), the reflection normal to \(x\). The zero-order covariance is \(\pi^{- 2} |x|^{- 4} \bigwedge^2 R_x\) tensored with the identity on \(\mathfrak{g}\). Wick's rule consequently gives, for \(|x| = |y|\),

\(\displaystyle B_x (P, Q) = B_y (\rho (R_y R_x) P, Q) \qquad (P, Q \in \mathcal{M}_4^{\text{Max}}) .\)

The two-point Ward sum says that \(U\) is skew for every \(B_x\). These forms are nondegenerate by strict reflected positivity. Comparing skewness for \(x\) and \(y\) therefore shows that \(U\) commutes with \(\rho (R_x R_y)\) on this space. In particular it commutes with each coordinate quarter-turn \(q_{ab}\), by choosing \(x\) parallel to \(e_a\) and \(y\) parallel to \(e_a + e_b\).

An alternating bilinear map on \(\mathfrak{so} (4)\) invariant under simultaneous conjugation by these quarter-turns is zero, even when its values lie in an arbitrary real vector space. To see this on the basis \(E_{ab} = e_a e_b^{\textsf{T}} - e_b e_a^{\textsf{T}}\), a half-turn in the plane spanned by the third and fourth axes fixes one member of a pair sharing an axis and negates the other. For a disjoint pair, the even permutation exchanging their two ordered axis pairs exchanges the arguments, so alternation gives zero. Equal pairs vanish by alternation. Bilinearity proves the claim. Covariance and the commutation just proved apply this observation to \(\Delta (K, L) P\) for every weight-four \(P\). In particular \(U\) kills the stress.

For each \(H \in \mathfrak{so} (4)\), repeat the preceding commutator argument using the rotation vector field \(v (y) = H (y - x_0)\) in the flux formula (3.20). This gives \([\mathcal{L}_H, U] P \in \mathcal{N}_d\). This time the commutator itself lies in \(\mathcal{M}_d^{\text{Max}}\), so a single reflected two-point pairing shows that it vanishes. The uniform bound \(D_{\ast}\) is unnecessary. Exponentiation in the finite-dimensional space shows that \(U\) commutes with every quarter-turn on the whole space. Covariance and the same alternating-map argument give \(\Delta (K, L) P = 0\) for every \(P\). The finiteness assertion again follows by taking a basis of the span of the Ward functionals in the dual of the finite-dimensional space of allowed \(\Delta\).\(\Box\)

Each criterion involves finitely many evaluations on a specified space of possible responses. Their application to the interacting construction requires proving that its responses obey the stated filtration, covariance, and comparison hypotheses. No such interacting input is part of the free-field results proved here.
