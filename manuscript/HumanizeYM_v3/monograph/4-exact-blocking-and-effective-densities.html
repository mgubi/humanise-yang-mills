Chapter 4
Exact blocking and effective densities

A renormalization step is first an exact integration, and only then an estimate. We define that integration on every fine configuration, including large fields. Its output is generally not a Wilson density. We therefore also introduce coordinates for the effective density: a prescribed leading action, a scalar normalization, local corrections on small-field regions, and a sum over polymers covering the large-field sites.

The gauge group, Haar measure and plaquette energy \(w\) are as in Chapter 1. Recall that \(a_m = D_m^{- 1}\) is the physical spacing, \(R_p\) is the physical period, and the odd ratio \(L_m\) relates levels \(m + 1\) and \(m\). In this chapter we examine a single step \(m + 1 \to m\). Write \(L = L_m\), fix a positive integer smoothing order \(j\), and set \(R = (j + 3) L\). Fine links \(U\) live on \(\Lambda_{R_p D_{m + 1}}\) and coarse links \(V\) on \(\Lambda_{R_p D_m}\). Patches are first described on the covering lattice and then placed periodically. The constants and path coefficients introduced here are inputs; their simultaneous choice along a trajectory will require the later estimates.

4.1Local gauges and a symmetric frame choice

A choice of gauge on a patch assigns a frame to each site. Relative to the original frames, this choice is specified by a family of matrices \(h_x \in G\): a transport \(U_{xy}\) then has matrix \(h_x U_{xy} h_y^{- 1}\). We choose \(h_0 = 1\), so that \(h_x\) expresses vectors at \(x\) in the frame at the patch centre. This lets us compare fine transports before averaging. A deterministic axial gauge is convenient for bounds but singles out coordinate directions. We combine a symmetric minimizing choice near the flat field with a finite randomized choice elsewhere. The use of gauge-covariant averages in a block transformation belongs to Bałaban's renormalization-group method [Bał87]; the precise branch rules below are part of the present construction.

Let \(\mathbb{B}_R = \{- R, \ldots, R\}^4\). For a permutation \(o\) of the four coordinate directions, denote by \(T_U (x \to y ; o)\) the transport along the path which changes coordinates in order \(o\). For a root \(z \in \mathbb{B}_R\), put

\(\displaystyle g_x^{z, o} = T_U (z \to x ; o), \qquad h_x^{z, o} = (g_0^{z, o})^{- 1} g_x^{z, o} . \) (4.1)

The normalized frame satisfies \(h_0^{z, o} = 1\). The unnormalized frame gives link coordinates \(U_{(x, \mu)}^{z, o} = g_x^{z, o} U (x, \mu) (g_{x + e_{\mu}}^{z, o})^{- 1}\). Their distances from the identity are unchanged if the normalized frame is used instead, since that change conjugates every transformed link by the same matrix.

A symmetric choice is possible if all relevant loop transports are small. For \(x, y \in \mathbb{B}_R\) and orders \(o, o_0\), define the loop based at the patch center by

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{T}_{xy ; o}^{o_0} = g_x^{0, o_0} T_U (x \to y ; o) (g_y^{0, o_0})^{- 1} .\)

Its normalized Hilbert–Schmidt distance from the identity is gauge invariant. Fix a small constant \(\delta_{\text{fr}} > 0\). If \(\chi (\mathcal{T}_{xy ; o}^{o_0}) < \delta_{\text{fr}}\) for every \(x, y \in \mathbb{B}_R\) and every \(o, o_0 \in S_4\), use the normalized minimizer, with \(h_0 = 1\), of

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{E}_U (h) = \frac{1}{24 |\mathbb{B}_R |^2} \sum_{x, y \in \mathbb{B}_R} \sum_{o \in S_4} w (h_x T_U (x \to y ; o) h_y^{- 1}) . \) (4.2)

The test includes every order \(o_0\), so it does not privilege a preliminary axial gauge.

If the test fails, use axial frames. Fix \(a_{\text{ax}} \geqslant 16\) and call a pair \((z, o)\) eligible when

\(\displaystyle \sum_{e \text{ internal to } \mathbb{B}_R} \chi (U_e^{z, o}) \leqslant a_{\text{ax}} (2 R + 1) \sum_{\square \text{ internal to } \mathbb{B}_R} \chi (U_{\square}) . \) (4.3)

Choose one pair \((z, o) \in \mathbb{B}_R \times S_4\) uniformly among all eligible pairs. Use this same root and order throughout the patch: the gauge at each \(x \in \mathbb{B}_R\) is given by \(h_x^{z, o}\) in (4.1). The coordinate paths from \(z\) form a spanning tree, and in this gauge every link of that tree is the identity. On the minimizing branch give all pairs the same uniform mass, but let each pair specify the same minimizing frame. The redundant labels allow both branches to be described by one finite sum.

The following local assertion ensures that these rules are well-defined. Its uniformity in the size of the patch is essential.

Lemma 4.1. (Uniform frame choice) There is a \(\delta_{\text{fr}} > 0\), depending only on the matrix group, for which the minimizer of (4.2) with \(h_0 = 1\) is unique whenever the loop test holds. For every configuration and every fixed coordinate order, at least one root satisfies (4.3) with \(a_{\text{ax}} = 16\). These constants do not depend on \(R\).

Proof. We first record the geometric input. The nearest-point projection \(\pi\) onto the compact smooth submanifold \(G\) is uniquely defined and smooth on a uniform tubular neighborhood [Foo84, p. 153, Lemma]. Its derivative at \(1\) is the orthogonal projection \(\Pi_{\mathfrak{g}}\) onto the Lie algebra. Hence on a sufficiently small convex ball about \(1\),

\(\displaystyle \| \pi (A) - \pi (B) - \Pi_{\mathfrak{g}} (A - B)\|_{\text{F}} \leqslant \tfrac{1}{4} \|A - B\|_{\text{F}} . \) (4.4)

This is the mean-value estimate for \(D \pi - \Pi_{\mathfrak{g}}\), not a new projection theorem.

Fix \(o_0 \in S_4\) and use the axial gauge rooted at the origin with this coordinate order. In this gauge the transport from \(x\) to \(y\) in order \(o\) is \(\mathcal{T}_{xy ; o}^{o_0}\). On the minimizing branch the loop test gives \(\|\mathcal{T}_{xy ; o}^{o_0} - 1\|_{\text{F}} \leqslant d\), where \(d\) can be chosen arbitrarily small by reducing \(\delta_{\text{fr}}\). Reversing a route gives its inverse and permutes the coordinate orders. A minimizer exists by compactness. Common left multiplication leaves the energy invariant, so minimization with the normalization \(h_0 = 1\) also gives an unconstrained minimizer after allowing that common multiplier. Comparison with the constant frame, and the squared triangle inequality, give

\(\displaystyle \frac{1}{|\mathbb{B}_R |^2} \sum_{x, y \in \mathbb{B}_R} \|h_x - h_y \|_{\text{F}}^2 \leqslant 4 d^2 .\)

Multiply all \(h_x\) on the left by one group element so that \(1\) is nearest to their centroid \(\bar{h} = |\mathbb{B}_R |^{- 1} \sum_{x \in \mathbb{B}_R} h_x\). Then \(\| \bar{h} - 1\|_{\text{F}} \leqslant 2 d\). For each fixed \(x\), coordinatewise minimization shows that \(h_x\) is nearest to

\(\displaystyle F_x (h) = \frac{1}{24 |\mathbb{B}_R |} \sum_{y \in \mathbb{B}_R} \sum_{o \in S_4} h_y (\mathcal{T}_{xy ; o}^{o_0})^{- 1} .\)

Including the diagonal term \(y = x\) does not invalidate this assertion: it adds a term whose maximum is attained at the old coordinate \(h_x\). Since \(\|F_x (h) - \bar{h} \|_{\text{F}} \leqslant d\), all feedback matrices lie within \(3 d\) of \(1\). For small enough \(d\) they lie in the projection ball, and \(h_x = \pi (F_x (h))\).

Center two minimizers \(h, k\) in this way and set \(u = \max_x \|h_x - k_x \|_{\text{F}}\). Their feedback differences satisfy

\(\displaystyle \|F_x (h) - F_x (k)\|_{\text{F}} \leqslant u, \qquad \|F_x (h) - F_x (k) - (\bar{h} - \bar{k})\|_{\text{F}} \leqslant du.\)

The two centroids are normal to \(G\) at \(1\), so \(\Pi_{\mathfrak{g}} (\bar{h} - \bar{k}) = 0\). At an index attaining the maximum, (4.4) gives \(u \leqslant (d + 1 / 4) u\). Taking \(d \leqslant 1 / 4\) forces \(u = 0\). Thus minimizers differ only by a common left multiplier, removed by \(h_0 = 1\). Only averages occur in the contraction, which explains why its constants do not grow with the patch.

For the axial alternatives, apply the usual plaquette telescoping argument to a rectangular slice \(X\) with \(r\) varying coordinates. Write \(\Sigma_{\text{link}} (X, U)\) and \(\Sigma_{\text{plaq}} (X, U)\) for the sums of \(\chi\) over its internal positive links and plaquettes, respectively. Choose a layer \(X_0\) perpendicular to the last coordinate whose internal plaquette sum is no greater than the average over layers. Use the axial construction on that layer, then extend it along the last coordinate, setting those links to one. Every remaining link differs from its transported link on the chosen layer by a product of intervening plaquettes. The triangle inequality for the normalized Hilbert–Schmidt distance and conjugation invariance yield, writing \(n = 2 R + 1\),

\(\displaystyle \Sigma_{\text{link}} (X, U^{\text{ax}}) \leqslant n (\Sigma_{\text{link}} (X_0, U^{\text{ax}}) + \Sigma_{\text{plaq}} (X, U)) .\)

Induction with the bound \(4 rn \Sigma_{\text{plaq}} (X, U)\) closes because \(n \Sigma_{\text{plaq}} (X_0, U) \leqslant \Sigma_{\text{plaq}} (X, U)\). The construction follows the prescribed coordinate order; the selected layers specify a root. At \(r = 4\) the bound is (4.3) with constant \(16\).\(\Box\)

Only smoothness of the nearest-point projection was used here. Analytic stationary-field estimates will need more; they are not consequences of this lemma alone.

4.2Gauge-covariant block averaging and the exact kernel

Translate the preceding frame construction to every patch \(Ly +\mathbb{B}_R\) centered at the fine site \(Ly\) corresponding to the coarse site \(y\). For \(z \in \mathbb{B}_R\), let \(h_{y, z}\) be the gauge matrix at \(Ly + z\), normalized by \(h_{y, 0} = 1\). For a coarse edge \(e = (y, \mu)\) define

\(\displaystyle Y_{e, z} = h_{y, z} \left( \prod_{r = 0}^{L - 1} U (Ly + z + re_{\mu}, \mu) \right) h_{y + e_{\mu}, z}^{- 1} . \) (4.5)

Both ends are expressed in the frames of the coarse endpoints, as shown for two offsets in Figure 4.1. In particular, under a fine gauge transformation \(g\), this connector becomes \(g (Ly) Y_{e, z} g (Ly + Le_{\mu})^{- 1}\).

Let \(w^{\text{av}}\) be the \(j\)-fold convolution of the uniform probability on \(\mathbb{B}_{(L - 1) / 2}\). It is nonnegative, has sum one, is invariant under \(H_4\), and is supported in \(\mathbb{B}_{j (L - 1) / 2} \subset \mathbb{B}_R\). Put \(\bar{Y}_e = \sum_{z \in \mathbb{B}_R} w_z^{\text{av}} Y_{e, z}\). The gauge-covariant block average, obtained by projecting the weighted connector average to the group, minimizes

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{P}_e (u) = \sum_z w_z^{\text{av}} w (Y_{e, z} u^{- 1}), \qquad u \in G. \) (4.6)

Since all matrices \(Y_{e, z}\) and \(u\) are unitary,

\(\displaystyle 2 n_G \mathcal{P}_e (u) = \sum_z w_z^{\text{av}} \|Y_{e, z} - \bar{Y}_e \|_{\text{F}}^2 + \| \bar{Y}_e - u\|_{\text{F}}^2 .\)

Thus the minimizers are precisely the nearest group points to \(\bar{Y}_e\). If \(\min_{u \in G} \mathcal{P}_e (u) < \varepsilon_{\text{pred}}\), the identity above gives \(\text{dist}_{\text{F}} (\bar{Y}_e, G) < \sqrt{2 n_G \varepsilon_{\text{pred}}}\). Choose \(\varepsilon_{\text{pred}}\) small enough that \(\bar{Y}_e\) then lies in the tubular neighborhood used in Lemma 4.1. The block average is then unique, independently of the number of connectors or the weights.

On this branch set \(Q_e^{\text{pred}}\) to that minimizer and assign uniform mass to redundant block average labels \(z \in \mathbb{B}_R\). If the test fails, let label \(z\) select \(Q_e^{\text{pred}} = Y_{e, z}\) with probability \(w_z^{\text{av}}\). Equality with either cutoff belongs to the alternative branch; the strict inequalities defining the minimizing branches are part of the convention.

Figure 4.1. Two connectors in the matrix average \(\bar{Y}_e\) for \(e = (y, \mu)\), shown in a coordinate plane. Each solid path traverses \(L\) fine links. The dashed arrows are frame factors, which can depend on the whole patch. They give the framed transport \(Y_{e, z}\) the same coarse endpoints for every offset \(z\).

Collect all frame and block average choices in a finite label \(\alpha\) and denote their joint probability by \(\lambda (U, \alpha)\). Frame choices are made first, followed by conditionally independent block average choices. Thus

\(\displaystyle \lambda (U, \alpha) \geqslant 0, \qquad \sum_{\alpha} \lambda (U, \alpha) = 1. \) (4.7)

The frame lemma supplies an eligible choice whenever it is needed.

Fix the block penalty \(b > 0\).[MH: Apparently \(b\) will be fixed throughout and could just be taken as \(b = 1\). It controls the soft conditioning used here.] For \(\gamma \geqslant 0\), let

\(\displaystyle q_{\gamma} (u) = \frac{e^{- \gamma w (u)}}{\int_G e^{- \gamma w (v)} \hspace{0.17em} dh (v)} .\)

This is a probability density on \(G\) with respect to Haar measure.

For a fixed fine field \(U\) and choice \(\alpha\), the block average \(Q_e = Q_e^{\text{pred}} (U, \alpha)\) represents transport across the coarse edge \(e\). We impose agreement of \(V_e\) with \(Q_e\) by a soft constraint. By unitarity and (1.6), its energy is

\(\displaystyle b \beta w (V_e Q_e^{- 1}) = \frac{b \beta}{2 n_G} \|V_e - Q_e \|_{\text{F}}^2 .\)

This penalizes the discrepancy in a gauge-invariant way, since \(V_e\) and \(Q_e\) transform by the same matrices at their endpoints. At large \(b \beta\) the conditional law thus concentrates near the block average. Given \(U\) and \(\alpha\), we sample the coarse links independently with these normalized densities. Averaging their product over \(\alpha\) gives the conditional density of \(V\) given \(U\); integrating against the fine density gives its coarse marginal.

Definition 4.2. (Exact block map) The superscript \((L)\) denotes the block ratio: on a coarse torus \(\Lambda_s\) the fine torus is \(\Lambda_{Ls}\). The period \(s\), smoothing order \(j\) and penalty \(b\) are understood. For \(\beta \geqslant 0\), define the kernel and its action on fine densities by

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} k^{(L)}_{\beta} (U, V) & = \sum_{\alpha} \lambda (U, \alpha) \prod_{e \text{ coarse}} q_{b \beta} (V_e Q_e^{\text{pred}} (U, \alpha)^{- 1}), \hspace{3cm} \text{(4.8)} \\ (\mathcal{B}^{(L)}_{\beta} f) (V) & = \int k^{(L)}_{\beta} (U, V) f (U) \hspace{0.17em} dU, \hspace{3cm} \text{(4.9)} \end{array}\)

where \(dU\) and \(dV\) denote product probability Haar measure on their respective link spaces.

For fixed \(\beta\) this is a bounded Borel kernel. Indeed, the loop inequalities of Section 4.1 and the axial eligibility inequality (4.3) compare continuous functions of the fine links. Once the frames are chosen, the condition for using the minimizing block average on edge \(e\) is \(\min_{u \in G} \mathcal{P}_e (u) < \varepsilon_{\text{pred}}\). This minimum is continuous in the connector matrices \((Y_{e, z})_z\) by compactness of \(G\). On each minimizing branch the unique minimizer depends continuously on its data. Thus the choices and their probabilities in the finite sum (4.8) are Borel functions of \(U\). Boundedness follows from boundedness of \(q_{b \beta}\) and (4.7).

Proposition 4.3. (Normalization and covariance) The kernel is nonnegative and normalized in the coarse variable:

\(\displaystyle \int k^{(L)}_{\beta} (U, V) \hspace{0.17em} dV = 1. \) (4.10)

It is covariant under fine gauge transformations and the induced coarse gauge transformations \(y \mapsto g (Ly)\). It is also covariant under signed coordinate permutations and corresponding fine/coarse translations. For every integrable fine density \(f\),

\(\displaystyle \int \mathcal{B}^{(L)}_{\beta} f \hspace{0.17em} dV = \int f \hspace{0.17em} dU. \) (4.11)

Proof. For normalization, integrate each coarse-link factor using right invariance of Haar measure, then sum (4.7). Fubini gives (4.11), with absolute integrability following from the same computation applied to \(|f|\). For covariance, gauge transformations conjugate rooted loop tests and transform the endpoints of (4.5); signed lattice isometries permute roots, orders and offsets and invert reversed links. The objective functions and eligibility tests are unchanged. Uniqueness on the minimizing branches, and reindexing of the finite alternatives, then give the stated covariance of the kernel.\(\Box\)

In particular, if \(U\) has normalized density \(f / \int f\), the coarse field has density \(\mathcal{B}^{(L)}_{\beta} f / \int f\). The equality of integrals (4.11) holds even when the coarse action has terms beyond the Wilson plaquette action.

4.3A first-power bound for the actual block average

The later tree comparison needs the block average on both of its branches, including configurations far from the identity. Let \(\mathcal{P}_y (U)\) be the sum of fine plaquette distances from the identity in the lifted macro-box of radius \((j + 7) L\) centered at \(Ly\). Write \(\mathcal{P}_y (U) = \sum_q m_y (q) \chi (U_q)\), counting periodic lifts with their multiplicities. For a coarse plaquette \(p\) based near \(y\), fix the block choices \(\alpha\) and abbreviate \(Q_e^{\text{pred}} (U, \alpha)\) to \(Q_e (U)\). We shall prove

\(\displaystyle \chi (V_p) \leqslant \sum_{e \in \partial p} \chi (V_e Q_e (U)^{- 1}) + C_j L^{- 2} \mathcal{P}_y (U), \qquad \sup_q \sum_y m_y (q) \leqslant K_j . \) (4.12)

The constants are independent of the block ratio and period. The first-power estimate is essential: a square-root consequence of a squared-energy comparison would not give the factor needed here.

Lemma 4.4. (First-power block average comparison) Fix the smoothing order \(j\) and frame eligibility constants. For the block average of (4.6), every set of choices \(\alpha\) with \(\lambda (U, \alpha) > 0\) satisfies (4.12), with \(C_j, K_j\) independent of \(L\) and the period, whenever the coarse period is at least \(100 (j + 8)\).

Proof. We first record the estimate for a minimizing frame on a finite set of \(N\) points. Write \(W_{ij \alpha}\) for its path transports, with reverse paths paired among the labels \(\alpha\). In what follows \(\text{av}\) denotes the uniform average over its subscripts: each point index ranges over the \(N\) points and \(\alpha\) over the path labels. Put \(b_i = \text{av}_{j, \alpha} \hspace{0.17em} \chi (W_{ij \alpha})\) and \(b = \text{av}_i \hspace{0.17em} b_i\). If \(h\) minimizes the averaged squared-distance energy, then some \(c \in G\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \text{av}_i \hspace{0.17em} \chi (h_i c^{- 1}) \leqslant C_G b. \) (4.13)

Here the constant is independent of \(N\) and the number of path labels. To verify this, comparison with the constant frame and \(\chi^2 / 2 \leqslant \chi\) give \(\text{av}_{i, j} \|h_i - h_j \|_{\text{F}}^2 \leqslant C_G b\). Let \(m = \text{av}_j \hspace{0.17em} h_j\) and \(m_i = \text{av}_{j, \alpha} \hspace{0.17em} h_j W_{ij \alpha}^{- 1}\). Varying one frame coordinate shows that \(h_i\) is a nearest group point to \(m_i\), and \(\|m_i - m\|_{\text{F}} \leqslant \sqrt{n_G} b_i\). The diagonal self-term causes no problem: freezing its second endpoint adds a nonnegative squared distance from the identity which is zero at the minimizer.

When \(b\) is sufficiently small, choose a nearest group point \(c\) to \(m\). The mean-square bound puts \(m\) in a fixed tubular neighborhood of \(c\). The smooth nearest-point projection of Lemma 4.1 is uniformly Lipschitz there. For \(b_i\) smaller than a fixed constant it therefore gives \(\chi (h_i c^{- 1}) \leqslant C_G b_i\). For the other indices, \(\chi \leqslant 2 \leqslant C_G b_i\). Averaging proves (4.13). If \(b\) is not small, the same conclusion follows from \(\chi \leqslant 2\) after enlarging \(C_G\).

Fix a box \(X\) of side \(q\) with \(N = q^4\) sites and a coordinate order. Write \(U_{x \to y}\) for transport along the corresponding path from \(x\) to \(y\). Then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{x, y \in X} \chi (U_{x \to y}) \leqslant 4 Nq \sum_{e \text{ in box}} \chi (U_e) .\)

Indeed, choose \(x\) and \(y\) independently and uniformly in \(X\). During the segment in direction \(\mu\), the other three coordinates are uniform on the \(q^3\) transverse sites: each is taken from either \(x\) or \(y\) according to the fixed order. The triangle inequality bounds the distance for this segment by the sum of link distances on its entire coordinate line. Its expectation is thus at most \(q^{- 3} = q / N\) times the sum of link distances in the box. Sum over the four directions and multiply by \(N^2\) to recover the displayed bound. Apply (4.13) after any comparison gauge \(H\). For a minimizing frame \(f\), this gives a constant rotation \(c\) such that \(\sum_i \chi (c^{- 1} f_i H_i^{- 1}) \leqslant C_G qE (H \cdot U)\), where \(E\) is the sum of bond distances from the identity. For an eligible axial fallback frame, apply the same path count to the pure gauge \(fH^{- 1}\), whose bond sum is at most \(E (f \cdot U) + E (H \cdot U)\). The eligibility rule bounds the first term by \(K_{\text{ax}} q\) times the sum of plaquette distances from the identity. Thus both frame branches obey the same bound with this additional term.

Choose an axial gauge \(H\) on the macro-box of radius \((j + 7) L\). The axial estimate in Lemma 4.1 gives \(E (H \cdot U) \leqslant C_j L\mathcal{P}_y (U)\). On each translated frame patch, centred at \(Lz\), let \(h_{z, i}\) be the patch gauge at offset \(i\) as in Section 4.2. Its side is \(O_j (L)\), so the preceding bound gives a constant \(c_z \in G\) with

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i \in \mathbb{B}_R} \chi (c_z^{- 1} h_{z, i} H_{Lz + i}^{- 1}) \leqslant C_j L^2 \mathcal{P}_y (U) .\)

The convolution weights obey \(w_i^{\text{av}} \leqslant L^{- 4}\): condition on all but the first of the \(j\) uniform points. For a fixed sum there is at most one possible first point. The two endpoint errors in an averaged connector consequently cost at most \(C_j L^{- 2} \mathcal{P}_y (U)\). For the middle straight path, each of its \(L\) translations is injective, so its unweighted sum is at most \(LE (H \cdot U)\). Its weighted sum has the same bound \(C_j L^{- 2} \mathcal{P}_y (U)\).

Fix a coarse edge \(e = (z, \mu)\) and write \(Q = Q_e (U)\) and \(Y_i = Y_{e, i}\) for its selected block average and its connector at offset \(i\). For every \(u_{\ast} \in G\),

\(\displaystyle \chi (Qu_{\ast}^{- 1}) \leqslant 2 \max \{1, \varepsilon_{\text{pred}}^{- 1} \} \sum_i w_i^{\text{av}} \chi (Y_i u_{\ast}^{- 1}) .\)

On the minimizing branch this follows by comparison of the nearest group point to the matrix centroid with \(u_{\ast}\). On the fallback branch, the minimum cost is at least \(\varepsilon_{\text{pred}}\), whereas the cost at \(u_{\ast}\) is at most the displayed weighted first moment, and \(\chi \leqslant 2\). This holds for every selected fallback connector, however small its probability. Use \(u_{\ast} = c_z c_{z + e_{\mu}}^{- 1}\). The four rotations cancel around a coarse plaquette, proving its block average curvature bound. The triangle inequality bounds the resulting change in plaquette distance by the sum of the four link distances \(\chi (V_e Q_e (U)^{- 1})\). Finally, a fixed fine plaquette belongs to only \(O_j (1)\) macro-boxes centered at points of \(L\mathbb{Z}^4\); count its periodic lifts with multiplicity. This proves the second bound in (4.12) as well.\(\Box\)

4.4Small fields and density coordinates

The cutoff used to distinguish small from large fields must remain fixed while the actual coupling is varied for tuning. To express that distinction, let \(\bar{\beta} > 1\) and use the reference values

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rlll} \hat{\beta}_m & = \bar{\beta} + \kappa_G \tau_m, & M_m^{\text{cut}} & = \exp ((\log \log \hat{\beta}_m)^2), \hspace{3cm} \text{(4.14)}\\ t_m & = \hat{\beta}_m^{- 1 / 2} M_m^{\text{cut}}, & B_m & = \lceil (\log \hat{\beta}_m)^2 \rceil . \hspace{3cm} \text{(4.15)} \end{array}\)

Here \(\tau_m = \log D_m\) is the logarithmic scale from Definition 1.3. We use these formulas only where \(0 < t_m \leqslant 1\). The actual coupling ranges in \(\mathfrak{B}_m = [\hat{\beta}_m / 2, 2 \hat{\beta}_m]\). The reference value and threshold are not recomputed when that coupling changes. Their eventual compatibility with the scale schedule is a later choice.

At the present scale write \(\Lambda = \Lambda_{R_p D_m}\), and let \(\text{Bad}_m (U)\) be the sites incident to at least one plaquette with \(\chi (U_{\square}) > t_m\). A set \(X\) is small-field if \(X \cap \text{Bad}_m (U) = \varnothing\); write its indicator as \(h_X (U)\). Thus every plaquette meeting \(X\) must have small distance from the identity. This definition is gauge invariant and involves a closed condition on small-field sites.

We keep two kinds of local corrections.

Definition 4.5. (Regular and large-field activities) A regular activity consists of a support \(X_i\), an anchor \(o_i \in X_i\), and a real Borel gauge-invariant function \(E_i^{\text{reg}}\) local on \(X_i\). It vanishes at the flat configuration and has the required number of ordered left derivatives on small-field configurations.

A large-field activity has an exclusion support \(Y_i\), a nonempty prescribed set \(I_i^{\text{bad}} \subset Y_i\), and an additional Borel gauge-invariant eligibility test \(\mathcal{E}_i^{\text{extra}}\). Its real Borel value \(K_i^{\text{lf}}\) depends on links internal to \(Y_i\) and on the indicators of large-field sites in \(Y_i\), and vanishes outside

\(\displaystyle \{U : \text{Bad}_m (U) \cap Y_i = I_i^{\text{bad}} \} \cap \mathcal{E}_i^{\text{extra}} . \) (4.16)

The extra test reads the same links and large-field indicators.

Neither activity is assumed nonnegative. Activities may be indexed by a countable set; their summability will be part of the norm conditions.

There are two different meanings of support in this definition. The set \(Y_i\) determines exclusion and coverage: selected \(Y_i\) will be disjoint. But deciding whether a site belongs to \(\text{Bad}_m (U)\) reads plaquettes meeting that site, whose links can extend beyond \(Y_i\). The full dependence region includes those links as well as the internal links and any links read by other tests. Two disjoint exclusion supports can have overlapping full dependence regions. A connected expansion based on dependence must use the latter, whereas the exact-cover sum below uses the former. Disjoint exclusion supports do not imply probabilistic independence.

4.5The prescribed leading action

The running coupling is a coordinate relative to a prescribed leading action. Its construction will come from Gaussian integration and local matching; here we specify the form of that coordinate without claiming the estimates needed to select it.

At scale \(m\), fix constants \(c_{\text{dir}}, c_{\text{lin}}, R^{\text{rel}} > 0\) with \(16 c_{\text{lin}} < c_{\text{dir}}\). Define

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \text{clip}_t (r) & = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} c_{\text{dir}} r^2 / 2, & r \leqslant t,\\ c_{\text{lin}} tr, & r > t, \end{array} \right. \hspace{3cm} \text{(4.17)} \\ \chi^{\text{rel}}_{m, x} (U) & =\boldsymbol{1} \{ \chi (U_{y ; \mu \nu}) \leqslant t_m e^{\text{dist} (x, y) / R^{\text{rel}}} \text{ for every } y, \mu < \nu \} . \hspace{3cm} \text{(4.18)} \end{array}\)

The distance is periodic and measured in lattice units. Although written as a test on every plaquette, (4.18) reads only a finite neighborhood: \(\chi \leqslant 2\) makes all tests with \(t_m e^{\text{dist} (x, y) / R^{\text{rel}}} \geqslant 2\) automatic. Any claim of locality must include the remaining tested plaquettes in its dependence region.

For each orientation \(\mathfrak{p}= (\mu, \nu)\), fix a summable linear combination of transported curvatures,

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{L}^{\text{path}}_{\mathfrak{p}} (U, x) = \sum_i r_{\mathfrak{p}i} \mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{p}i} (U, x) .\)

Each \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{p}i}\) is a plaquette curvature transported to \(x\), as in (2.8). Also specify local real invariant multilinear expressions \(A_i^{(q)} (U, x)\), \(q = 2, 3, 4\), in such curvatures, each multiplied by the small-field indicator of its declared footprint. The footprint contains the paths, plaquettes and anchor; its event indicator may read additional adjacent plaquettes. Assume the coefficient sums converge absolutely at the fixed finite volume. Set

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} P^{\text{raw}}_{N, m} (\beta, U) & = \beta \sum_{x, \mathfrak{p}} \left[ \text{clip}_{t_m} (\chi (U_{x ; \mathfrak{p}})) + \tfrac{1}{2} \chi^{\text{rel}}_{m, x} (U) |\mathcal{L}^{\text{path}}_{\mathfrak{p}} (U, x) |^2 \right]\\ & \quad + \beta \sum_{x, i} (A_i^{(3)} + A_i^{(4)}) (U, x) + \sum_{x, i} A_i^{(2)} (U, x), \hspace{3cm} \text{(4.19)}\\ P^{\text{path}}_{N, m} (\beta, U) & = \frac{1}{|H_4 |} \sum_{h \in H_4} P^{\text{raw}}_{N, m} (\beta, h^{\ast} U) . \hspace{3cm} \text{(4.20)} \end{array}\)

The coefficients are held fixed as \(\beta\) varies. Thus this action is affine in \(\beta\). Its ultimate normalization requires the quadratic coefficient in the \(\beta\)-dependent part to be the curvature square, and no such coefficient in the separate quadratic correction. Those are matching conditions, not consequences of the displayed form.

The function called clip in (4.17) is not a smooth cutoff. At \(r = t\) it drops from \(c_{\text{dir}} t^2 / 2\) to \(c_{\text{lin}} t^2\) immediately to the right. Effective density coordinates are therefore Borel functions, not globally differentiable functions of the links. Derivative estimates refer to the regular expressions on small-field domains with the event indicators held fixed. In particular, a derivative with respect to the actual coupling does not differentiate the threshold or its indicator.

4.6The exact-cover representation and its norms

Recall that \(Y_i\) is an exclusion support and \(I_i^{\text{bad}}\) its prescribed set of large-field sites, as in Definition 4.5. Let \(\mathfrak{F}_m (U)\) be the finite subsets \(\mathfrak{a}\) of large-field activity indices such that the \(Y_i\), \(i \in \mathfrak{a}\), are pairwise disjoint and \(\bigcup_{i \in \mathfrak{a}} I_i^{\text{bad}} = \text{Bad}_m (U)\). These are exact covers of the large-field sites. They do not select a preferred partition into connected components.

A density coordinate tuple \(\rho\) consists of \(\beta\), a real scalar \(v_{\text{sc}}\) per site, the regular activities and the large-field activities, with the fixed action (4.20). Define

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} V_{\rho} (U) & = P^{\text{path}}_{N, m} (\beta, U) + \sum_i h_{X_i} (U) E_i^{\text{reg}} (U) - v_{\text{sc}} | \Lambda |,\\ \text{dens} (\rho) (U) & = e^{- V_{\rho} (U)} \sum_{\mathfrak{a} \in \mathfrak{F}_m (U)} \prod_{i \in \mathfrak{a}} K_i^{\text{lf}} (U) . \hspace{3cm} \text{(4.21)} \end{array}\)

The empty product is one. Because each \(I_i^{\text{bad}}\) is nonempty, if there are no large-field sites the only exact cover is empty and the sum is one. Otherwise a single support may cover two regions, or two disjoint supports may cover them separately; both alternatives belong in the sum. A tuple satisfying the definitions alone need not give a positive density. Positivity follows when a later construction proves that its density equals an exact block integral of a positive input.

Fix an integer derivative order \(q \geqslant 2\), a nonnegative spatial exponent \(\varpi\), a support-weight exponent \(\sigma > 0\), and the threshold \(t_m\). For a regular activity anchored at \(o_i\), use the seminorm

\(\displaystyle \|E_i^{\text{reg}} \|_m = \sup_{0 \leqslant k \leqslant q} \sup_{U, A_1, \ldots, A_k} |D_{A_1}^L \cdots D_{A_k}^L E_i^{\text{reg}} (U) |, \) (4.22)

where \(U\) ranges over configurations that are small-field on \(X_i\). Each \(A_r\) is a Lie algebra valued link field satisfying, on each link \(e\) with initial site \(e^-\), \(| (A_r)_e |_{\mathfrak{g}} \leqslant t_m (1 + \text{dist} (e^-, o_i))^{\varpi}\). The left derivatives are those of Section 2.4. Required regularity includes existence and uniform bounds at the boundary of the small-field domain, interpreted through the regular local expression; no derivative of \(h_{X_i}\) is included.

For a set \(X \subset \Lambda\), let \(\text{cov}_{B_m} (X)\) be the minimum number of radius-\(B_m\) periodic balls whose union is connected in the nearest-neighbor graph and covers \(X\). Set \(\text{cov}_{B_m} (\varnothing) = 0\). The covering union may contain additional sites. Its exponential weight penalizes extended supports without requiring a support itself to be connected. Choose majorants \(e_i \geqslant \|E_i^{\text{reg}} \|_m\) and \(k_i \geqslant \sup_U |K_i^{\text{lf}} (U) |\), and define

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} \mathcal{E}_m (\rho) & = \sup_{x \in \Lambda} \sum_{i : o_i = x} e^{\sigma \text{cov}_{B_m} (X_i)} e_i,\\ \mathcal{K}_m (\rho) & = \sup_{x \in \Lambda} \sum_{i : x \in Y_i} e^{\sigma \text{cov}_{B_m} (Y_i)} k_i . \end{aligned} \ \) (4.23)

The first is an anchored sum: each regular activity is counted at one site. The second counts a large-field activity at every site of its exclusion support. Replacing an anchored sum by a containing-site sum requires a bound for the number of possible anchors; it is not a harmless notational change. Likewise, replacing \(Y_i\) by its full dependence region changes the weight and requires a geometric estimate.

Finite weighted sums ensure that (4.21) is well-defined at fixed volume. Indeed

\(\displaystyle \sum_i \|E_i^{\text{reg}} \|_m \leqslant | \Lambda | \mathcal{E}_m, \qquad \sum_i \sup |K_i^{\text{lf}} | \leqslant | \Lambda | \mathcal{K}_m,\)

and the absolute sum over all finite selections is at most \(\prod_i (1 + k_i) \leqslant \exp (| \Lambda |\mathcal{K}_m)\). Restricting to disjoint exact covers only lowers this bound. The estimate is a finite-volume convergence statement, not a uniform bound on the entire partition function.

4.7Conditions for a one-step induction

The preceding coordinates describe possible densities; their smallness conditions will describe the inputs of a one-step theorem. There are additional normalization conditions. On a nonwrapping support, choose centered coordinate lifts \(\xi_{o_i} (y)\). For every pair of affine link fields of the form

\(\displaystyle A_{(y, \mu)} = u_{\mu} + \sum_{\nu} \xi_{o_i} (y)^{\nu} v_{\mu \nu}, \qquad u_{\mu}, v_{\mu \nu} \in \mathfrak{g},\)

require the Hessian at zero of \(A \mapsto E_i^{\text{reg}} (\exp A)\) to vanish on that pair. Together with \(E_i^{\text{reg}} (1) = 0\) and the vanishing linear term, this excludes the low-order local contributions assigned to the leading action. The first derivative vanishes for a gauge-invariant activity by invariance under constant gauge transformations: each linear coefficient would otherwise be an invariant linear functional on the simple Lie algebra. The meaning of the affine Hessian condition is fixed in the chosen local coordinates; no coordinate lift is imposed on a wrapping support by this definition.

One may prescribe the intended size bounds

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{E}_m \leqslant \hat{\beta}_m^{- 11 / 10}, \qquad \mathcal{K}_m \leqslant \exp (- (M_m^{\text{cut}})^{1 / 5}), \) (4.24)

with the reference quantities of (4.15). In an induction, \(q, \varpi, \sigma\) may depend on a regularity-stage index \(J_m\); that index is different from the scale \(m\). For now they remain specified input parameters. Neither (4.24) nor the density identity proves that the block map preserves the class.

Parameter continuity is also stated at fixed coordinates. A curve \(u \mapsto \rho_u\) has fixed supports, anchors, prescribed large-field sets, extra tests and event indicators. Its coupling and scalar are continuous. At each \(u_0\), differences of regular derivatives and large-field values have majorants \(e_i (u), k_i (u)\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_i e^{\sigma \text{cov}_{B_m} (X_i)} e_i (u) + \sum_i e^{\sigma \text{cov}_{B_m} (Y_i)} k_i (u) \longrightarrow 0 \quad (u \to u_0) .\)

Both sums here are over all indices, at a fixed finite volume. This continuity condition is strong enough to control the density and its integral; it differentiates no moving large-field boundary.

The analytic task remaining is now precise. Starting with a tuple at the fine level, one must construct a coarse tuple whose density is exactly \(\mathcal{B}^{(L_m)}_{\beta} \text{dens} (\rho)\), whose coupling has the claimed adjustment, and whose activities obey the coarse conditions. A class defined as the outputs reachable from a Wilson starting density can only be introduced after that construction. It must not be confused with the much larger set of coordinate tuples satisfying the displayed norm bounds.
