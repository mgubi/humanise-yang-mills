Chapter 7
Local matching and the one-loop coefficient

A renormalization step has two distinct normalization tasks. Its classical stationary action must retain the prescribed quadratic form and agree with a gauge-invariant local action through degree four. Its Gaussian determinant changes the coefficient of that quadratic form. The first task is a local Taylor construction; the second requires a comparison of determinants uniform in the block ratio. This chapter separates the two, so that a perturbative coefficient does not stand in for an estimate on the actual block integral.

Fix one step \(m + 1 \to m\), an odd block ratio \(L = L_m\), and the Lie algebra metric of Chapter 1. Momentum is measured in lattice units at the indicated level. The adjoint Casimir \(C_G\) is defined by \(\text{tr}_{\mathfrak{g}} (\text{ad} \hspace{0.17em} X \text{ad} \hspace{0.17em} Y) = - C_G \langle X, Y \rangle\); set

\(\displaystyle \kappa_G = \frac{11 C_G}{24 \pi^2} .\)

The group is compact with simple nonabelian Lie algebra. Constants may depend on fixed stage bounds, but constants described as uniform in \(L\) are chosen before \(L\). All local Taylor expansions below concern the analytic action near the flat field, where small-field indicators are constant. They do not differentiate a cutoff boundary.

7.1Common Taylor kernels and Ward identities

Write \(C_n (E) = D^n E (0) / n\)! for the normalized symmetric Taylor coefficient of an action \(E\). A translation-invariant coefficient is specified by its values on plane waves. After division by the number of lattice sites, its symbol is a tensor \(f (p_1, \ldots, p_{n - 1})\), with \(p_0 = - \sum_{i > 0} p_i\). The tensor has one direction and one colour index for each field. The symbol is periodic with period \(2 \pi\) in each independent momentum. A common symbol means one function chosen on the infinite lattice whose restriction to each finite momentum grid describes the corresponding torus coefficient. This requirement is stronger than a separate smooth extension of each finite grid.

For \(\sigma > 0\), write \(|f|_{\sigma} \leqslant A\) when every component is holomorphic and bounded by \(A\) on the strip \(| \text{Im} \hspace{0.17em} p_i^a | < \sigma\). There are only finitely many direction and colour components at fixed \(n\). Reducing the strip gives an exponentially weighted sum of Fourier coefficients, with a constant depending on \(n, \sigma, G\). Thus common strip bounds imply locality uniformly in the period. An order-\(r\) zero means that all momentum Taylor coefficients of total degree below \(r\) vanish.

Let \(q_a (p) = e^{ip_a} - 1\) and \(d_1 (p) v = q (p) \wedge v\) be the lattice curl. Suppose an analytic gauge-invariant difference \(E\) has vanishing Taylor coefficients below degree \(n\). Its infinitesimal gauge identity at that degree contains only the linear gauge variation \(d_0 \omega\): the nonlinear part multiplies lower coefficients, which are zero. Consequently its first nonzero symbol satisfies

\(\displaystyle \delta_i f = 0 \quad (0 \leqslant i < n), \qquad \delta_i = \iota_{q (p_i)} .\) (7.1)

Contraction is algebraic, without complex conjugation; it acts only on the direction index of the indicated leg. Reality means \(\overline{f (- p)} = f (p)\) on real momenta. Constant gauge transformations make its colour tensor adjoint invariant. A Ward identity verified on all finite momentum grids passes to the common continuous symbol by density. This last step requires a common kernel.

7.2Division by the lattice curl

The algebraic Poincaré lemma explains why gauge-invariant leading coefficients are built from curvature; see [BBH00, Section 4]. Its local continuum form does not give the periodic strip bounds needed here. We give the quantitative adaptation, including the constraint on the sum of momenta.

Lemma 7.1. (Quantitative curvature representation) Let \(f\) have \(n\) one-form legs, satisfy (7.1), and obey \(|f|_{\sigma} \leqslant A\). For \(n \geqslant 3\) there are \(\eta > 0, K < \infty\), depending only on \(n, \sigma\), and a periodic holomorphic tensor \(g\) with two-form legs such that

\(\displaystyle f = \delta_0 \cdots \delta_{n - 1} g, \qquad |g|_{\eta} \leqslant KA.\)

For \(n = 2\) the same assertion holds if \(f\) has order \(r + 2\), \(r \geqslant 0\); then \(g\) can be chosen with order \(r\). Reality and invariant colour coefficients can be imposed with a fixed additional norm factor.

Proof. The elementary division is

\(\displaystyle D_a f (p) = \frac{f (p) - f (p|_{p_a = 0})}{e^{ip_a} - 1} .\)

It is holomorphic at the apparent zeros. On a Fourier monomial the quotient is a geometric sum with \(|z_a |\) terms and no frequency larger than \(|z_a |\). A smaller strip absorbs this polynomial factor into the exponential Fourier decay. Hence \(D_a\) is bounded from a fixed strip to a smaller one and loses at most one Taylor order.

For a cycle \(\delta f = 0\) in one exterior factor, write \(f = e_a \wedge u + v\), with \(u, v\) containing no \(e_a\). Then \(\delta' v = - q_a u\), and direct substitution gives

\(\displaystyle f = \delta (e_a \wedge D_a v) + v|_{p_a = 0} .\)

Repeat over the four axes. At positive exterior degree the last remainder has no possible component and is zero. At degree zero the last remainder is \(f (0)\), so that case is solvable precisely when \(f (0) = 0\). The construction preserves auxiliary constraints independent of the momentum being divided. It raises the exterior degree by one, loses at most one Taylor order, and has a bounded smaller-strip norm.

Only the dependent momentum \(p_0\) needs extra care. The following induction supplies it. With \(t\) independent momenta and exterior degrees \((d, 1, \ldots, 1)\), a cycle in every factor has a zeroth-factor primitive preserving the other cycles whenever \(d + 2 t > 4\). For \(t = 0\) its degree exceeds four and it is zero. For \(t > 0\), replace \(p_1\) by \(p_0\) as independent variable and apply the preceding division. This invertible integral shear only reduces the strip by a fixed factor. It gives \(\delta_0 X = f\), preserving the cycles in factors \(2, \ldots, t\). Apply another zeroth-factor division to \(\delta_1 X\), obtaining \(\delta_0 Y = \delta_1 X\). At \(p_1 = 0\) the tensor \(Y_0\) is a cycle with zeroth degree \(d + 2\) and \(t - 1\) independent momenta. Induction gives \(\delta_0 Z = Y_0\). Extend \(Z\) independently of \(p_1\) and set \(Y' = Y - \delta_0 Z\). It vanishes at \(p_1 = 0\), so scalar division in factor one gives \(\delta_1 D = Y'\). The corrected primitive \(X - \delta_0 D\) has contraction \(f\) in factor zero and zero in factor one. All other cycles were retained. The invariant quantity \(d + 2 t\) makes this a terminating induction.

Initially \(d = 1, t = n - 1\), so the induction applies for \(n \geqslant 3\). After the zeroth-factor lift, divide the independent legs successively. For \(n = 2\), start instead with an order-\(r + 2\) tensor. The first lift has order \(r + 1\); contraction in factor one restores one order, and the next lift \(Y\) has order \(r + 1\). Thus \(Y (0) = 0\) without induction. Its scalar lift has order \(r\), the corrected primitive has order \(r + 1\), and the last division leaves order \(r\). All operations are bounded on a common smaller strip and their number is fixed.

Finally replace \(g\) by \((g (p) + \overline{g (- p)}) / 2\) and project each colour coefficient onto the invariant real tensors. Contraction acts on direction indices and commutes with this finite-dimensional projection. These operations preserve the reconstruction and all retained orders, with a fixed norm factor.\(\Box\)

Choose coordinate paths from the origin to the Fourier displacements \(z_1, \ldots, z_{n - 1}\). A curvature insertion transported along such a path is

\(\displaystyle F_{\gamma, ab} (U, x) = \text{Ad} (U_{x + \gamma}) \text{proj}_{\mathfrak{g}} \frac{U_{x + z ; ab} - U_{x + z ; ab}^{- 1}}{2}, \qquad \gamma : 0 \longrightarrow z.\) (7.2)

All factors transform at the common anchor \(x\), so an invariant multilinear colour contraction is gauge invariant. Its length is the sum of the path lengths plus four per plaquette. The linearization of each factor is the corresponding curl. The Fourier coefficients of \(g\) therefore produce an exact leading Taylor coefficient \(f\). Symmetrizing the legs implements the convention \(C_n = D^n / n\)!; it introduces no extra factorial.

If \(a\) denotes such a multilinear term and \(|a|\) the sum of absolute colour coefficients, the result is

\(\displaystyle \sum_a |a| e^{w \text{length} (a)} \leqslant K_w A \qquad (0 < w < \eta) .\) (7.3)

Averaging over signed coordinate symmetries preserves matching and this bound. Absolute convergence permits periodization on every torus. For the two-leg case with \(r = 2\), the curvature coefficients \(c (z)\) obey

\(\displaystyle \sum_z c (z) = 0, \qquad \sum_z z_a c (z) = 0 \quad (1 \leqslant a \leqslant 4) .\) (7.4)

These are the value and first derivatives of its symbol at zero. They hold separately for each orientation and colour pair.

7.3The marginal quadratic coefficient

A quadratic Taylor symbol includes the factor \(1 / 2\)!. For its transverse entry, define

\(\displaystyle \alpha (f) = 2 \text{Re} [t^2] f_{22}^{uu} (te_1),\) (7.5)

where \(u\) is any unit colour basis vector. The kinetic symbol \(b_H\) of \(J\) has quadratic homogeneous term

\(\displaystyle (b_H)_{ab}^{uv, [2]} (p) = \tfrac{1}{2} (|p|^2 \delta_{ab} - p_a p_b) \delta_{uv},\)

so \(\alpha (b_H) = 1\). The factor two in (7.5) is therefore fixed by the Taylor convention, not by a choice of coupling scheme.

Lemma 7.2. (Removal of the kinetic coefficient) An analytic quadratic symbol from an invariant real action, with exchange symmetry and signed-coordinate symmetry, has \(f - \alpha (f) b_H = O (|p|^4)\). Its coefficient \(\alpha\) is independent of the transverse axis pair and the chosen unit colour.

Proof. The Ward identity first kills the constant term. Its linear term is also zero: inversion of all coordinates acts positively on the two one-form indices and negatively on a linear momentum polynomial. Edge translation phases have constant term one; their higher terms multiply the already vanishing constant coefficient.

At quadratic order the colour-symmetric diagonal forms are scalar multiples of the invariant metric. A self-adjoint operator commuting with the adjoint action has invariant eigenspaces, which are ideals; simplicity leaves only one eigenvalue. Coordinate reflections allow only \(p_c^2\) in a diagonal direction entry and \(p_a p_b\) in an \(a, b\) off-diagonal entry. Permutation symmetry identifies their coefficients. The transverse Ward equations then force the tensor to be a scalar multiple of \(|p|^2 I - pp^{\textsf{T}}\). One transverse test determines that scalar. Subtracting the actual kinetic symbol preserves all symmetries, not merely their leading parts. Its residual cubic coefficient vanishes by inversion, since all lower coefficients now vanish. Thus the residual has order four.\(\Box\)

Apply the two-leg curvature representation to this residual. The resulting quadratic curvature terms have the zero moments (7.4). They cannot change the coupling: their curvature symbol is \(O (|p|^2)\) and their link symbol is \(O (|p|^4)\). The coefficient of curvature square is carried entirely by \(\beta\). This statement refers to Taylor coefficients at the flat field; it does not identify a nonlinear effective action with its quadratic truncation.

7.4Haar measure and gauge completion

The Gaussian contribution at a stationary fine configuration \(U_v\) is

\(\displaystyle T (v) =\mathcal{L}_{m + 1} (U_v) + \tfrac{1}{2} \log \det Q (v) - \log J_{\text{Haar}} (v) .\) (7.6)

Here \(Q\) is the fluctuation Hessian without its factor \(\beta\), \(J_{\text{Haar}}\) is the density of Haar measure in the chosen chart, and \(\mathcal{L}_{m + 1}\) is the inherited quadratic loop action. Field-independent constants may be subtracted throughout. This does not alter any Taylor coefficient of positive degree.

The following finite-dimensional identity accounts for the ghost factor. Let \(\textsf{J}\) be the joint Hessian in left tangent coordinates, \(\mathcal{R}\) the infinitesimal gauge insertion, and \(\mathcal{I}\) a slice embedding. Assume

\(\displaystyle \textsf{J} \mathcal{R}= 0, \quad | \det (\mathcal{R}, \mathcal{I}) | = 1, \quad Q = (Ds)^{\ast} \mathcal{I}^{\ast} \textsf{J} \mathcal{I} \hspace{0.17em} Ds, \quad J_{\text{Haar}} = | \det Ds| .\)

These are identities at the stationary configuration. Given a gauge condition \(F\) with invertible \(G = F\mathcal{R}\), put \(C = \textsf{J} + F^{\ast} F\). Block triangular factorization in the basis \((\mathcal{R}, \mathcal{I})\) gives

\(\displaystyle \det C = (\det G)^2 \det (\mathcal{I}^{\ast} \textsf{J} \mathcal{I}), \qquad \tfrac{1}{2} \log \det Q - \log J_{\text{Haar}} = \tfrac{1}{2} \log \det C - \log | \det G| .\)

The fluctuation form is positive on the slice. With a positive regulated ghost operator the absolute value can be dropped. This is the finite-dimensional Faddeev–Popov determinant identity; the moving Haar Jacobian is part of it. Omitting that Jacobian would change the quantity being compared. Background-field perturbation theory uses exactly this vector-minus-ghost combination [Abb82].

For the lattice comparison choose \(\mu = L^{- 1}\) and

\(\displaystyle P_{\mu} = \mu^2 (\Delta_U + \mu^2 I)^{- 1}, \qquad m_{\mu} = \mu^2 P_{\mu}^9, \qquad \Delta_U = d_U^{\ast} d_U .\)

If \(X\mathcal{R}= I\) is the derivative of the gauge frame, take \(F = d_U^{\ast} P_f + m_{\mu} X\); then \(G = \Delta_U + m_{\mu}\). The auxiliary fine operator is \(A_{\mu} = A + d_U d_U^{\ast} + M_{\mu}\), where \(A\) is the fine kinetic Hessian and \(M_{\mu}\) applies \(m_{\mu}\) to each one-form component. Its determinant response is \(\Pi_{\mu} = \frac{1}{2} \log \det A_{\mu} - \log \det G\). The same ghost appears in both expressions and cancels in their difference.

7.5The auxiliary lattice determinant

Perturbative matching for the Wilson lattice action is treated in Lüscher–Weisz [LW95]; our comparison additionally controls the selected non-Wilson path action and its block average. The continuum coefficient is the classical calculation of Gross–Wilczek and Politzer [GW73, Pol73]. We use the background-field formulation [Abb82]; the following normalization check and integrable error criterion are the parts needed for the lattice application. Recall that \(\alpha\) includes the factor two converting a normalized quadratic Taylor coefficient into the coefficient of \(\frac{1}{2} \langle d_1 v, d_1 v \rangle\).

For external momentum \(te_1\), polarization \(e_2\), and unit colour \(u\), the leading ghost vertex is \(- 2 ik_2 \text{ad} \hspace{0.17em} u\). The vector vertex has four copies of this orbital term and the spin term \(2 it (E_{21} - E_{12}) \text{ad} \hspace{0.17em} u\). The orbital–spin trace is zero. With vector and ghost bubble weights \(- 1 / 4\) and \(+ 1 / 2\), respectively, and \(\text{tr} (\text{ad} \hspace{0.17em} u)^2 = - C_G\), the coefficient extracted by (7.5) has homogeneous integrand

\(\displaystyle h (k) = 4 C_G \left( \frac{k_2^2}{|k|^6} - \frac{4 k_1^2 k_2^2}{|k|^8} - \frac{1}{|k|^4} \right) .\) (7.7)

This is the standard background-field bubble calculation, with its colour normalization expressed in our metric. It is not a claim that the full lattice integrand equals \(h\).

Proposition 7.3. (The lattice integrand remainder) Let the incoming analytic path action have a fixed positive exponential coefficient bound, a uniformly positive curvature metric, and kinetic normalization \(H (0) = I\). Form its actual left-coordinate Hessian, complete it by \(d_U d_U^{\ast}\), and use the covariant regulator \(m_{\mu}\) defined above. The auxiliary quadratic coefficient has an integrand \(I_{\mu}\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \alpha (\Pi_{\mu}) & = (2 \pi)^{- 4} \int_{[- \pi, \pi]^4} \text{Re} \hspace{0.17em} I_{\mu} (k) \hspace{0.17em} dk,\\ |I_{\mu} (k) | & \leqslant A (|k| + \mu)^{- 4},\\ |I_{\mu} (k) - h (k) | & \leqslant D (|k|^{- 3} + \mu^2 |k|^{- 6}) \quad (\mu \leqslant |k| \leqslant 1) . \end{array}\)

The constants depend on the fixed path bounds, metric floor and group, but not on \(\mu \in (0, 1]\) or the torus period. The finite-torus Hessian is the corresponding momentum mesh average, with an exponentially small per-site error relative to the integral at each fixed \(\mu\).

Proof. We specify the derivatives used in the comparison. In one vector or ghost channel let \(a (k)\) be the zero-background denominator, \(b (p ; k)\) its first background insertion, and \(c (p, p' ; k)\) its second insertion. These are actual derivatives of the same operator; the internal momentum of \(b (p ; k)\) changes from \(k\) to \(k + p\). Their contribution to the logarithmic Hessian is

\(\displaystyle \text{tr} \{a (k)^{- 1} c (- p, p ; k) - a (k)^{- 1} b (- p ; k + p) a (k + p)^{- 1} b (p ; k)\} .\) (7.8)

Take half the vector expression minus the ghost expression and apply the normalized external coefficient functional. This defines \(I_{\mu}\). Finite Fourier inversion gives the identical formula with the internal integral replaced by the mesh mean. Thus the integrand is associated with the actual fine action, rather than with a separately chosen continuum model.

We first check its local Taylor data. A transported plaquette curvature has, under the formal scaling \(A (x) = \epsilon a (\epsilon x)\), leading term \(\epsilon^2 (da + [a, a])\) in the corresponding orientation. Its remaining terms have weight at least three; a Taylor derivative or a factor of \(A\) counts as one weight. This follows by expanding the four edge exponentials and their transports. At weight four the row squares and direct term therefore have colour-direction coefficient \(c_{\text{dir}} I + \hat{\rho} (0)^{\ast} \hat{\rho} (0) = I\). Cubic and quartic curvature terms have weights at least six and eight. The leading local density is consequently the Wilson curvature-square density. All differences from it have weight at least five. Exponential path summability justifies these expansions term by term and bounds their Taylor remainders: each derivative costs a polynomial in path length, absorbed by a smaller width.

The Hessian in left increments is obtained from \(S (\log (e^{x_+} e^{x_-}))\) by two \(x_+\) derivatives. The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff correction starts with two fields and hence raises the weight relative to its linear term. Accordingly, after two internal and \(n\) background derivatives, the difference from the Wilson response has momentum order at least \(3 - n\), for \(0 \leqslant n \leqslant 2\). This is a statement about the combined momenta of all legs. It gives uniform compact derivative bounds as well as the indicated orders.

In particular the completed vector denominator is

\(\displaystyle a_{\text{loc}} (k) = d_1 (k)^{\ast} H (k) d_1 (k) + d_0 (k) d_0 (k)^{\ast} = |k|^2 I + O (|k|^3) .\)

The ghost denominator is \(\omega (k) I\), with \(\omega (k) = \sum_a |q_a (k) |^2 = |k|^2 + O (|k|^4)\). For a mark of direction \(a\) and colour \(u\), expanding the Wilson cubic vertex and adding the gauge-fixing vertex gives the linear vector insertion

\(\displaystyle b_{\text{lin}} (p ; k)_{ij} = [- i (2 k_a + p_a) \delta_{ij} + 2 ip_j \delta_{ia} - 2 ip_i \delta_{ja}] \text{ad} \hspace{0.17em} u.\)

The ghost linear insertion is \(- i (2 k_a + p_a) \text{ad} \hspace{0.17em} u\). These expressions can also be read from the continuum quadratic operator in background gauge. The actual lattice remainder in each is \(O ((|k| + |p|)^2)\), by the weight comparison just proved. The second insertion and its first two external momentum derivatives are uniformly bounded on the real momentum cube. No cancellation of a seagull term has been assumed.

For completeness we give the bounds that propagate this information through the regulator and the inverses. At a fixed internal momentum \(k\) put \(T = |k| + \mu\), and for a matrix path \(M (t)\) write

\(\displaystyle \|M\|_{T, 2} = \max_{0 \leqslant j \leqslant 2} T^j \|M^{(j)} (0)\| .\)

The paths have displaced momenta \(k + te_1\), with velocities of bounded size. The product rule bounds products by a fixed multiple of the product of these norms. The two inverse derivative identities give \(\|M^{- 1} \|_{T, 2} \leqslant C / T^2\) whenever \(\|M\|_{T, 2} \leqslant CT^2\) and \(\|M (0)^{- 1} \| \leqslant C / T^2\).

The real denominator floor holds globally on the centered cube. Indeed \(\omega (k) \geqslant c |k|^2\), the exterior identity controls the sum of transverse and longitudinal parts, and

\(\displaystyle x + \mu^2 \left( \frac{\mu^2}{x + \mu^2} \right)^9 \geqslant 2^{- 10} (x + \mu^2) \quad (x \geqslant 0) .\)

Thus both channel denominators are bounded below by \(cT^2 I\). The massive ghost operator \(\Delta_U + \mu^2 I\) has denominator, first-insertion and second-insertion bounds of respective sizes \(T^2, T, 1\) in the scaled derivative norms. Inverting gives sizes \(T^{- 2}, T^{- 3}, T^{- 4}\): a first insertion is \(- RbR\), and a second is the sum of \(- RcR\) and the two ordered \(RbRbR\) terms. Multiplication by \(\mu^2\), taking nine products, and multiplying once more by \(\mu^2\) gives regulator sizes

\(\displaystyle \mu^2, \qquad \mu^2 / T, \qquad \mu^2 / T^2 .\) (7.9)

This uses \(\mu^2 / T^2 \leqslant 1\). Product differentiation through order two preserves the same sizes in \(\| \cdot \|_{T, 2}\); the number nine is fixed. The four constant direction selectors in the vector regulator do not change these powers.

The first term of (7.8), differentiated twice in \(t\), is bounded by \(CT^{- 2} + C \mu^2 T^{- 6}\). Its unshifted inverse is independent of \(t\); the local second insertion has a unit-scale bound, while its regulator part uses (7.9). The second term has two inverses of size \(T^{- 2}\) and two insertions of size \(T\), with two derivatives costing \(T^{- 2}\). Its bound is \(CT^{- 4}\). Since \(T\) stays bounded above on the cube, these estimates give the first asserted radial bound.

For the comparison use \(T = |k|\) and restrict to \(\mu \leqslant T \leqslant 1\). Let \(\delta = T + \mu^2 / T^2\). The actual denominator minus the free quadratic denominator has scaled norm at most \(C \delta T^2\). The resolvent identity therefore bounds the inverse difference by \(C \delta T^{- 2}\). Likewise the first insertion minus its displayed linear part is bounded by \(C \delta T\): the local remainder is \(O (T^2)\) and the regulator contribution is \(O (\mu^2 / T)\). Subtract the free bubble by telescoping its four factors. Each term contains one difference, so its second external derivative is bounded by \(C \delta T^{- 4} = C (T^{- 3} + \mu^2 T^{- 6})\). The seagull estimate above is bounded by the same expression on this annulus. The free vector-minus-ghost bubble is exactly (7.7), proving the second radial inequality.

At fixed \(\mu > 0\) the denominators are invertible on a smaller complex strip by their positive real floor and analyticity. All three response layers come from the same infinite-lattice path series. The integrand thus has a common holomorphic extension for that fixed \(\mu\). For a periodic analytic function, the difference between its mesh mean and its integral is the sum of nonzero Fourier coefficients whose indices are multiples of the mesh side. Exponential coefficient decay bounds this error by \(C_{\mu} e^{- c_{\mu} n}\). The constants in this last recording estimate may depend on \(\mu\); the radial constants proved above do not. This proves the final assertion and justifies the coefficient integral.\(\Box\)

7.6Uniform comparison of determinants

Two estimates must be distinguished. An absolute determinant Taylor bound may grow like the number \(L^4\) of fine sites per block. The difference between the blocked and auxiliary determinant must have a bound independent of \(L\) in order to identify the logarithmic coefficient. The relevant mechanism is finite rank together with a trace-class regulator.

Here is the structure behind the trace-norm requirement. Writing \(H\) for the block average derivative, \(B\) for its penalty Hessian, \(Z\) for the coarse-frame projection, and \(K = m_{\mu} X\), cancellation of the fine kinetic Hessians leaves

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \Theta = & H^{\ast} BH - Z^{\ast} Z + P_f^{\ast} d_U K + K^{\ast} d_U^{\ast} P_f + K^{\ast} K \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.10)} \\ & \hspace{12mm} - P_f^{\ast} M_{\mu} P_f . \end{array}\)

The first two terms factor through spaces of dimensions at most \(4 \dim \mathfrak{g}\) and \(\dim \mathfrak{g}\) per coarse momentum. Thus \(\|VW\|_1 \leqslant r \|V\| \|W\|\) with \(r\) independent of \(L\). Derivatives preserve the intermediate spaces.

For the regulator, at zero background the alias eigenvalues of \(P_{\mu}\) are bounded by \(C (1 + |z|)^{- 2}\) in a smaller complex strip. Consequently

\(\displaystyle \|P_{\mu}^3 \|_1 \leqslant C \sum_{z \in \mathbb{Z}^4} (1 + |z|)^{- 6} < \infty .\)

Given bounded first two insertions of \(P_{\mu}\), distribute at most two derivatives among the three factors in \((P_{\mu}^3)^3\). Every term retains one undifferentiated trace-class cube; the other factors have operator bounds. Hence the first two insertions of \(m_{\mu}\) have trace norm \(O (\mu^2)\). This explains the ninth power and avoids an erroneous factor \(L^4\). To apply this trace estimate, we establish the actual weight conversions, stationary insertions and scaled inverse bounds below.

7.6.1The stationary branch and the block average contact term

We verify the contact assertion for the block average actually used in the block integral. Let \(\zeta (x)\) be its stationary representative in centered coordinates, with \(\zeta (0) = 0\) and tangent \((Wx, r_x)\), where \(r_x\) is its coarse scalar component after recentering. For each scalar determinant function \(f (x, z)\), constant colour rotations act orthogonally on its matrices and leave \(f\) invariant. Every invariant linear functional on the simple Lie algebra vanishes, since it annihilates brackets and \([\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] =\mathfrak{g}\). Applying this to each coordinate gives \(Df (0) = 0\). Thus

\(\displaystyle D^2 [f (x, \zeta (x))] (0) [u, v] = D^2 f (0) [(u, Wu, r_u), (v, Wv, r_v)] .\) (7.11)

Second derivatives of the stationary branch have disappeared for this specific reason. Matrix Hessians themselves need not have vanishing first derivative; the statement is about their invariant scalar determinants.

Use rooted coordinates for the following exact calculation. Let \(w_x (v)\) be the logarithmic change in the block average under an internal left increment \(v\), with \(w_x (0) = 0\). Let \(r (x)\) be the derivative of the block penalty with respect to that block average increment, and set

\(\displaystyle S_x (a)_{ij} = \langle a, D_v^2 w_x (0) [e_i, e_j] \rangle, \qquad Z (x) = S_x (r (x)), \qquad A (x) = M_{\text{core}} (x) .\)

The Hessian chain rule gives \(M_{\text{act}} = A + Z\), with \(r (0) = Z (0) = 0\). The rooted-to-centered internal change is triangular with determinant one, so it does not change the determinant. Define \(Y (x)\) by \(\langle a, Y (x) \rangle = \text{tr} (A (x)^{- 1} S_x (a))\). The block average is covariant, and the internal matrices transform by the same congruence. Cyclicity of trace therefore makes \(Y\) a covariant coarse vector. Constant colour rotations imply \(Y (0) = 0\).

The linear block identity \(QW = I - b^{- 1} K_c\) fixes the derivative of the penalty gradient: \(Dr (0) = - K_c = : J\). Expanding \(\log \det (A + Z) - \log \det A\) through degree two now gives

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} D^2 (\log \det M_{\text{act}} - \log \det M_{\text{core}}) (0) [u, v] = & \langle Ju, DY (0) v \rangle + \langle Jv, DY (0) u \rangle \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.12)} \\ & - \text{tr} (A (0)^{- 1} S_0 (Ju) A (0)^{- 1} S_0 (Jv)) . \end{array}\)

In particular the derivative of \(A^{- 1}\) is included in \(DY\); \(Y (0) = 0\) removes the term with \(D^2 r\).

There is an exact identity \(DY (0) d_{0, c} = 0\). Gauge the trivial configuration by a coarse gauge transformation, then restore the chosen tree gauge. Gauge invariance preserves stationarity; uniqueness of the local stationary solution identifies the restored configuration with the selected branch on this curve. Covariance and \(Y (0) = 0\) show that \(Y\) vanishes there. Differentiation proves the identity. This uses the actual block average, rather than an independently chosen linear average.

At fixed \(L\), the finite-stencil block average and the analytic inverse of \(A (0)\) give common holomorphic kernels for every term in (7.12). Normalized finite-torus traces are momentum mesh averages. Their limit is the integral of these same kernels; exponential Fourier decay gives uniform convergence also along varying external mesh momenta. Write \(M (p)\) for the mixed kernel of \(DY\). Its Ward identity along \(p = te_{\nu}\) reads \(q_{\nu} (te_{\nu}) M_{j \nu} (te_{\nu}) = 0\) in each colour. Hence every column of \(M (0)\) is zero and \(M (p) = O (|p|)\). Since \(J (p) = - K_c (p) = O (|p|^2)\), the first two terms of (7.12) have order at least three, and the last has order at least four. Therefore the contact correction has zero \(\alpha\). Only qualitative analyticity at fixed \(L\) was used here. Its constants need not be uniform in \(L\), because this is an exact vanishing statement.

7.6.2Bounds for the actual centered matrices

The actual stationary tangent is \(W_a\) from (5.30); its forest representative is \(W_F\) from (5.33). As above, \(\beta\) is the actual inverse coupling, and \(b\) is the soft constraint coefficient in the blocking kernel.

We establish the operator estimates for the selected path and block average. The inputs are their positive normalized metric, fixed exponential path bounds, and the stationary identification and smooth representative of Section 5.6; all refer to the same adjacent pair. The coarse scalar part of the smooth representative may be nonzero after recentering. Its derivatives below are merely required to be bounded, not to vanish.

Recall that \(\mu = L^{- 1}\) is the inverse block ratio. Use the unitary block Fourier transform \(P (p)\) on fine residues, and set

\(\displaystyle h_l (p) = (|q (\text{Re} (p + 2 \pi l) / L) |^2 + \mu^2)^{1 / 2}, \qquad \|u\|_p^2 = \sum_l h_l (p)^2 | (P (p)^{- 1} u)_l |^2 .\)

The joint space also has a coarse scalar component with its ordinary Euclidean norm. Opposite momenta are paired in the dual norm. On a fixed small coarse strip, \(P\) and \(P^{- 1}\) have bounded operator norms, \(h_l \geqslant \mu\), and \(|q (k_l) | \leqslant 5 h_l\). Thus a unit internal direction has normalized residue \(L^2\) norm \(O (\mu)\) and its first fine difference has normalized \(L^2\) norm \(O (\mu^2)\). External smooth bridge legs have the same powers in supremum norm, by (5.32). Extending to the fixed padded block average patch only changes constants: its number of residue blocks is fixed at the given smoothing order, and its Bloch phases are uniformly bounded.

Fine kinetic insertions.
The zero-background kinetic form is a curl on each internal leg. Its first background insertion is a sum of products with one finite difference among three legs; its second insertion needs none among four legs. These representations have exponentially summable coefficients with uniform bounds. For the first insertion, subtract its constant momentum coefficient, which is zero by the weight-four Taylor calculation in Proposition 7.3, and use coordinate division. The zero-background representation is exactly \(d_1^{\ast} Hd_1\); the second insertion already has the required form. The factor count is

\(\displaystyle (n + 2) + \text{number of differences} = 4 \qquad (0 \leqslant n \leqslant 2) .\)

Hölder with two internal \(L^2\) factors and the remaining supremum factors costs \(O (\mu^4)\) in normalized counting measure. Summing the fine anchor costs \(L^4\), canceling it. A translation by \(z\) introduces a Bloch factor of modulus at most \(Ce^{\sigma |z| / L}\). The exponential decay of the coefficients makes the resulting sum over \(z\) uniformly bounded. Thus the fine kinetic Hessian and its first two background insertions have bounded operators between the stated weighted spaces, uniformly in \(L\). This is an estimate for the actual left-coordinate response, whose split-field definition was used above.

The centered frame and penalty.
A small supremum bound alone is not enough for two internal directions. Use normalized probability measure on the padded block and the three norms \(L^{\infty}, L^2, L^1\). External directions use the first, one internal direction the second, and two internal directions the third. Products are controlled by \(L^2 L^2 \subset L^1\), and averages contract these norms. A path of \(O (L)\) links, each of whose positive derivatives is \(O (\mu)\) in its assigned norm, has bounded derivatives of every fixed order. Indeed adjoining one more link changes the next derivative by \(O (\mu)\) times a polynomial in already bounded lower derivatives; summing \(O (L)\) such changes costs \(O (1)\). For coordinate connector paths, a fixed-position read is a coordinatewise splice of its endpoints. On the endpoint pair space this is a measure-preserving coordinate permutation, followed by a bounded-measure restriction and translation. The argument therefore applies to the actual averaged connectors.

Here is why centering gives uniform frame derivatives. Let \(F_x\) be the minimizing frame after recentering, \(E_{xy}\) its averaged transport, \(\nu\) normalized counting measure on its block, and \(\omega\) the smoothing probability. If \(\mathcal{P}\) is the nearest-group map and \(\Pi = D\mathcal{P} (1)\), define

\(\displaystyle J_0 = \sum_y \nu (y) F_y, \qquad B_x = \sum_y \nu (y) F_y (E_{xy} - 1),\)
\(\displaystyle R (T) =\mathcal{P} (T) - \Pi T, \qquad S_x = \Pi B_x + R (J_0 + B_x) .\)

The minimizing equation \(F_x =\mathcal{P} (J_0 + B_x)\) and centering \(\sum_x \omega (x) \Pi F_x = \Pi 1\) imply exactly

\(\displaystyle F_x = \Pi 1 - \sum_y \omega (y) \Pi S_y + S_x .\)

Now \(E_{xy} (0) - 1 = 0\) and \(DR (1) = 0\). At derivative order \(n + 1\) the right side depends on frame derivatives only through order \(n\). Induction using the preceding product bounds gives uniform derivatives through order four, also with two internal directions. The smoothing probability satisfies \(\omega \leqslant C_j \nu\): its reduction modulo \(L\) is uniform, so every atom has mass at most \(L^{- 4}\), and its support has \(O_j (L^4)\) sites. This controls the change of averaging measure. The argument estimates the selected minimizer; its existence and local uniqueness come from Chapter 4.

Connector averaging and the fixed smooth penalty are compositions of these operations. Their mixed derivatives with up to two external and two internal directions are uniformly bounded. The centered frame logarithmic derivative \(X\) has normalized \(L^2\) output bounded by a constant, so its unnormalized output costs at most \(CL^2\). Rooted and centered descriptions differ by a triangular coordinate change of determinant one; the same averaged-path bound controls its external coarse coordinates. Consequently \(F = d_U^{\ast} P_f + m_{\mu} X\) has a uniform weighted operator bound, since \(m_{\mu} = O (\mu^2)\) cancels \(L^2\). Frame covariance gives \(X\mathcal{R}= I\) in the same coordinates, and hence \(F\mathcal{R}= \Delta_U + m_{\mu}\). Gauge signs are fixed here by taking the fine component of \(\mathcal{R}\) to be \(d_U\).

Uniform inverse bounds.
At zero background the auxiliary fine form has the weighted floor \(2^{- 10} \min (1, \varepsilon)\), where \(H_f \geqslant \varepsilon I\). This follows from the exterior identity and the scalar regulator floor proved in Proposition 7.3. The block-completed form needs a quotient estimate. In centered coordinates it is

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{G} (u, r) = \langle u, K_f u \rangle + b |Mu - d_{0, c} r|^2 .\)

Here \(M\) is the straight averaged connector. Indeed the rooted linear block average is \(Q = M + d_{0, c} R\) by (5.24); setting \(r = r_{\text{root}} - Ru\) converts \(Qu - d_{0, c} r_{\text{root}}\) to the displayed expression. Under a fine gauge variation, the new scalar coordinate changes by the smoothed scalar mean, since \(Rd_{0, f}\) is root evaluation minus that mean. This is the same centering used in the frame calculation, and the triangular coordinate change has determinant one. Subtract a gauge vector to make every nonprincipal alias of \(u\) transverse and eliminate \(r\) using the principal scalar alias. The latter scalar mean is \(\mu^2\) times a product of centered box sums, bounded away from zero after division by \(\mu^2\) on the principal real cube. Write the resulting fine aliases as \(u_l\) and set \(A_h = \sum_{l \ne 0} h_l^2 |u_l |^2\). The fine metric floor gives control of \(A_h\), since \(|q (k_l) | \geqslant c \mu\) for \(l \ne 0\). The principal coefficient of \(M\) has least singular value \(c_j \mu\): its connector sum is of order \(L\) and the unitary block Fourier coefficient is \(L^{- 2}\). For its upper bound, each fixed-position averaged read has coefficients \(O_j (L^{- 4})\) on \(O_j (L^4)\) sites, hence Euclidean row norm \(O_j (L^{- 2})\). Summing its \(L\) connector positions and converting a weighted unit vector with \(h_l \geqslant L^{- 1}\) gives a fixed bound. Thus the full \(M\) has bounded weighted norm. Consequently

\(\displaystyle \mu^2 |u_0 |^2 \leqslant C_j (|Mu|^2 + A_h), \qquad \inf_a \|(u, r) -\mathcal{R}a\|_p^2 \leqslant C_j \mathcal{G} (u, r) .\)

These estimates include zero coarse momentum; the scalar principal alias removes the coarse scalar coordinate there as well.

Moreover \(\|\mathcal{R}a\|_p \leqslant C | \Gamma_{\mu} a|\), where \(\Gamma_{\mu} = \Delta + m_{\mu}\). For the fine component this is \(h_l |q_l | \leqslant h_l^2 \leqslant C \Gamma_{\mu} (k_l)\); the coarse averaging component has norm \(O (\mu^2)\) and obeys the same bound. Since \(F\mathcal{R}= \Gamma_{\mu}\) and \(F\) is bounded, the quotient estimate implies a positive weighted floor for \(\mathcal{G}+ F^{\ast} F\). All constants are fixed before \(L\).

The weights are not holomorphic. To continue this floor, at each real anchor freeze \(h_l\) while retaining the holomorphic block Fourier matrix. On a fixed-radius coarse ball these frozen weights are uniformly comparable to the moving weights, because \(h_l \geqslant \mu\) and \(|q (k_l (p)) - q (k_l (x)) | \leqslant C \mu |p - x|\). Cauchy's inequality in the frozen coordinates bounds the change in operator norm, and a fixed smaller strip preserves half the real floor. This proves uniform holomorphic inverse bounds for both matrices. Differentiating \(M^{- 1} M = I\) then bounds the first two inverse insertions.

The kinetic response cancels identically, including its two derivatives, in (7.10). The finite-rank terms retain their fixed-dimensional coarse intermediate spaces. For the regulator, the trace-class cube argument preceding this subsection applies. In the terms containing \(m_{\mu} X\), its factor \(\mu^2\) compensates for the \(L^2\) frame derivative. In the separate term \(P_f^{\ast} M_{\mu} P_f\), it compensates for two weighted-to-Euclidean conversions, each of size \(O (\mu^{- 1})\). Proposition 7.5. (Block comparison) For the same normalized path and block average, the curvature-square coefficients of the blocked response and the auxiliary fine determinant satisfy \(| \alpha_{N, m} - \alpha (\Pi_{\mu}) | \leqslant C\), uniformly in \(L, N, p\).

\(\displaystyle D_v D_w \log \det A = \text{tr} (A^{- 1} A_{vw} - A^{- 1} A_v A^{- 1} A_w) .\)

Subtract this formula for \(C_0\) and \(\tilde{A}_{\mu}\), and use \(C_0^{- 1} - \tilde{A}_{\mu}^{- 1} = - C_0^{- 1} \Theta \tilde{A}_{\mu}^{- 1}\). Telescoping produces terms containing a trace-class difference or one of its derivatives, with all other factors bounded. The inherited loop has order four and contributes no \(\alpha\). 7.6.3The one-loop drift

Proposition 7.6. (One-loop drift) For an adjacent pair with the normalized path, metric floor and actual stationary identification used in Section 7.6.2,

\(\displaystyle | \alpha_{N, m} + \kappa_G \log L| \leqslant C\)

with a constant depending only on the fixed stage inputs.

Proof. On the unit three-sphere the angular means are \(\mathbb{E} \theta_2^2 = 1 / 4\) and \(\mathbb{E} \theta_1^2 \theta_2^2 = 1 / 24\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle (2 \pi)^{- 4} \int_{\mu < |k| < 1} h (k) \hspace{0.17em} dk = \frac{8 \pi^2 C_G}{(2 \pi)^4} \left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{6} - 1 \right) \log \mu^{- 1} = - \kappa_G \log \mu^{- 1} .\)

The two error integrals on this annulus are uniformly bounded: \(\int_{\mu}^1 r^3 r^{- 3} dr \leqslant 1\) and \(\mu^2 \int_{\mu}^1 r^3 r^{- 6} dr \leqslant 1 / 2\). The ball \(|k| \leqslant \mu\) has volume \(O (\mu^4)\) and integrand \(O (\mu^{- 4})\); the region outside the unit ball is also uniformly integrable. Thus \(\alpha (\Pi_{\mu}) = - \kappa_G \log \mu^{- 1} + O (1)\). Proposition 7.5 and \(\mu = L^{- 1}\) finish the proof.\(\Box\)

The sign is for downward blocking: the coefficient \(\beta\) of the kinetic action decreases by \(\kappa_G \log L\) at one loop. This proposition does not assert the exact nonlinear recursion for \(\beta\). Connected interactions contribute to that recursion and are handled by the one-step integral identity.

7.7.1Uniform classical stationary coefficients

To construct the cubic and quartic matching terms, we first need classical coefficient bounds fixed before \(L\). \(\displaystyle \mathscr{C}^{- 1} = (\mathscr{H} + F^{\ast} F)^{- 1},\)

with the uniform weighted bound just proved. For a coarse direction \(u\) let \(Tu = (u, W_a u, r_u)\) be the full stationary tangent in the smooth centered representative. In particular

\(\displaystyle D^2 \mathscr{S} (0) [Tu, (0, z)] = 0 \quad \text{for every internal } z.\) (7.13)

This is the differentiated stationarity equation, with the actual block average identification of Chapter 5. Thus coarse-frame directions are included in (7.13). Define the internal covector

\(\displaystyle B (u, v) [z] = D^3 \mathscr{S} (0) [Tu, Tv, (0, z)] .\)

Both the external cubic and quartic vertices and \(B\) have uniformly bounded common Fourier kernels. Here is the power count in their actual weighted norms. A fine-action cubic vertex has at least one finite difference among its three legs; a quartic vertex needs none. Their exponentially summable coefficient bounds follow from the same normalized-path expansion and coordinate division used for the kinetic insertions above. An undifferentiated external leg costs \(O (\mu)\) in supremum norm, and its differentiated leg costs \(O (\mu^2)\). An internal unit weighted leg costs these same powers in normalized \(L^2\) norm. Thus three external legs with one difference, four external legs, and two external legs with one internal leg and one difference each cost \(O (\mu^4)\) in normalized residue summation. In the last case \(L^2 \subset L^1\) on probability measure is sufficient. The sum over \(L^4\) fine anchors cancels this factor. Finitely many coordinate-change terms have the same or higher weight. Penalty vertices are bounded by the centered-frame mixed-derivative induction above, which allows up to four derivatives and includes the bounded coarse scalar legs \(r_u\). The bounds for \(B\) are dual weighted norms, not Euclidean coordinate bounds with a dimension-dependent conversion.

The covector \(B\) annihilates gauge directions. To check this, write the internal gauge identity as \(D_z \mathscr{S} (v, z) [\mathcal{R}(v, z) a] = 0\) and differentiate twice along \(Tu, Tv\). The term containing \(D \mathscr{S} (0)\) is zero. The two terms with a derivative of \(\mathcal{R}\) vanish by (7.13), since those derivatives are internal vectors. The remaining term is

\(\displaystyle B (u, v) [\mathcal{R}(0) a] = 0.\)

Therefore \(w = \mathscr{C}^{- 1} B (u, v)\) is a representative of the quotient solution \(\mathscr{H} w = B (u, v)\). Indeed, pairing \(\mathscr{C} w = B (u, v)\) with \(\mathcal{R}a\) gives \(\langle F\mathcal{R}a, Fw \rangle = 0\). The regulated gauge matrix \(F\mathcal{R}\) is invertible, so \(Fw = 0\) and \(\mathscr{H} w = B (u, v)\). Any other solution differs by a gauge vector, against which \(B\) vanishes. This justifies using the bounded completed inverse in the exchange term; it does not replace a degenerate Hessian by an arbitrary inverse.

If \(E (v)\) is the stationary value, implicit differentiation now gives

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} D^3 E (0) [u_1, u_2, u_3] & = D^3 \mathscr{S} (0) [Tu_1, Tu_2, Tu_3], \hspace{3cm} \text{(7.14)} \\ D^4 E (0) [u_1, u_2, u_3, u_4] & = D^4 \mathscr{S} (0) [Tu_1, Tu_2, Tu_3, Tu_4]\\ & \quad - \sum_{\{ij\} \{kl\}} B (u_i, u_j) \mathscr{C}^{- 1} B (u_k, u_l), \end{array}\)

where the sum is over the three partitions into two unordered pairs. These formulas follow by differentiating stationarity; the higher branch derivatives multiply its first derivative or (7.13). On the diagonal the last sum is \(3 B (u, u) \mathscr{C}^{- 1} B (u, u)\), agreeing with the earlier formula. The gauge-annihilation argument makes the formulas independent of the representative chosen to differentiate the stationary branch.

For Fourier waves, each exchange inverse has intermediate coarse momentum equal to a sum of two external momenta. Restrict each external strip by a fixed factor so that all these sums remain in the inverse strip. The vertex and inverse bounds then give a common strip and a uniform bound for both symbols in (7.14), before choosing \(L\). Their finite-volume identities are exact: every vertex and inverse is the restriction of the same common Fourier matrix, and momentum conservation leaves the coarse volume factor exactly once. Dividing by \(3\)! and \(4\)! gives normalized common Taylor symbols with the required bounds. No stationary neighborhood of size uniform in \(L\) has been inferred from this argument; only its fixed-order coefficients need that uniformity here.

7.7.2Construction of the matching curvature terms

Let \(E (v)\) be the classical stationary action of Chapter 5, with the factor \(\beta\) removed. Its Hessian is prescribed by the exact free block relation. Write the corresponding plaquette metric as \(H = c_{\text{dir}} I + \rho^{\ast} \rho\), with \(H (0) = I\) and \(c_{\text{dir}} > 0\). The direct Wilson term together with the squares of the transported rows of \(\rho\) gives an analytic gauge-invariant action \(J (v)\) with this Hessian. Indeed the transported rows linearize to \(\rho d_1 v\), so

\(\displaystyle D^2 J (0) [v, v] = \langle d_1 v, Hd_1 v \rangle = D^2 E (0) [v, v] .\)

The construction uses the coarse metric matched to the incoming Gaussian form, not an arbitrary positive coarse metric.

The preceding stationary coefficient estimates and the fixed row bounds supply the hypotheses of the following construction.

Proposition 7.7. (Classical matching) Suppose \(E\) and \(J\) are invariant analytic germs on every torus, have zero value and first derivative and identical Hessians. Suppose their normalized coefficients of orders three and four have common strip bounds, and the row coefficients of \(J\) have an exponential bound. There are invariant cubic and quartic curvature terms \(S_3, S_4\), chosen once for all periods, such that

\(\displaystyle E - J - S_3 - S_4 = O (v^5) .\)

Their weighted sums are bounded by a fixed multiple of the input Taylor bounds on a smaller positive width.

Proof. The leading coefficient of \(E - J\) is cubic and satisfies the homogeneous Ward identities. Lemma 7.1 and the transported construction produce \(S_3\) with that coefficient. The fourth coefficient of \(E - J - S_3\) is again transverse, since its lower coefficients vanish. Apply the lemma again to obtain \(S_4\). It is essential to subtract the fourth coefficient of \(S_3\): a nonlinear transported cubic curvature term has nonzero higher Taylor coefficients. The exponential path bound controls each fixed Taylor derivative on a smaller complex link ball; factors of path length are absorbed by a width loss. Thus the fourth coefficient of \(S_3\) has the required common bound. This proves both the matching and the bounds. Polarization converts the equality on rays to equality of all derivatives through order four.\(\Box\)

7.7.3Preserving the classical coefficient bound

These estimates can also be arranged to preserve a fixed classical coefficient bound, rather than producing an unspecified function of the incoming bound. Keep the Gaussian metric bounds fixed, and denote the bound on the remaining incoming path coefficients by \(A\). Compare the fine action with the Wilson action, evaluated on the same tangents \(T\) and with the same completed inverse \(\mathscr{C}^{- 1}\). The weight-four normalization proved above implies that their cubic vertex difference has at least two finite differences, and their quartic vertex difference has at least one. Coordinate division of their common symbols gives these factors with exponentially summable coefficients bounded by a polynomial \(P (A)\). A slightly stronger incoming exponential width absorbs the polynomial path-length factors.

When all legs are external, the extra difference gives \(L^4 \mu^5 = \mu\). For the difference of the covectors \(B\), move differences on the single internal leg to the two external legs by momentum conservation. In a coordinate this uses

\(\displaystyle q (- p_1 - p_2) = - e^{- i (p_1 + p_2)} \{q (p_1) + e^{ip_1} q (p_2)\} .\)

It is a discrete integration by parts, with bounded translation factors; it leaves at most two differences on any external leg. The actual smooth tangent bounds through order two therefore give the same gain. The internal leg is estimated only in normalized \(L^2\), without asking for a second weighted derivative. Thus the vertex and dual-vertex remainders are bounded by \(P (A) / L\).

The Wilson vertices, block penalty, and their exchange pairing have bounds depending only on the fixed Gaussian metric and block average data. The inverse in the exchange term depends only on the quadratic action, so higher incoming coefficients do not change it. Expanding the two factors \(B = B_{\text{W}} + \delta B\) in (7.14) shows that the normalized classical symbols, and then their curvature families, satisfy a bound of the form

\(\displaystyle A_{\text{out}} \leqslant a_{\text{stage}} + \frac{P_{\text{stage}} (A)}{L} .\) (7.15)

The squared remainder costs \(P (A)^2 / L^2\) and is absorbed into the displayed polynomial. Subtracting the prescribed coarse quadratic row and the quartic contribution of the constructed cubic family preserves this form: those operations have fixed coarse-scale bounds, and the latter operation is linear in that cubic family. The output strip is chosen from the Gaussian coarse strip. It need not shrink with each step: an incoming coefficient displaced by \(z\) costs at most \(e^{c \sigma |z| / L}\) on that output strip, which is absorbed by the fixed incoming width after choosing \(L\) large enough. Finally choose an outgoing classical bound larger than \(a_{\text{stage}}\) and then \(L\) so that the second term in (7.15) is smaller than the difference between these two bounds. For a finite collection of stage data these choices are simultaneous.

Consequently the cubic and quartic curvature families are constructed for this same stationary action with bounds fixed at the stage before \(L\). Their input Hessian is the matched coarse metric. This completes the classical matching construction without invoking a later scale schedule or a nonlinear local-integral estimate.

7.8Inherited loops: four differences and alias suppression

Taylor degree here counts field factors. It is distinct from scaling dimension, which also counts derivatives, as in Section 2.1; a momentum difference therefore improves scaling dimension without adding a field factor.

Let \(\Lambda_{m + 1}^{\text{loop}}\) denote the incoming weighted loop sum. We prove the estimate

\(\displaystyle \|\mathcal{L}_{m + 1} \text{ pulled back} \|_{w'} \leqslant CL^{- 2} \Lambda_{m + 1}^{\text{loop}} .\) (7.16)

from the transports of Chapter 5. Group incoming quadratic curvature terms by their relative displacement \(z\). Their coefficient matrix \(r (z)\) has exponential sum bounded by \(\Lambda_{m + 1}^{\text{loop}}\) and zero zeroth and first moments. Thus the fine link symbol

\(\displaystyle S (p) = d_1 (- p)^{\textsf{T}} \hat{r} (p) d_1 (p)\)

has order four, satisfies \(Sd_0 = d_0^{\ast} S = 0\), and has strip bound \(C \Lambda_{m + 1}^{\text{loop}}\). Symmetrization and colour indices are understood; they cost fixed constants.

An analytic periodic function with an order-four zero is a sum of products of four coordinate differences \(q_a (p)\) times analytic periodic functions on a smaller strip, with linearly bounded coefficient norms. To see this quantitatively, write \(f (p) - f (0) = \sum_a q_a (p) D_a f (p_1, \ldots, p_a, 0, \ldots, 0)\) using the divisions of Lemma 7.1, and repeat on the quotients, which retain one fewer vanishing order. There are finitely many terms. For a quadratic symbol, move two differences to the opposite leg by \(q_a (p) = - e^{ip_a} q_a (- p)\). The phases merely translate a coefficient by finitely many lattice sites. Each term now has two differences on each of its two field arguments.

The straight stationary transport \(W_s\) has common exponentially local kernels with \(\nabla_f^r W_s = O (L^{- 1 - r})\) for \(r \leqslant 2\), by Section 5.6. By (5.30), the tangent of the actual block average differs from this representative by a fine gradient; the gauge-invariant quadratic symbol \(S\) annihilates that difference on both sides. Consequently it is legitimate to use \(W_s\) in this calculation, even if the tree-gauge representative itself has poor difference bounds. Each differentiated leg costs \(CL^{- 3}\). Summing the fine anchor in one coarse block costs \(L^4\), so the pulled-back quadratic symbol has bound

\(\displaystyle CL^4 L^{- 3} L^{- 3} \Lambda_{m + 1}^{\text{loop}} = CL^{- 2} \Lambda_{m + 1}^{\text{loop}} .\)

Displacing an argument by a fine displacement \(z\) costs at most \(Ce^{\sigma |z| / L}\) on a coarse strip of width \(\sigma\). Choose \(L\) after the fine coefficient width \(w\) so that \(\sigma / L < w\); absolute summability controls these displacements. Holomorphy and the common finite-torus representation follow from normal convergence. A final fixed strip reduction converts this to the weighted coefficient bound required in (7.16).

The order-four zero on the coarse lattice requires another argument. For aliases \(k_l (p) = (p + 2 \pi l) / L\), let \(N_l (p)\) be the fine-wave amplitude of the transport. The coarse symbol is

\(\displaystyle H (p) = L^4 \sum_l N_{- l} (- p)^{\textsf{T}} S (k_l (p)) N_l (p) .\)

For \(l \ne 0\), use \(K_f (k_l) N_l = A_l K_c (p)\), the exact stationary transport equation. Project \(N_l\) orthogonally off the gradient \(q (k_l)\). The positive fine curvature metric implies that \(K_f (k_l) + d_0 (k_l) d_0 (k_l)^{\ast}\) is invertible near \(p = 0\) for this fixed \(l, L\). As \(K_c (p) = O (|p|^2)\), the transverse amplitude is \(O (|p|^2)\). The Ward identities of \(S\) remove the longitudinal amplitudes, so each nonprincipal alias has order four. The principal alias has order four because \(S (p / L)\) does. This argument requires no lower inverse bound uniform in \(L\) for an individual nonprincipal alias.

Finally the nonlinear stationary pullback has the same Hessian as this linear pullback: the inherited action has zero first derivative, so its chain rule has no term involving the second derivative of the branch. This proves the weighted gain and retained moment cancellations for the actual inherited action, once the actual and straight stationary tangents have been identified up to gauge as above.

7.9Output coefficients and the remaining analytic width

Suppose the stationary determinant symbol has bound \(\Phi\) at a common width, the inherited loop symbol has bound \(R\) and order four, and the kinetic symbol has bound \(B_H\). Subtract the extracted coefficient \(\alpha b_H\). Lemma 7.2 and the two-leg division produce a quadratic curvature family with bound

\(\displaystyle K (\Phi + | \alpha |B_H + R)\)

and the zero moments (7.4). Together with the prescribed rows and the cubic/quartic families of Proposition 7.7, this defines one common output path action. If the determinant common symbol approximates its finite period values with error \(| \Lambda | \epsilon\), the same coefficient error is retained by linear subtraction. For real plane waves with component amplitudes at most one, the Hessian error is at most \(2 (4 \dim \mathfrak{g})^2 | \Lambda | \epsilon\): expansion in component waves costs the square of their number, and \(D^2 = 2 C_2\).

All sums should be bounded first at a width \(w' > w > 0\). Each fixed differentiation of a transported term costs a polynomial in its path length; the factor \(e^{- (w' - w) \text{length}}\) absorbs it. This gives the fixed derivative orders needed for local expansion. Parameter derivatives require the corresponding derivative bounds on the common input kernels. Linear curvature division preserves such bounds because its operations and widths are chosen independently of the particular kernel. No parameter regularity follows merely from matching the kernels pointwise.

For a quantitative descent, use the inherited estimate (7.16). If the new loop bound is \(AL^4 + B \log L + C_0\), choose \(L\) so that \(CL^{- 2} \leqslant 1 / 4\), then the coupling entry threshold so that

\(\displaystyle AL^4 + B \log L + C_0 \leqslant \tfrac{1}{2} \Lambda_m^{\text{loop}}, \qquad \Lambda_{m + 1}^{\text{loop}} \leqslant 2 \Lambda_m^{\text{loop}} .\)

Their sum fits the outgoing bound. This is the order of choices: widths and fixed amplitudes, block ratio, then coupling threshold.

The comparison bounds and lattice integrand estimates have now been derived from the same normalized analytic path, positive metric and actual stationary block average. The inherited estimate follows from its identified tangent and the common transport bounds. These results are one-step statements: their application to an entire selected history requires that its incoming paths obey the fixed stage bounds and its stationary charts satisfy the identifications of Chapter 5. They do not replace the nonlinear local-integral bounds or prove the existence of an infinite scale schedule.
